    Part I · Chapter 2

    MuJoCo Essentials for MJWarp Users

    MJWarp speaks MuJoCo. Every scene you will simulate on the GPU starts as an MJCF file that MuJoCo compiles, and every array you read back carries the name MuJoCo gave it. This chapter teaches that vocabulary: models and data, bodies and joints, quaternions, soft contacts, equality constraints, mocap, actuators, sensors, options, the step loop, building models in Python, and getting a picture out.

    Beginner About 90 min· Prerequisites: Ch 1

    You will learn

    • Read and write MJCF: worldbody, bodies, joints, geoms, sites, actuators, sensors, keyframes, options, default classes, limits, equality constraints and mocap bodies.
    • Build the same model in Python with mujoco.MjSpec and compile it.
    • Map MJCF to MjModel sizes (nq, nv, nu, nbody, ngeom, nsensordata, nmocap, neq) and the address tables jnt_qposadr / jnt_dofadr.
    • Explain why a free joint has 7 qpos but 6 qvel, and read quaternions in MuJoCo's (w, x, y, z) order.
    • Picture contacts and limits as soft, slightly penetrating constraints tuned by solref and solimp.
    • Run mj_step / mj_forward, reset to keyframes, set ctrl, read sensors and poses, and render a frame.
    Open in Colab Download notebook

    In Colab: Runtime > Change runtime type > GPU, then run the first cell. Most of this chapter runs C MuJoCo on the CPU; examples 2.1, 2.2, 2.9 and 2.12 also touch MJWarp. Examples are numbered by file (exN_*.py) and appear in the section where their topic is taught.

    Before you start: recap

    • A simulator maps a state (qpos, qvel, act, time) to the next state, one step at a time (Ch 1 §1).
    • MuJoCo compiles an MJCF file into an MjModel (constants) and keeps the changing state in an MjData; C MuJoCo runs one world per MjData in float64 (Ch 1 §2).
    • MJWarp is MuJoCo rewritten as Warp kernels: mjw.put_model and mjw.make_data copy a model to the device and allocate nworld worlds in float32 (Ch 1 §4, §6).
    • Some MuJoCo features are not supported by MJWarp, for example the PGS solver; put_model raises NotImplementedError for them (Ch 1 §7).

    1The two structs: MjModel and MjData

    MuJoCo splits a simulation into two objects, and MJWarp keeps the split exactly.

    • An MjModel holds everything that does not change while the simulation runs: sizes such as nq and nbody, the kinematic tree, geom shapes and sizes, masses, actuator gains and the physics options in opt. It is produced once, by compiling an MJCF file.
    • An MjData holds everything that changes: the state (time, qpos, qvel, act), the inputs you write (ctrl, qfrc_applied, xfrc_applied, mocap_pos, mocap_quat, eq_active) and the outputs the pipeline computes (body poses xpos, contacts, constraint rows, sensordata, accelerations qacc, ...).

    The split matters for performance and for correctness. A step reads the model and the current state and overwrites every output. If you know which fields are state and which are outputs, you know what you must save to rewind a simulation (state only), and what you may read right after a step (outputs, which belong to the state at the start of that step, Section 9).

    Source

    mujoco_warp/_src/types.py · class Model mirrors the MjModel field names and adds warp-only precomputed fields; class Data declares every field with a shape spec such as qpos: array("nworld", "nq", float) and mocap_pos: array("nworld", "nmocap", wp.vec3). The spec strings are the allocation recipe used by io.make_data.

    Two consequences you will meet in every later chapter. First, anything you learn about a MuJoCo field name here transfers directly: mjd.qpos becomes d.qpos, with shape (nworld, nq) instead of (nq,). Second, MJWarp stores floats as float32 (C MuJoCo uses float64), so expect agreement to about 1e-6, not to the last digit (Example 2.9 measures it).

    2MJCF anatomy: the kinematic tree

    MJCF is MuJoCo's XML format. A file has one <mujoco> root, an optional <option> (Section 8), a <worldbody> that holds the scene, and sections for actuators, sensors, equality constraints and keyframes. The heart is the kinematic tree of bodies inside <worldbody>:

    • A body is a rigid frame with mass and inertia. Bodies nest: a child body is placed relative to its parent. The worldbody is body 0, the root of the tree, and never moves.
    • A joint inside a body says how that body may move relative to its parent. A body without a joint is welded to its parent.
    • A geom is a shape attached to a body. Geoms collide, are drawn, and (by default) give the body its mass from their volume and density. A geom placed directly in worldbody is static scenery, like the floor.
    • A site is a massless marker frame on a body; sensors and tendons attach to sites.
    • Lights and cameras also hang off bodies. Everything can have a name; names turn into integer ids at compile time, and mujoco.mj_name2id / mj_id2name translate.

    Hover a line of MJCF on the left, or a node of the tree on the right: the matching part lights up on both sides. Switch tabs to see a model with a mocap body (dashed) and a weld equality (the spring), both explained in Sections 5 and 6.

    3Joints, coordinates and quaternions

    MuJoCo describes the whole system with generalized coordinates: one list of numbers qpos that fixes every body's pose, and one list qvel of velocities. Every joint owns a contiguous slice of each. A degree of freedom (DOF) is one independent direction of motion; nv counts them.

    Joint (mjw.JointType)Motionqposqvelqpos contents
    FREEmoves and turns freely76x y z, then quaternion w x y z
    BALLturns about a point43quaternion w x y z
    SLIDEslides along an axis11distance [m]
    HINGEturns about an axis11angle [rad]

    Why 7 and 6? An orientation needs 4 numbers when it is stored as a unit quaternion (below), but rotating has only 3 independent directions, so the angular velocity is a 3-vector. That is why nq and nv differ, and why you can never compute qpos += dt * qvel by hand for free or ball joints: the integrator uses quaternion integration for those slices (Ch 3, Ch 13).

    Quaternions are (w, x, y, z)

    MuJoCo stores orientations as unit quaternions. A rotation by angle \theta about the unit axis \mathbf{a} is

    q = \left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; a_x \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; a_y \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2},\; a_z \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)

    with the scalar part first. The identity is (1, 0, 0, 0), which is why the quaternion part of a free joint's qpos0 reads 1 0 0 0. Two facts to keep: q and -q are the same rotation, and a quaternion must have length 1 (normalize after editing by hand). Many other libraries (SciPy's Rotation.as_quat() by default, ROS messages) use (x, y, z, w). Feeding those four numbers to MuJoCo unchanged gives a different rotation, and nothing raises an error.

    The wrong reading turns a 90° turn about z into a 180° flip about a tilted axis. The helper functions mju_axisAngle2Quat, mju_quat2Mat, mju_rotVecQuat and mju_mulQuat all use the w-first order, and so do qpos, xquat, mocap_quat and every quat attribute in MJCF.

    Pitfall

    Converting from SciPy: Rotation.as_quat() returns (x, y, z, w) unless you pass scalar_first=True (SciPy 1.14+). Reorder with q = np.roll(q_xyzw, 1). MJWarp stores orientations in wp.quat arrays but keeps MuJoCo's w-first meaning; how that interacts with Warp's own quaternion functions is the storage pitfall of Ch 4.

    Joint limits

    A slide or hinge with range="lo hi" is limited (MJCF infers limited="true" from a range by default). Two units traps: MJCF angles are in degrees unless <compiler angle="radian"/>, but the compiled jnt_range is always in radians. A limit is not a hard wall. When the joint passes its range, MuJoCo adds one constraint row that pushes back, so the joint ends slightly beyond the limit, just like a contact penetrates slightly (Section 4).

    The class gave both joints damping 0.5 and a ±30° range; the elbow overrode the range. Pushed by gravity and the motor, the shoulder sits 0.1° past its limit, held there by a single mjCNSTR_LIMIT_JOINT row whose force (-4.587 N m) balances gravity and the motor. Inside the range the row does not exist: nefc is 0. Constraint rows appear and disappear every step, which is why MJWarp needs a row budget njmax (Ch 9).

    4Geoms and contacts: soft by design

    Geoms are the shapes that collide. Their type decides what size means:

    sphere

    size = radius.

    capsule, cylinder

    size = radius, half-length; or fromto + radius.

    box, ellipsoid

    size = three half-sizes / radii.

    plane

    infinite for collision; size = drawing half-extents and grid spacing. Only on static bodies.

    hfield, mesh, sdf

    shape from an asset (height grid, triangle mesh, signed-distance plugin); Ch 17-18.

    MJWarp mirrors the list in mjw.GeomType (PLANE, HFIELD, SPHERE, CAPSULE, ELLIPSOID, CYLINDER, BOX, MESH, SDF, plus FLEX for soft bodies). Which collision routine handles a pair of types is a lookup table (collision_driver.py::MJ_COLLISION_TABLE): simple pairs such as plane-sphere use closed-form primitive colliders (Ch 16), most of the rest go to the general convex algorithm (Ch 17); SDF and heightfield details are in Ch 18.

    Which pairs may touch: contype and conaffinity

    Each geom has two 32-bit masks. Two geoms are allowed to collide when (\text{contype}_1 \mathbin{\&} \text{conaffinity}_2) \ne 0 or (\text{contype}_2 \mathbin{\&} \text{conaffinity}_1) \ne 0. Both default to 1, so by default everything collides with everything. Setting both to 0 makes a geom a ghost (the mocap target's sphere in MOCAP_TARGET). On top of the masks, MuJoCo never collides two geoms of the same body, skips parent-child body pairs (unless the filterparent flag is disabled), skips pairs where neither body can move, and honours explicit <contact><exclude> and <pair> lists.

    Source

    mujoco_warp/_src/io.py · put_model precomputes every geom pair once: mask = (contype1 & conaffinity2) | (contype2 & conaffinity1), then removes same-body (self_collision), parent-child and excluded pairs. Pairs that fail never reach the GPU collision kernels.

    Version note

    In the 4.0.1 wheel this filter is inline code in put_model (the result is the local variable nxn_pairid_contact); the development tree moves it into a helper function _nxn_pairid_contact. Same rule, different place.

    Friction, condim, margin

    • friction="1 0.005 0.0001" (the defaults): sliding, torsional and rolling coefficients. Pairs use the element-wise maximum of the two geoms.
    • condim: how many directions the contact resists. 1 = normal only (frictionless), 3 = normal + 2 sliding directions (default), 4 adds torsion, 6 adds rolling.
    • margin makes contacts appear before the surfaces touch; gap keeps such early contacts inactive. Both default to 0.

    Contacts penetrate a little, on purpose

    Here is the idea that surprises most newcomers. MuJoCo does not forbid overlap. When two geoms come closer than their margin, collision detection writes a contact with a signed distance dist (negative = penetration), a position and a frame whose first row is the normal. The contact becomes constraint rows (four for the default pyramidal friction cone with condim 3), and the solver computes forces so that the penetration behaves like a stiff, damped spring that pulls it back toward zero. The result is a smooth, well-posed problem with a unique solution, which is good for optimization, for gradients and for the GPU. The price: a resting object sinks a fraction of a millimetre into the floor. The physics of why contacts are constraints is Ch 3 §8; the math is Ch 19.

    An intuition you can keep for the rest of the course (the derivation is Ch 19): solref = (timeconst, dampratio) of a spring-damper. timeconst is roughly how stiff (smaller corrects faster, so the object sinks less); dampratio is roughly how bouncy (1 = no bounce, smaller bounces). solimp shapes the impedance: how much of the violation the constraint resists, as a function of the penetration depth. Geoms carry solref/solimp; joint limits and equality constraints have their own.

    The five numbers of solimp = (dmin, dmax, width, midpoint, power) describe one curve: the impedance d, a number between 0 and 1, as a function of penetration depth. A larger d makes the constraint harder (it resists more of the violation). Example 2.7 below prints the default, [0.9 0.95 0.001 0.5 2]: d starts at dmin = 0.9 at zero penetration and rises to dmax = 0.95 over the first width = 0.001 m (1 mm) of penetration, then stays at 0.95. midpoint = 0.5 and power = 2 set the shape of that rise: an S-curve that is halfway up at half the width, where power 1 would make it a straight ramp. In practice you rarely touch solimp. The formula and how d enters the solver are in Ch 19.

    Halving timeconst cut the resting penetration to 29%. A spring that corrects twice as fast is about four times as stiff, so the static sag should drop toward a quarter; the impedance curve of solimp moves it a little. The widget below replays the real runs that --dump-json recorded for five time constants and two damping ratios.

    Two things show up in real data that a formula would hide. With a slow contact (timeconst = 0.08) the ball sinks centimetres during the impact before it is pushed back. And a lower damping ratio bounces, but also sinks less at rest, because MuJoCo's spring stiffness scales with 1/(\text{timeconst}\cdot\text{dampratio})^2. Domain randomization of solref is in Ch 10.

    5Equality constraints

    Joints build a tree. Sometimes you need a loop (a four-bar linkage), a glued pair of bodies, or two joints that move together. An equality constraint adds such a relation as soft constraint rows, solved together with contacts and limits.

    <equality>
  <connect body1="coupler" body2="rocker" anchor=".5 0 0"/>   <!-- anchor in body1's frame -->
  <weld    body1="box" body2="target" solref="0.02 1"/>
  <joint   joint1="finger_a" joint2="finger_b" polycoef="0 1 0 0 0"/>
</equality>

    Every equality has an active attribute, compiled into MjModel.eq_active0. At run time the switch lives in the data: mjd.eq_active[i] (in MJWarp d.eq_active, shape (nworld, neq), so each world can switch independently). It is an input you write, like ctrl.

    6Mocap bodies: positioned by you

    A mocap body (mocap="true") is a body whose pose you set directly through mjd.mocap_pos[k] and mjd.mocap_quat[k]. Physics never moves it: forces do not act on it, it has no joint and adds nothing to qpos. It must be a direct child of the world. body_mocapid[b] maps a body id to its mocap index k (-1 for normal bodies).

    On its own a mocap body is a moving obstacle or a target marker. Welded to a physical body, it becomes a handle: move the mocap body and the weld's spring drags the real body along, with realistic lag and contact forces. That is how teleoperation and motion-capture retargeting are done, and why the field is called mocap.

    While the weld is on, the box trails the target by 2.5 cm (the soft weld needs a small error to produce the force that drags the box around the circle). When eq_active[0] becomes 0, the box flies off along its current velocity and lands on the floor at z = 0.05 (its half-size, minus a tiny penetration). The MJWarp run with the same inputs agrees with C MuJoCo to 1.6e-7 m: same fields, same physics, float32. Writing d.mocap_pos from the host every step is fine for one world; Ch 8 writes per-world targets with a kernel.

    7Actuators, controls and sensors

    Actuators turn your commands into forces. You write the commands into mjd.ctrl (ctrl, length nu); every step the actuator computes

    \text{force} = \text{gain}\cdot\text{ctrl} + \text{bias}(\text{length}, \text{velocity})
    \text{joint torque} = \text{gear}\cdot\text{force}
    • <motor>: gain 1, no bias. ctrl is the force or torque itself (times gear).
    • <position kp="50"/>: a P servo. ctrl is a target position; force = kp (ctrl - q).
    • <velocity kv="5"/>, <intvelocity>, <muscle>, ...: other gain/bias combinations, and actuators with internal state act (length na). Ch 12 covers them all.
    • ctrlrange="-5 5" with ctrllimited (inferred from the range) clamps ctrl before use. Out-of-range commands are not an error; they are silently clamped.
    <actuator>
  <motor    name="shoulder" joint="hinge1" gear="1" ctrlrange="-5 5"/>   <!-- ctrl = torque -->
  <position name="elbow"    joint="hinge2" kp="20" ctrlrange="-1.5 1.5"/> <!-- ctrl = target angle -->
</actuator>

    MuJoCo 4: nu vs nactuator

    nu is the length of ctrl (control inputs) and nactuator counts actuators. With ordinary actuators, each reads exactly one control and the two are equal, as in every model of this chapter. MuJoCo 4 lets an actuator read several controls or none (actuator_ctrladr, actuator_ctrlnum), so the two can differ; MJWarp sizes d.ctrl by nu and d.actuator_force by nactuator. Ch 12 shows such actuators.

    Sensors and sensordata addressing

    Sensors are declared in <sensor> and computed during the step. All their values are packed, in declaration order, into one flat vector mjd.sensordata of length nsensordata. Sensor i occupies sensordata[sensor_adr[i] : sensor_adr[i] + sensor_dim[i]].

    A constant 2 N m on the shoulder does not hold the pendulum at an angle; it shifts the point it swings around (q1 oscillates between about 0 and 0.55 rad; it starts at 0). Because energy is enabled in this model (<flag energy="enable"/>), mjd.energy holds potential and kinetic energy; the total moves because the motor does work. The last line shows the clamp: asking for 50 N m gives 5 N m.

    9The simulation loop

    Two calls do almost everything:

    • mujoco.mj_forward(m, d) computes every output from the current state and inputs: poses, the mass matrix, contacts, constraint rows, forces, accelerations qacc, sensors. It does not change the state or the time.
    • mujoco.mj_step(m, d) = forward + integrate: it then uses qacc to advance qvel, qpos, act and time by one timestep.

    The stepper below walks through one call of MJWarp's step on BALL_DROP, stage by stage, with the numbers MuJoCo actually produces. The first pass is the very first step (ball in the air); the second is a step at t = 1 s, when the ball rests on the floor and the contact carries its weight.

    One step, stage by stage

    forward.py::stepnumbers from C MuJoCo 4.0.1
    def step(m, d):
  forward(m, d):  # _forward
    fwd_position(m, d)  # kinematics, M,
                        # collision, efc rows
    sensor_pos(m, d)
    fwd_velocity(m, d)  # bias forces
    fwd_actuation(m, d) # ctrl -> force
    fwd_acceleration(m, d)  # M^-1 f
    solve(m, d)         # -> qacc
  euler(m, d):
    # eulerdamp: qacc re-solved with (M + h*D)
    # unless disabled (Ch 13); then _advance:
    qvel += h * qacc
    qpos += h * qvel    # new qvel
    act  += h * act_dot # (if na > 0)
    time += h

    Recording a trajectory

    Resets and keyframes

    mj_resetData(m, d) puts the state back to the model defaults: qpos = qpos0 (the pose written in the MJCF), zero velocities, zero time. A keyframe stores a named state in the model (key_qpos, key_qvel, key_ctrl, ...); mj_resetDataKeyframe(m, d, k) resets and then loads keyframe k.

    Pitfall: set the state, then call forward

    Resetting or writing qpos by hand does not update xpos, contacts or sensors. Call mj_forward before reading derived quantities, as the last two lines of Example 2.4 show. The same holds in MJWarp: after d.qpos.assign(...), call mjw.forward(m, d) before reading d.xpos.

    MJWarp has the same resets, for all worlds at once: mjw.reset_data(m, d, reset=None) (an optional per-world boolean mask) and mjw.reset_data_keyframe(m, d, key), where key may be one integer for every world or an array with one keyframe index per world. Ch 8 uses them for episode resets.

    10Derived quantities: poses, frames and the mass matrix

    After mj_forward (or a step) the data holds the results of kinematics, all in world coordinates:

    Dynamics adds velocities (cvel), forces (qfrc_bias, qfrc_passive, qfrc_actuator, qfrc_constraint) and the joint-space mass matrix M(q): kinetic energy is \tfrac12 \dot q^\top M \dot q, and the equation of motion is M \ddot q = \tau - c. M is symmetric positive definite and changes with the pose. MuJoCo stores only its nonzeros (MuJoCo 4 names the field mjd.M; older versions used qM), and mujoco.mj_fullM(m, d, dst) expands it.

    For a planar double pendulum the matrix has a closed form, M_{11} = a + 2b\cos q_2,\; M_{12} = c + b\cos q_2,\; M_{22} = c, and the two printed matrices fix a = 0.427, b = 0.125, c = 0.0885 (b = m₂ L₁ l₂ = 1 x 0.5 x 0.25 exactly).     11Building models in Python with MjSpec

    Writing XML strings with f-strings works (the shared box_stack(n) does exactly that), but it is fragile: a missing quote is a parse error at run time. mujoco.MjSpec is MuJoCo's editable model object. You add elements with methods, edit attributes as Python properties, and call compile() to get an MjModel. A spec can also be loaded from XML (MjSpec.from_string) and written back with to_xml().

    body.add_freejoint()

    Both routes give identical models. The spec stays editable after compiling: the example adds a fourth box and recompiles. Note what to_xml() writes: <compiler angle="radian"/>, because a spec stores angles in radians, and no dir on the light because 0 0 -1 is the default. Ch 17 builds its test shapes with MjSpec.     Tip

    spec.compile() raises a ValueError with the compiler's message when the model is invalid (for example a mocap body that is not a child of the world), which is easier to debug than a malformed XML string.

    12Seeing a frame

    MuJoCo renders with OpenGL through mujoco.Renderer(m, height, width): update_scene(d, camera="name") then render() returns an (H, W, 3) uint8 image. On a headless machine such as Colab, OpenGL needs a backend chosen before import mujoco: os.environ["MUJOCO_GL"] = "egl" (GPU) or "osmesa" (CPU, slow). The [viewer] extra of mujoco-warp installs the Python OpenGL packages the renderer needs. If mujoco was already imported with another backend, restart the runtime.

    The fallback is not just a workaround: MJWarp has its own renderer that ray-traces all worlds in one launch, directly from device data (create_render_context, refit_bvh, render, get_rgb). Ch 5 compares the two paths and Ch 27 is the deep dive.

    Pitfall

    Without PyOpenGL, or with MUJOCO_GL=egl but no EGL library, constructing mujoco.Renderer raises ImportError, not RuntimeError. Catch Exception and print the class, as Example 2.12 does. On the CPU box used to verify this course the output shows exactly that.

    14Check yourself

    Quiz

    A model has one free body and two hinges. What are nq and nv?

    Free: 7 qpos (3 position + 4 quaternion), 6 qvel. Each hinge: 1 + 1. Total 9 and 8.

    You set mjd.qpos[3:7] = [0, 0, 0, 1] on a free joint, thinking "identity". What happens?

    MuJoCo reads (w, x, y, z): w = 0 and the axis is z, so the angle is 2 acos(0) = 180°. The identity is (1, 0, 0, 0).

    What does mj_forward not do?

    Forward computes outputs only. mj_step = forward + integration, which is what changes the state and time.

    How is a mocap body different from a free body?

    You set the pose every step; forces do not act on it. Weld a free body to it to drag that body around (Example 2.9).

    A ball resting on the floor reports contact.dist = -0.00037. What is the right reaction?

    The constraint behaves like a stiff spring-damper, so a small penetration carries the weight. Example 2.7 halves timeconst and the depth drops to 29%.

    Geom A has contype 1, conaffinity 0. Geom B has contype 0, conaffinity 1. Do they collide?

    The test is (A.contype & B.conaffinity) OR (B.contype & A.conaffinity). One non-zero half is enough.

    A model has 3 sensors: jointpos, jointvel and framepos. What is nsensordata?

    1 + 1 + 3 = 5. Sensor i lives at sensordata[sensor_adr[i] : sensor_adr[i] + sensor_dim[i]].

    In MJWarp, why is m.opt.timestep an array while m.opt.iterations is a plain int?

    Discrete options choose which kernels run (and are the same for every world); continuous ones can be randomized per world (Ch 10).

    Stretch: close a four-bar linkage

    Stretch

    A tree cannot contain a loop. Build a four-bar linkage: a crank hinged to the world at (0, 0, 1), a coupler hinged to the crank's lower end, and a rocker hinged to the world at (0.5, 0, 1). Close the loop with a <connect> equality between the coupler's far end and the rocker's lower end. Spin the crank (qvel[0] = 2) and measure how far the two ends drift apart over 2 s. Then tighten the equality's solref and watch the drift shrink. Bonus: build it with MjSpec and step it in MJWarp.

    Show one solution
    <mujoco>
  <option timestep="0.002"/>
  <default><geom contype="0" conaffinity="0"/></default>  <!-- links overlap at joints -->
  <worldbody>
    <body name="crank" pos="0 0 1">
      <joint name="j1" type="hinge" axis="0 1 0" damping="0.05"/>
      <geom type="capsule" fromto="0 0 0 0 0 -.3" size=".02"/>
      <body name="coupler" pos="0 0 -.3">
        <joint name="j2" type="hinge" axis="0 1 0"/>
        <geom type="capsule" fromto="0 0 0 .5 0 0" size=".02"/>
      </body>
    </body>
    <body name="rocker" pos=".5 0 1">
      <joint name="j3" type="hinge" axis="0 1 0"/>
      <geom type="capsule" fromto="0 0 0 0 0 -.3" size=".02"/>
    </body>
  </worldbody>
  <equality><connect body1="coupler" body2="rocker" anchor=".5 0 0"/></equality>
</mujoco>

    Measure the gap as the distance between xpos[2] + xmat[2] @ (0.5, 0, 0) and xpos[3] + xmat[3] @ (0, 0, -0.3). With default solref the CPU run gave a maximum gap of about 7 mm over 1000 steps, with nq = 3, neq = 1 and 3 constraint rows. Without the contype="0" default the overlapping capsules also produce contacts (7 rows instead of 3).

    Now tighten the equality: <connect body1="coupler" body2="rocker" anchor=".5 0 0" solref="0.005 1"/>. The time constant drops from the default 0.02 s to 0.005 s, and the maximum gap over the same 1000 steps falls from about 6.8 mm to about 0.44 mm (C MuJoCo 4.0.1, CPU run). Keep timeconst at least twice the timestep (here 0.004 s), or MuJoCo clamps it.

    16Summary

    Key takeaways

    • MjModel is constant; MjData holds state (time, qpos, qvel, act), inputs (ctrl, mocap_pos, eq_active, xfrc_applied) and outputs. MJWarp keeps the names, with a few renames such as ncon → nacon, and adds a leading nworld axis to most Data arrays.
    • Bodies form a tree under the world body (body 0); joints say how a body moves relative to its parent; geoms collide; sites mark points. Default classes are an MJCF convenience that disappears at compile time.
    • Free joints own 7 qpos / 6 qvel, ball 4 / 3, slide and hinge 1 / 1. Use jnt_qposadr / jnt_dofadr to find a joint's slots.
    • Quaternions are (w, x, y, z); the identity is (1, 0, 0, 0). Other libraries often use (x, y, z, w).
    • Contacts and limits are soft constraints: small penetration is normal. solref = (timeconst, dampratio) ≈ (how stiff, how bouncy); solimp shapes the impedance.
    • Equality constraints (connect, weld, joint, tendon) close loops and glue bodies; eq_active switches them per world. Mocap bodies are posed by you, and a weld turns them into handles.
    • mj_step = mj_forward + integrate. Reset with mj_resetData / mj_resetDataKeyframe and call forward before reading derived quantities.
    • MjSpec builds and edits models in Python; mujoco.Renderer needs MUJOCO_GL set before import, and MJWarp's batch renderer is the fallback.

    Stuck? See Troubleshooting or the course map.
