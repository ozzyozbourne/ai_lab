    Part V · Chapter 19

    Constraints as Convex Optimization

    Every step, MJWarp turns each contact, joint limit, friction loss and equality into a few soft constraint rows, then finds the one acceleration per world that best trades "move as the unconstrained dynamics want" against "respect every row". This chapter builds that problem from a single ball on a floor up to the full efc arrays, and verifies the answer numerically from MJWarp's own outputs.

    Before you start: recap

    • solref = (timeconst, dampratio) of a spring-damper; solimp shapes impedance (Ch 2 §4). This chapter derives the formulas.

    • The equation of motion is M\ddot q = \tau_{smooth} + J^T f; qacc_smooth = M^{-1} qfrc_smooth is the acceleration with no constraints, and factor_m writes the factor qLD (Ch 11).
    • qfrc_smooth collects passive, bias and actuator forces (Ch 12).
    • d.eq_active switches an equality on or off per world, and reset_data restores it from m.eq_active0 (Ch 8 §6).
    • Float atomics sum in arbitrary order, so low bits can change; Ch 4 §9 (Ch 4). Counter atomics hand out slots in arrival order.

    1Why contacts are hard, and how MuJoCo makes them soft

    A ball resting on a floor seems simple: the floor pushes up exactly enough to stop the ball from falling through. Writing that down as math is surprisingly awkward. Call the gap between ball and floor g and the contact force f. A rigid, perfect contact obeys three rules at once:

    g \ge 0, \qquad f \ge 0, \qquad g \cdot f = 0

    Either the ball is separated (g>0, no force) or it touches (g=0, any non-negative force). That is a complementarity condition. With many contacts and friction it becomes a linear complementarity problem (LCP). LCPs are combinatorial: you must guess which contacts are touching and which are sliding, and with friction the problem can have no solution or several. The force is also a discontinuous function of the state: it jumps from zero to "whatever it takes".

    MuJoCo, and MJWarp with it, does something different. It lets a constraint be violated a little, like a stiff spring-damper, and it computes forces by minimizing one convex function of the acceleration. A convex problem has exactly one minimum, every local minimum is that global minimum, and the answer depends smoothly on the state. That is what makes it a good fit for a GPU running thousands of worlds with the same code: no case analysis per world, just "go downhill until done".

    The 1-D toy: one ball, one row

    Take a ball of mass m pressed into the floor by depth p and moving with vertical velocity v. Without the floor its acceleration would be a_0 = -9.81 (gravity): that is qacc_smooth. With the floor, MuJoCo chooses the acceleration a that minimizes

    \ell(a) = \tfrac12\, m\,(a - a_0)^2 \;+\; \begin{cases} \tfrac12\, D\,(a - a_{ref})^2 & a < a_{ref} \\ 0 & a \ge a_{ref} \end{cases}

    The first term says "stay close to what the unconstrained dynamics want", weighted by the mass. The second term is the constraint row. It is active only while a < a_{ref}: the row tries to push the acceleration up to a reference acceleration a_{ref}, and gives up the moment the ball already accelerates upward faster than that. D is the row's weight (its "constraint mass"). Setting the derivative to zero on the active branch gives a closed form:

    a^* = \frac{m\,a_0 + D\,a_{ref}}{m + D}, \qquad f = D\,(a_{ref} - a^*) = m\,(a^* - a_0)

    The last equality is Newton's second law: the force the row exerts equals mass times the change in acceleration it caused. Where do D and a_{ref} come from? From solref and solimp, through three numbers k (stiffness), b (damping) and the impedance d \in (0,1) (§8 derives them line by line from constraint.py::_contact_kbimp):

    a_{ref} = -b\,v - k\,d\,\mathrm{pos}, \qquad D = \frac{d}{(1-d)\,w}, \qquad \mathrm{pos} = -p

    Here w is the row's inverse weight (about 1/m for a ball). So a_{ref} is a spring-damper in acceleration units: deeper penetration or a faster approach asks for more upward acceleration. And D blends "hard" and "soft": as d \to 1, D \to \infty and a^* \to a_{ref}; as d \to 0 the row has no say.

    Example 19.1 does the same computation in NumPy, one world per (p, v) pair, and checks it against MJWarp. The floor and ball use condim="1", so each contact has exactly one row of type CONTACT_FRICTIONLESS and the toy is exact.

    Read the table row by row. At 0.2 mm the impedance is still climbing (0.904), so D is smaller and the ball still accelerates downward (a^* = -0.489): it keeps sinking. From 1 mm on, |pos| ≥ width, so the impedance is saturated at dmax = 0.95 and D = 0.95/(0.05 \cdot 1) = 19. A downward velocity (-0.5 m/s) adds -b v = 105.3 \cdot 0.5 \approx 52.6 (with b = 2/(d_{max}\,\mathrm{timeconst}) = 2/(0.95 \cdot 0.02)) to a_{ref}; an upward one (+0.5) makes a_{ref} negative and the row inactive, so the force is exactly 0. The last case has the ball 1 mm above the floor: no row exists at all (nefc = 0). Newton's law holds at the optimum in every world.

    From toy to spring-damper

    Integrate the toy over time and the ball behaves like a mass on a spring-damper sitting under the floor. k and b come from solref = (timeconst, dampratio): with the impedance near 1, the penetration obeys \ddot x + b\,\dot x + k\,x \approx 0, and timeconst is its time scale. A small time constant gives a stiff contact that recovers quickly; a large one gives a soft, spongy contact that sinks deeper and rings longer. dampratio = 1 is critical damping (no bounce); below 1 the contact oscillates.

    The real thing, with one world per time constant, is Example 19.5 in §8.

    2The math toolkit the solver needs

    Before scaling the toy up, we need five ideas from optimization. They are the whole vocabulary of the next chapter's solvers, so this section owns them (they are also collected in the math toolkit reference). Example 19.12 is plain NumPy.

    A quadratic and its minimum

    A quadratic in n variables is

    f(x) = \tfrac12\, x^T A x - b^T x, \qquad \nabla f(x) = A x - b, \qquad \nabla^2 f = A

    If A is symmetric positive definite (SPD, all eigenvalues positive) the level sets are ellipses and there is exactly one minimum, where the gradient vanishes: A x^* = b. Solving that system is the whole problem. For an SPD matrix the standard way is a Cholesky factorization A = L L^T followed by two triangular solves.

    Recall from Ch 11

    The mass matrix M is SPD. factor_m writes qLD (an L D L^T factorization) and solve_m uses it to apply M^{-1}; solving with an old qLD gives wrong qacc (Ch 11 §9). The Gauss term of the constraint problem is exactly a quadratic with A = M.

    Convexity

    A function is convex if the chord between any two points of its graph lies above the graph. A quadratic with SPD A is (strictly) convex. So is \tfrac12 D \min(x,0)^2, the one-sided row cost of the toy, and so is a sum of convex functions. For convex functions "downhill" always leads to the same place: there are no false valleys, and any point where the gradient is zero is the global minimum. This is why MJWarp can stop each world independently as soon as its gradient is small.

    Three ways downhill: gradient descent, Newton, conjugate gradient

    • Gradient descent steps against the gradient, x \leftarrow x - s\,\nabla f. Cheap per step, but on an elongated bowl it zigzags; the number of steps grows with the condition number \lambda_{max}/\lambda_{min}.
    • Newton's method uses the curvature, x \leftarrow x - (\nabla^2 f)^{-1}\nabla f. On a quadratic it lands on the minimum in one step, at the price of a factorization.
    • Conjugate gradient (CG) picks each new direction to be A-conjugate to the previous ones and minimizes exactly along it. On an n-dimensional quadratic it is exact in at most n steps, using only products with A.

    Smooth vs piecewise quadratic, and the line search

    The constraint cost is not one quadratic. Each row contributes a quadratic only on one side of a threshold (active vs satisfied), and friction loss turns linear beyond a threshold. The total is a piecewise quadratic: smooth inside each region where no row changes state, with a jump in curvature at every kink where a row switches. It is still convex and its slope is continuous.

    Both of MJWarp's solvers work along a search direction and then ask: how far should I go along it? Along a ray x + \alpha s, the cost is a 1-D piecewise quadratic in \alpha, and minimizing it exactly is cheap: inside one piece, one Newton step is exact; if it lands in another piece, repeat from there. Each probe costs a single pass over the rows (each row is one quadratic in \alpha, see solver.py::_eval_pt). That is an exact line search. The stepper below walks Part 2 of Example 19.12.

    3The Gauss principle and the primal problem

    In 1829 Gauss stated that a constrained mechanical system moves so that its acceleration is as close as possible to the unconstrained one, measured in the mass metric. For hard constraints that is "minimize \tfrac12 (a-a_s)^T M (a-a_s) subject to the constraints". MuJoCo replaces "subject to" with soft penalty terms, one per scalar constraint row i:

    \ddot q^* = \arg\min_a \;\underbrace{\tfrac12 (a - a_s)^T M (a - a_s)}_{\text{Gauss term}} \;+\; \sum_i s_i\big(J_i a - a_{ref,i}\big), \qquad a_s = \texttt{qacc\_smooth}
    • J is the constraint Jacobian, one row per scalar constraint (nefc x nv). J_i a is the acceleration along constraint i.
    • a_{ref,i} is the row's reference acceleration (efc.aref), the spring-damper of §1.
    • s_i is a convex row cost, weighted by efc.D. Its shape depends on the row type: \tfrac12 D x^2 for equalities (two-sided), \tfrac12 D \min(x,0)^2 for limits and pyramidal contact rows (one-sided), a Huber function for friction loss (§9), and a distance-to-cone cost for elliptic contacts (§9).

    The constraint force of a row is minus the slope of its cost, f_i = -s_i'(J_i a - a_{ref,i}) (efc.force), and the generalized constraint force is J^T f (qfrc_constraint). Setting the gradient of the total cost to zero gives exactly the equation of motion:

    \nabla \ell(a) = M(a - a_s) - J^T f(a) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad M\ddot q = \underbrace{M a_s}_{\texttt{qfrc\_smooth}} + J^T f

    MJWarp's solver computes exactly this gradient: grad = Ma - qfrc_smooth - qfrc_constraint (solver.py::_update_gradient). Because every term is convex and the Gauss term is strictly convex (M is SPD), the problem has one minimizer and the solver may start anywhere. MJWarp starts from the previous step's acceleration (qacc_warmstart), unless DisableBit.WARMSTART is set or there are no rows (solver.py::_solve_init_dof).

    Going deeper: the dual view, R and why MJWarp has no PGS

    Write R_i = 1/D_i (the regularizer, _efc_D computes R = w(1-d)/d). Eliminating the acceleration from the optimality conditions gives a problem in the forces alone: minimize \tfrac12 f^T (A + R) f + f^T(J a_s - a_{ref}) over the friction cones, with the Delassus matrix A = J M^{-1} J^T. That is the dual, and a projected Gauss-Seidel (PGS) solver works there. Because R > 0, the dual is strictly convex too: soft constraints make the force unique, which the rigid LCP cannot promise. MJWarp implements only the two primal solvers (CG and Newton) that work on a; types.SolverType lists # unsupported: PGS, and put_model raises NotImplementedError for it. The primal form suits a GPU: it is one smooth-ish problem per world over nv unknowns, with row terms that are independent and can be evaluated in parallel.

    4Row types, states and the efc arrays

    Every scalar constraint row lives in the per-world d.efc arrays (types.py::Constraint). Rows are grouped by category, always in this order:

    \underbrace{\text{equality}}_{ne} \;\Big|\; \underbrace{\text{friction loss}}_{nf} \;\Big|\; \underbrace{\text{limits}}_{nl} \;\Big|\; \text{contacts}

    The counts are per world: d.ne, d.nf, d.nl, d.nefc, all of shape (nworld,). Kernels rely on the order: the solver decides is_equality = efcid < ne and is_friction = efcid < ne + nf, and contact rows start at ne + nf + nl.

    Example 19.2 builds one row of every category: a joint equality, a hinge with friction loss that starts 5 degrees past its limit, and a box resting on a plane (4 contacts, condim 3, pyramidal: 4 rows each). It prints every row next to MuJoCo C.

    Three things to notice. First, the limit row is SATISFIED even though pos = -0.087 (5 degrees past the limit): the equality row yanks the hinge back so hard that the acceleration along the limit row already exceeds its aref. A violated constraint is not automatically an active one; activity is decided at the optimum. Second, the friction-loss row is saturated (LINEARNEG) and carries exactly +frictionloss = 0.05. Third, the forces agree with MuJoCo C to 3e-5: MJWarp computes in float32, C in float64. The iteration counts differ (3 vs 1) because the two codes start from different points (MJWarp has no "best of warmstart and smooth" choice outside the discrete integrator) and use different stopping arithmetic.

    5Verify the optimum yourself

    This is the centerpiece of the chapter: take MJWarp's arrays, rebuild the cost of §3 in NumPy, and check that MJWarp's qacc really is its minimizer. For pyramidal contacts every row cost is \tfrac12 D \min(x, 0)^2, so the rebuild is four lines. The scene is a box tilted by 10 degrees resting on one edge (2 contacts, 8 rows), sliding at a different speed in each world.

    What this proves, in order:

    1. The force formula. f = -D\min(J\ddot q - a_{ref}, 0) recomputed in NumPy matches efc.force to within about 1e-3 (float32 rounding).
    2. The equation of motion. M(\ddot q - a_s) = J^T f holds to about 1e-3 on forces of order 100, using MuJoCo C's mj_fullM for M, and also using MJWarp's own efc.Ma and qfrc_smooth.
    3. Optimality. 200 random perturbations of size 1e-3 never lower the cost. The smallest change is always positive, 1e-11 to 1e-9 relative: the second-order growth of a minimum, at float32 resolution.
    4. Pyramid edges switch off. At rest all 8 rows are active. As the box slides faster, rows go SATISFIED: at 3 m/s only rows 2 and 6 carry force. Each pyramidal row is J_n \pm \mu J_t; when sliding is fast, only the edge that opposes the motion stays in contact with the cone, which is the pyramidal version of "kinetic friction at the cone boundary".

    Note

    MJWarp works in float32. Expect relative differences around 1e-4 to 1e-6 against MuJoCo C, and do not ask for a tolerance below about 1e-7: the solver cannot resolve it (solver.py scales its noise floor _ALPHA_NOISE_EPS from float32 epsilon). The repository's own test accepts a cost within 2.5 % of C's (solver_test.py).

    6Inside make_constraint: how rows are reserved

    forward calls constraint.make_constraint(m, d) right after collision (forward.py::fwd_position). It launches one kernel family after another, always in this order, and the order of launches is what makes the categories contiguous:

    Inside a category, rows are not handed out in a fixed order. Every thread that wants rows does

    base_efcid = wp.atomic_add(nefc_out, worldid, ndim)   # _efc_contact_init: one block per contact
for dim in range(ndim):
    efcid = base_efcid + dim
    if efcid >= njmax_in:
        contact_efc_address_out[conid, dim] = -1      # this row does not fit
    else:
        contact_efc_address_out[conid, dim] = efcid

    So each contact reserves a contiguous block whose width is its row count (§9), and the contact's efc_address remembers where it landed. Which contact gets the first block depends on which GPU thread reaches the atomic first. Run the same step twice on a GPU and the blocks can come out in a different order; each block's contents are identical. Equality rows come out per kernel (all connects, then all welds, ...), not in eq-id order as in MuJoCo C.

    Switch the stepper to njmax 16 and step to the end: the counter keeps growing past njmax (so d.nefc can be larger than d.njmax), rows past the line are dropped, and a contact whose block straddles the line keeps the rows that fit and gets -1 for the rest. Dropped rows are missing constraints: objects sink. Sizing is Ch 9's topic.

    7Dense or sparse: how J is stored

    A contact row touches only the dofs of the two bodies' kinematic chains. In a scene of many free bodies almost every entry of J is zero, so MJWarp can store it in CSR form. The rules, from io.py:

    • jacobian="auto" (the default) is sparse when nv > 32 and dense otherwise (io.py::is_sparse). "dense" or "sparse" force a layout through MuJoCo's mj_isSparse.
    • Forcing jacobian="dense" above 60 dofs raises ValueError: Dense is unsupported for nv > 60 in put_model; "auto" is already sparse there.
    • Dense: efc.J has shape (nworld, njmax_pad, nv_pad). njmax_pad is njmax rounded up to the 16-row tile (TILE_SIZE_JTDAJ_DENSE = 16). nv_pad is round_up(nv, 4) for nv ≤ 48 (one-tile Cholesky), and above 48 round_up(nv + 1, 16) for Newton (augmented row) and round_up(nv, 16) otherwise (CG) (io.py::_get_padded_sizes).
    • Sparse: efc.J and J_colind have shape (nworld, 1, njmax_nnz); J_rownnz and J_rowadr have shape (nworld, njmax). njmax_nnz defaults to the estimate of io.py::_default_njmax_nnz (all equalities, friction and limits active, and njmax contact rows at the largest body-pair nonzero count). Running out sets its own bit, OverflowType.NJMAX_NNZ (_efc_nnz_overflow).

    To count a contact row's nonzeros, _efc_contact_init walks the two bodies' dof chains upward through dof_parentid, always stepping the larger dof index, and stops where the chains meet (common ancestor dofs cancel in J = J_{body2} - J_{body1}). That walk visits dofs from high to low, which is why J_colind comes out in descending order.

    At nv 36 the dense layout costs only 1.4x more, because nv_pad = 36 and most of the buffer is the unused rows up to njmax. The ratio grows with nv: dense memory is nworld * njmax_pad * nv_pad * 4 bytes, so 8192 worlds with njmax 512 and nv 60 (nv_pad 64 with Newton) need about 1 GiB for efc.J alone, while CSR grows with the actual nonzeros. Sparsity also changes which kernels run (_efc_contact_jac_sparse vs the tiled dense kernel, and the sparse J^T D J blocks in Newton), which is a Ch 20 and Ch 25 topic.

    8Softness: from solref and solimp to D and aref

    Every row's D and aref come from two functions in constraint.py. _contact_kbimp turns solref and solimp into (k, b, d); _efc_row turns those into the row. Despite its name, _contact_kbimp serves every row type: equalities use eq_solref/eq_solimp, limits jnt_solref/jnt_solimp, friction loss dof_solref/dof_solimp, contacts the mixed values of the two geoms.

    Stiffness and damping: solref

    \text{solref} = (\tau, \zeta) > 0:\quad k = \frac{1}{d_{max}^2\,\tau^2\,\zeta^2}, \qquad b = \frac{2}{d_{max}\,\tau}
    \text{solref} = (-K, -B) \le 0 \;\text{(direct)}:\quad k = \frac{K}{d_{max}^2}, \qquad b = \frac{B}{d_{max}}

    The closed-loop violation obeys \ddot x + d\,(b\dot x + k\,d\,x) = (1-d)\,a_0 (the constrained acceleration is d\,a_{ref} + (1-d)\,a_0). With d \approx d_{max} that is \ddot x + (2/\tau)\,\dot x + x/(\tau\zeta)^2 \approx 0: natural frequency 1/(\tau\zeta) and damping ratio \zeta, which is why "timeconst, dampratio" are the knobs. The refsafe rule runs first: unless DisableBit.REFSAFE is set (or the integrator is DISCRETE, which has its own rule), \tau \leftarrow \max(\tau, 2h) with timestep h. A spring stiffer than the step can resolve is quietly softened, which is why a tiny timeconst cannot make a step explode. MJWarp reads the timestep per world (opt.timestep is batched), so refsafe is per world too.

    Impedance: solimp

    solimp = (dmin, dmax, width, mid, power) sets the impedance d as a function of how deep the violation is, x = |\mathrm{pos}|/\text{width}:

    d(x) = d_{min} + (d_{max} - d_{min})\,y(x),\qquad y(x) = \begin{cases} x^p / m^{p-1} & x \le m \\ 1 - (1-x)^p / (1-m)^{p-1} & m < x < 1 \\ 1 & x \ge 1 \end{cases}

    Two power curves meet at mid (m) to form a sigmoid from dmin at zero violation to dmax at width. power = 1 is a straight line. Before use, dmin, dmax and mid are clamped to [MJ_MINIMP, MJ_MAXIMP] = [0.0001, 0.9999], width to at least MJ_MINVAL and power to at least 1. If dmin == dmax the impedance is just their mean.

    The row: _efc_row

    # constraint.py::_efc_row (continuous-time integrators), simplified
kbimp = _contact_kbimp(opt_disableflags, timestep, solref, solimp, pos_imp, is_discrete)
k = 0.0 if type is FRICTION_DOF or FRICTION_TENDON else kbimp[0]   # friction rows: no spring
b, imp = kbimp[1], kbimp[2]
D_out[worldid, efcid]    = 1.0 / max(invweight * (1.0 - imp) / imp, MJ_MINVAL)   # _efc_D
aref_out[worldid, efcid] = -k * imp * pos_aref - b * vel - aref_shift
pos_out[worldid, efcid]  = pos_aref + margin
    • invweight approximates the row's diagonal of J M^{-1} J^T, precomputed at qpos0 (body_invweight0, dof_invweight0, tendon_invweight0; the derived constants of Ch 10). For a contact it is the sum of the two bodies' translational body_invweight0; the world body contributes 0.
    • So R = 1/D = w(1-d)/d scales with the row's natural "softness" w: the same solimp means the same relative softness for a light and a heavy body.
    • vel is J\dot q (efc.Jqvel). pos stored in efc.pos includes the margin back: dist - includemargin + includemargin = dist.

    Friction rows get extra scaling in _efc_contact_update. For a pyramidal contact with condim > 1 and friction \mu = friction[0]:

    w_{pyr} = 2\,\mu^2\,(1 + \mu^2)\,w \,/\, \text{impratio}

    For an elliptic contact the normal row keeps w; tangent row dimid ≥ 1 divides its D by (\mu_0/\mu_{dimid-1})^2/\text{impratio}, with \mu = contact.friction (its two sliding entries are equal, so both sliding rows simply get D \cdot \text{impratio}), uses solreffriction if it is non-zero, and has no spring term (pos_aref = 0): its aref is -b\,v. In ex2 the box (8 kg, w = 0.125) has \mu = 1, because a contact takes the larger friction of its two geoms and the floor's default is 1, so w_{pyr} = 0.5 and D = 0.95/(0.05 \cdot 0.5) = 38, exactly the printed value.

    Pitfall: impratio is stored inverted

    You set <option impratio="..."/> (or mjm.opt.impratio) as in MuJoCo. MJWarp stores m.opt.impratio_invsqrt = 1/sqrt(impratio) instead (io.py), batched per world. To randomize impratio on an mjw.Model, write impratio_invsqrt, not impratio: there is no m.opt.impratio field. A larger impratio makes friction rows stiffer relative to the normal row, which reduces slip.

    A solref sweep across worlds

    geom_solref is a batchable model field, so one launch can test eight time constants. Example 19.5 drops BALL_DROP from 0.3 m with timeconst from 0.005 to 0.1 s (log-spaced), dampratio 1. Stiffness falls as 1/\tau^2, so the ball lands deeper and settles more slowly as \tau grows.     9Friction: pyramidal and elliptic cones, and friction loss

    Recall from Ch 9

    Contact force must lie inside a cone of half-angle atan(mu) (Ch 9 §4). Ch 9 gave "pyramidal 2(condim-1), elliptic condim" as a black box. Here is where it comes from.

    condim: which directions resist

    condimresistspyramidal rowselliptic rowstype
    1normal only (frictionless)11CONTACT_FRICTIONLESS
    3+ 2 sliding directions43PYRAMIDAL / ELLIPTIC
    4+ torsion about the normal64PYRAMIDAL / ELLIPTIC
    6+ 2 rolling directions106PYRAMIDAL / ELLIPTIC

    Pyramidal: independent one-sided rows

    The pyramidal cone (ConeType.PYRAMIDAL, the default) approximates the circular cone by its edges. For condim 3 the edges are n \pm \mu_1 t_1 and n \pm \mu_2 t_2, and each edge is one row with the same one-sided cost as a frictionless contact. In _efc_contact_jac_dense row dimid is J_normal + frii_sign * J_tangent, with dimid2 = dimid/2 + 1 picking the direction and the sign alternating with dimid & 1: rows 0,1 are n \pm \mu_1 t_1, rows 2,3 are n \pm \mu_2 t_2, then torsion, then rolling. The contact-frame force is recovered from the non-negative edge forces f_j \ge 0:

    f_n = \sum_j f_j, \qquad f_{t_i} = \mu_i\,(f_{2i-2} - f_{2i-1}), \quad i = 1, \dots, \text{condim}-1

    Every force made this way lies inside the pyramid, and the pyramid is inscribed in the circular cone (its cross-section is a square with corners on the circle). Each row is independent, which makes the pyramid cheap and GPU friendly: no coupling inside the line search, and Newton can update its Hessian incrementally when a few rows flip (Ch 20).

    Elliptic: coupled rows and three zones

    The elliptic cone (ConeType.ELLIPTIC) keeps the exact cone f_n \ge \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i (f_{t_i}/\mu_i)^2}. Its condim rows (normal first, then the tangents) are coupled. solver.py::_eval_constraint classifies the contact from the rows' residuals x = J a - a_{ref}. Here \mu is not the raw friction: the kernel uses mu = friction[0] * impratio_invsqrt, i.e. \mu = \mu_1/\sqrt{\text{impratio}}, and this \mu is the one in N, in the zone tests and in d_m. The tangent weights stay unscaled (friction[i]). With N = \mu\,x_0 and T = \lVert (\mu_i x_i)_i \rVert \ge 0, there are three zones:

    • top, N \ge \mu T: the contact separates fast enough. SATISFIED, zero force.
    • bottom, \mu N + T \le 0: deep inside the dual cone. Every row is QUADRATIC, force -D x, as if all directions were stuck.
    • middle: CONE. The cost is \tfrac12 d_m (N - \mu T)^2 with d_m = D_0/(\mu^2(1+\mu^2)) (_eval_elliptic_middle): the force is projected onto the cone surface, which is sliding friction.

    Elliptic contacts need fewer rows and represent friction exactly, but the coupling costs: the line search must precompute per-contact quadratics, and Newton cannot use its incremental Hessian update (_use_incremental is true only for Newton with a non-elliptic cone). Ch 20 measures the trade. Example 19.6 builds one ball contact for every cone and condim and prints rows, states, forces and the contact-frame force from mjw.contact_force.

    Read the pyramidal condim-3 line: the ball slides along +x, and only row 2 (n + \mu t_2) is active. The contact frame's second tangent is aligned with x here, and contact_force reports f_{t_2} = 0.8 \cdot 13.167 = 10.533: the force sits exactly on a pyramid edge, the pyramidal version of sliding friction. The elliptic line puts every row in CONE (the middle zone), and the friction force 10.65 = 0.8 \cdot 13.313 lies on the circular cone. efc_address always has nmaxpyramid columns, padded with -1 for elliptic.

    Friction loss: a Huber cost

    Joint and tendon frictionloss model dry friction in a joint (stiction in a gearbox). Each dof with frictionloss > 0 gets one FRICTION_DOF row with k = 0 (no spring, only damping). Its cost is quadratic near zero and linear beyond r_f = \text{frictionloss}/D (_eval_constraint, is_friction branch):

    s(x) = \begin{cases} \tfrac12 D x^2 & |x| < r_f \quad(\text{QUADRATIC}) \\ \text{frictionloss}\,(|x| - \tfrac12 r_f) & |x| \ge r_f \quad(\text{LINEARPOS / LINEARNEG}) \end{cases}

    So the force -s'(x) is -Dx until it reaches \pmfrictionloss, then stays there: below the threshold the joint sticks (with a tiny soft creep), above it the joint slides against a constant torque.

    At the first forward the sticking rows carry only 90 % of the torque (-0.09 for 0.1 N m) and the wheel accelerates a little: that is the soft creep of a quadratic row (D is finite). After 0.1 s the creep velocity has built up, the damping-only aref balances it, and the force equals the torque exactly while qvel stays at a few mrad/s (0.013 rad/s at 0.5 N m). Above 0.5 N m the row is LINEARPOS with force exactly -0.5, and the net torque spins the wheel up (qacc = (tau - 0.5)/I, I = 0.04).

    10Limits and the six equality types

    A limit row appears when a limited joint (slide, hinge or ball) or tendon comes within its margin of the range: pos = min(q - lo, hi - q) - margin < 0 (_limit_slide_hinge). It is one-sided like a contact, with jnt_solref/jnt_solimp. A ball joint's limit is one row on the rotation angle (_limit_ball).

    An equality is two-sided: its cost \tfrac12 D x^2 pulls the violation toward zero from either side, and its rows are always QUADRATIC. Each type constrains something different and produces a different number of rows:

    Example 19.8 puts CONNECT, WELD, JOINT and TENDON equalities in one model, declared in the order TENDON, JOINT, WELD, CONNECT (eq ids 0-3), each with a different eq_solref time constant, and starts the joint and tendon equalities violated.

    The rows come out as [3 3 3 2 2 2 2 2 2 1 0]: connect first, then weld, joint, tendon, the launch order of the four kernels, the reverse of the declaration order. Code that maps rows to equalities must use efc.id, not a running count. Under gravity the soft rows hold a small steady violation: the connect (time constant 0.04 s) sags 0.83 mm while its z row carries the capsule's weight (9.81 N in the force array), and the stiffer weld (0.005 s) moves its translation rows by only about 1e-5 m; its largest entry, 4.6e-3, is one of its three rotational rows. Why does the stiffest equality show the largest violation? With no body2 and the default anchor, the weld point is body2's frame origin, here the world origin, 0.7 m horizontally from the box. Holding the box there means also holding its weight as a torque about that point, so the rotational rows carry load (the largest weld entry in the force array is -5.6), and being soft they let the box twist by about 9 mrad (the rotational rows hold the quaternion vector part, so 4.6e-3 is \sin(\theta/2)), and because it swings about the world-origin anchor its centre drifts by about 9 mm in x. Only the horizontal offset produces the gravity torque; the 1 m height does not. The initial violations of the joint (0.2 rad) and tendon (5 cm) equalities decay within a few time constants. The tendon pair is a pure position coupling with no gravity imbalance, so it ends at float32 zero.

    Note: sleep and equalities

    With sleep enabled, forward.py::fwd_position calls sleep.wake_equality right after make_constraint: a sleeping tree tied by an active equality to an awake one is woken, so an equality never pulls on a frozen body (Ch 21).

    11The discrete integrator regularizes differently

    Looking ahead: Ch 23

    DISCRETE solves with an effective metric built from passive stiffness (eff_build/eff_solve). Ch 23 covers the integrator itself; this section covers only how it changes the constraint rows.

    Everything above is for the continuous-time integrators (Euler, implicit, implicitfast, RK4). With integrator="discrete", _efc_row takes its is_discrete branch: refsafe becomes "if h^2 k d > 1, soften k to 1/(h^2 d) and limit b", then with \phi = 1 + h b + h^2 k d it sets R = \max(R_0/\phi, R_{min}) and a_{ref} = (-k d\,\mathrm{pos} - (b + h k d)\,v)/\phi: the spring-damper is integrated implicitly over the step. After actuation, forward.py::fwd_actuation also calls constraint.regularize_constraint, which rescales each row's D by how much the effective metric qH differs from M along the row's Jacobian. The cost of §3 is then built with the effective metric instead of M. Ch 23 owns the details.

    12Check yourself

    Quiz

    What makes MuJoCo's contacts "soft"?

    The row cost \tfrac12 D (Ja - a_{ref})^2 with finite D (from the impedance) trades violation against the Gauss term. As d \to 1 it would become hard.

    A condim-4 contact in a pyramidal model. How many efc rows does it get?

    Pyramidal: 2*(condim-1) = 6 (two sliding pairs and one torsion pair). Elliptic would give 4.

    How many rows do CONNECT, WELD and JOINT equalities produce?

    A point (3), a frame (6), one scalar relation (1). TENDON is also 1; FLEX is one per edge.

    Why can the order of contact row blocks differ between two GPU runs of the same step?

    Counter atomics hand out blocks in whatever order threads arrive. Block contents are identical; always go through contact.efc_address. Ch 22 covers reproducibility.

    A contact row has efc.pos = -0.002 (penetrating) but efc.state = SATISFIED. How?

    Activity is decided by J\ddot q - a_{ref} at the optimum, not by pos. ex2's limit row and ex3's fast-sliding pyramid edges show this.

    You want to randomize impratio per world on an mjw.Model. Which field?

    io.py stores impratio_invsqrt = 1/sqrt(max(impratio, mjMINVAL)), batched per world.

    Newton's method on f(x) = \tfrac12 x^T A x - b^T x with SPD A, from any start, takes how many steps?

    The Hessian is A, so x_1 = x_0 - A^{-1}(Ax_0 - b) = A^{-1} b. On a piecewise quadratic it is exact inside one piece, which is why the line search is cheap.

    The model has nv = 36 and jacobian="auto". What is d.efc.J.shape with nworld 2?

    is_sparse: auto and nv > 32. Sparse J is a value ribbon per world, indexed by J_rowadr, J_rownnz, J_colind.

    You set solref="0.0005 1" with timestep 0.002 and refsafe on. Which time constant is used?

    timeconst = max(timeconst, 2*timestep) in _contact_kbimp, unless DisableBit.REFSAFE or the discrete integrator.

    14Summary

    Key takeaways

    • MJWarp solves, per world, \min_a \tfrac12 (a-a_s)^T M(a-a_s) + \sum_i s_i(J_i a - a_{ref,i}): a convex problem with one minimizer, not an LCP. Forces are -s_i', and the optimum is exactly M\ddot q = \tau_{smooth} + J^T f.
    • Rows are grouped equality | friction | limit | contact per world; inside a category each kernel thread reserves rows with wp.atomic_add, so contact blocks can come out in any order; use contact.efc_address and efc.id.
    • Overflow does not raise: nefc keeps counting past njmax, rows that do not fit are dropped, and step sets OverflowType.NEFC and prints a warning (unless warn_overflow masks it).
    • _contact_kbimp: k = 1/(d_{max}^2\tau^2\zeta^2), b = 2/(d_{max}\tau), refsafe \tau \ge 2h, impedance sigmoid from solimp. _efc_row: D = d/((1-d)w), a_{ref} = -bv - kd\,\mathrm{pos}.
    • Pyramidal: 2(condim-1) independent rows n \pm \mu t; elliptic: condim coupled rows with SATISFIED / CONE / QUADRATIC zones. impratio is stored as impratio_invsqrt.
    • Equalities: CONNECT 3, WELD 6, JOINT 1, TENDON 1, FLEX 1 per edge, FLEXSTRAIN a block per cell; rows come out per kernel type; toggle with d.eq_active.
    • J is dense (nworld, njmax_pad, nv_pad) for nv ≤ 32 under auto and sparse CSR (nworld, 1, njmax_nnz) above; forced dense is refused above nv 60.
    • The toolkit for Ch 20: Newton is exact in one step on a quadratic, CG in n, GD zigzags; on a piecewise quadratic, an exact line search is a few cheap Newton steps from piece to piece.

    Next: Ch 20 opens solver.solve and shows how CG and Newton use these pieces, world by world, on the GPU.
