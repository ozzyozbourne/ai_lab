Chapter 5

Statistics and Rigor in Evals

In 2025 a group at Tübingen and Cambridge re-ran a batch of celebrated “RL makes small models reason” results under one controlled protocol. Changing nothing but the random seed moved AIME’24 Pass@1 by 5 to 15 points; running the same model on a different GPU cluster moved it by up to 8 points; and many of the reported gains shrank or vanished under the rigorous protocol[1]. Even “deterministic” settings are not: with temperature 0 and a fixed seed, five LLMs varied by up to 15% in accuracy across ten identical runs[2]. A benchmark score is a measurement with noise in it, and a leaderboard gap of two points is often smaller than that noise. This chapter teaches you to put a correct error bar on every number, compare two models properly, decide how big an eval must be before you run it, and report results so that a sceptical reader can check them.

5.1Why an eval score is an estimate

Suppose your model gets 124 of the 200 questions in an internal reasoning benchmark right. Nobody cares about those particular 200 questions: a different 200 testing the same skill would have given a different score. What we want is how the model does on the whole space of questions the benchmark represents. Evan Miller’s paper “Adding Error Bars to Evals” (and Anthropic’s companion blog post) makes this the starting point: treat the questions in a benchmark as a random sample from an unseen super-population of questions, and treat the benchmark score as an estimate of the super-population mean[3][4].

The rest of the chapter asks: if I repeated this measurement, how much would the number move?

Setup noise (prompt template, few-shot examples, answer parser) is partly systematic: a new template can move every score in one direction. Single-prompt results are brittle[5], so fix the prompt as part of the eval’s definition and report it, or sample prompts as an extra random factor (PromptEval, 5.12).

The law of total variance

The tool that organises these sources is the law of total variance. Let s be the score of a single attempt at a randomly drawn question q. Then

Var(s) = Eq[ Var(s | q) ] + Varq( E[s | q] ) (5.1) The first term is the average within-question variance (prediction noise: how much the score of one question changes between attempts). The second is the between-question variance (data noise: how much questions differ in their expected score).

For a binary score, write pi = E[s | qi] for the model’s true probability of solving question i. The within term is E[pi(1 − pi)] and the between term is Var(pi). Their sum is μ(1 − μ). The terms shrink with different levers: K answers per question divide the within term by K; only more questions shrink the between term. Sida Wang found that for paired model comparisons prediction noise usually exceeds data noise, making resampling a cheap source of power[6].

A confidence interval captures the noise from sampling: which questions, which model samples. It says nothing about whether the benchmark measures the right construct, whether its labels are wrong, or whether the model saw the test during training. The super-population is an idealisation. Label errors and contamination (Chapter 3) bias the estimate, and no statistics here remove that bias.

5.2Error bars for one model

Start with the simplest case: one model, n questions drawn independently, one attempt per question, score si per question. The eval score is the sample mean s̄.

The CLT standard error

By the central limit theorem, for large n the sample mean is approximately normal around the true mean μ, with standard deviation equal to the standard error (SE):

SECLT = √( 1n − 1 Σi (si − s̄)² )√n,    and for binary scores   SE = √( s̄(1 − s̄) / n ) (5.2) The 95% interval is s̄ ± 1.96 · SE. These are the formulas in Miller (2024), Section 2[3]; Inspect’s stderr() computes exactly np.std(values, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)[7].

Worked example. 124 of 200 correct: s̄ = 0.62, SE = √(0.62 × 0.38 / 200) = 0.0343, and the 95% CI is 0.62 ± 1.96 × 0.0343 = [0.553, 0.687]. That is ±6.7 points. A second model at 66% on the same benchmark is well inside that band, so on these numbers alone you cannot call it better (5.5 shows how pairing changes this).

AIME-sized benchmark. 12 of 30 correct: SE = 0.0894, ±17.5 points; one question is 3.3 points[1]. Both reproduced by code/ch05/error_bars.py.

The SE is largest at 50% accuracy and scales as 1/√n: halving the error bar takes four times the questions. At n = 1,000 and 50% the 95% half-width is still ±3.1 points.

When the CLT fails

With small n, or accuracy near 0 or 1, the normal approximation is poor: the “Wald” interval s̄ ± 1.96 SE can extend outside [0, 1], collapse to zero width when every answer is right or wrong, and cover the true value less often than the advertised 95%. Bowyer, Aitchison and Ivanova argue that CLT intervals should not be used for LLM evals with fewer than a few hundred data points; in their simulations nominal 95% CLT intervals reach only about 92.5% coverage even at N = 100[8]. AIME has 30 problems, many agentic suites a few dozen tasks, and slices of larger benchmarks are often tiny.

Three alternatives are standard:

Wilson:   centre = p̂ + z²/2n1 + z²/n,   half-width = z √( p̂(1 − p̂)/n + z²/4n² )1 + z²/n (5.3) Wilson inverts the score test: it is the set of μ for which the observed p̂ is not surprising. It is asymmetric, never leaves [0, 1], and has close to nominal coverage on average.
  • Clopper–Pearson (“exact”) inverts two one-sided binomial tests. The lower bound for k successes is the 2.5% quantile of Beta(k, n − k + 1) and the upper bound the 97.5% quantile of Beta(k + 1, n − k). It guarantees at least 95% coverage for every μ, so it is conservative (wider than necessary).
  • Beta posterior. With a uniform prior on μ, the posterior after k of n is Beta(1 + k, 1 + n − k); report its 2.5% and 97.5% quantiles as a 95% credible interval. Bowyer et al. recommend Wilson or this Beta–Bernoulli interval for a single model with IID questions[8].

None of these needs SciPy. For integer parameters the Beta CDF equals a binomial tail, P(Beta(a, b) ≤ x) = P(Binomial(a + b − 1, x) ≥ a), so a quantile is a bisection on a sum of binomial probabilities. The chapter’s shared helper file does exactly that:

def binom_cdf(k: int, n: int, p: float) -> float:
    """P(X <= k) for X ~ Binomial(n, p), summed in log space for stability."""
    if k < 0:
        return 0.0
    if k >= n:
        return 1.0
    if p <= 0.0:
        return 1.0
    if p >= 1.0:
        return 0.0
    lp, lq = math.log(p), math.log1p(-p)
    total = 0.0
    for i in range(k + 1):
        total += math.exp(math.lgamma(n + 1) - math.lgamma(i + 1) - math.lgamma(n - i + 1) + i * lp + (n - i) * lq)
    return min(1.0, total)


def beta_cdf_int(x: float, a: int, b: int) -> float:
    """CDF of Beta(a, b) for integer a, b >= 1, via the binomial identity
    P(Beta(a, b) <= x) = P(Binomial(a + b - 1, x) >= a)."""
    return 1.0 - binom_cdf(a - 1, a + b - 1, x)


def beta_ppf_int(q: float, a: int, b: int) -> float:
    """Quantile of Beta(a, b) for integer parameters, by bisection."""
    return _bisect(lambda x: beta_cdf_int(x, a, b) - q, 0.0, 1.0)


def wilson(k: int, n: int, z: float = 1.959964) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Wilson score interval."""
    p = k / n
    denom = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / denom
    return max(0.0, centre - half), min(1.0, centre + half)


def clopper_pearson(k: int, n: int, alpha: float = 0.05) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Exact (Clopper-Pearson) interval: invert the two one-sided binomial tests."""
    lo = 0.0 if k == 0 else beta_ppf_int(alpha / 2, k, n - k + 1)
    hi = 1.0 if k == n else beta_ppf_int(1 - alpha / 2, k + 1, n - k)
    return lo, hi


def beta_posterior(k: int, n: int, alpha: float = 0.05) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Equal-tailed credible interval of the Beta(1 + k, 1 + n - k) posterior (uniform prior)."""
    return beta_ppf_int(alpha / 2, 1 + k, 1 + n - k), beta_ppf_int(1 - alpha / 2, 1 + k, 1 + n - k)

Measuring coverage exactly

“Coverage” is a property you can compute, not a slogan. For a true accuracy μ and sample size n, the number correct is Binomial(n, μ), so the probability that an interval method covers μ is Σk P(k) · 1[μ ∈ CI(k)]: a finite sum, no simulation needed. error_bars.py evaluates it for all four methods, at two fixed accuracies and averaged over μ uniform on [0.05, 0.95], then checks one cell by Monte Carlo:

def exact_coverage(n: int, ps: np.ndarray) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Average over ps of P(interval contains p), summing exactly over k ~ Binomial(n, p)."""
    ints = all_intervals(n)
    k = np.arange(n + 1)
    lc = np.array([math.lgamma(n + 1) - math.lgamma(t + 1) - math.lgamma(n - t + 1) for t in k])
    res = {}
    for name, (lo, hi) in ints.items():
        covs = []
        for p in ps:
            pmf = np.exp(lc + k * math.log(p) + (n - k) * math.log1p(-p))
            covs.append(float(pmf[(lo <= p) & (p <= hi)].sum()))
        res[name] = float(np.mean(covs))
    return res

5.3Clustered data

The formulas above assume independent scores. Reading-comprehension benchmarks ask several questions per passage; SWE-bench draws many tasks from a few repositories; agentic suites generate several tasks per environment; K answers to one question form a cluster. Scores within a cluster are positively correlated, so the naive SE is too small. Miller makes ignoring clustering one of his central warnings, and Anthropic lists clustered SEs as the second of its five recommendations[3][4].

The clustered standard error

With clusters c = 1..C and scores si,c, the clustered SE adds the within-cluster cross-products to the naive variance:

SEclustered = ( SECLT² + 1n² Σc Σi Σj≠i (si,c − s̄)(sj,c − s̄) )1/2 ≈ 1n √( Σc ( Σi∈c (si,c − s̄) )² ) (5.4) The second form sums the deviations inside each cluster first, then squares. Positive correlation inside clusters makes the squared sums large, so the SE grows. Inspect multiplies the variance by C/(C − 1), a finite-cluster correction that matters when there are few clusters.

The survey statistician’s design effect is a useful mental model: with clusters of size m and intra-cluster correlation ICC, the variance grows by 1 + (m − 1)·ICC (ten questions per passage at ICC 0.2: ×2.8). Measure it per eval; there is no universal multiplier.

Here is Inspect’s implementation, quoted from the current source. It cites Appendix A of Miller’s paper:

def _clustered_stderr(partition: list[list[float]]) -> float:
    """Clustered standard error of the mean over a cluster partition.

    For details, see Appendix A of https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.00640.
    The version here uses a finite cluster correction (unlike the paper)
    """
    import numpy as np

    cluster_count = len(partition)

    # The finite-cluster correction divides by (cluster_count - 1), so
    # mirror the non-clustered path's n < 2 guard and return 0 rather
    # than NaN/inf when there is only a single cluster.
    if cluster_count < 2:
        return 0.0

    cluster_arrays = [np.asarray(group, dtype=float) for group in partition]
    values = np.concatenate(cluster_arrays)
    mean = float(np.mean(values))

    # sum_i sum_j (s_i - mean)(s_j - mean) over a cluster is the square
    # of that cluster's deviation sum. Computing the identity directly
    # avoids materialising a k-by-k outer product for every cluster.
    clustered_variance = 0.0
    for cluster_data in cluster_arrays:
        clustered_variance += ((cluster_data - mean).sum()) ** 2

    # Multiply by C / (C - 1) to unbias the variance estimate
    standard_error = np.sqrt(
        clustered_variance * cluster_count / (cluster_count - 1)
    ) / len(values)

    return cast(float, standard_error)

A simulation: passages with shared difficulty

clustered.py builds a benchmark of 50 passages with 4 questions each. Each passage’s difficulty is drawn from {0.2, 0.5, 0.9}, and all four of its questions are answered correctly with that probability. It computes the naive SE, Miller’s clustered SE, Inspect’s version, and a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole passages, then repeats the experiment 2,000 times to measure how often each 95% interval covers the true super-population mean (0.5333).

def naive_se(s: np.ndarray) -> float:
    return float(np.std(s, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(s)))


def clustered_se(s: np.ndarray, cid: np.ndarray, finite_correction: bool) -> float:
    """(1/n) * sqrt( sum_c (sum_{i in c} (s_i - mean))^2 ), optionally times sqrt(C/(C-1))."""
    mean = s.mean()
    clusters = np.unique(cid)
    v = sum(((s[cid == c] - mean).sum()) ** 2 for c in clusters)
    if finite_correction:
        v *= len(clusters) / (len(clusters) - 1)
    return float(np.sqrt(v) / len(s))


def cluster_bootstrap_se(s: np.ndarray, cid: np.ndarray, rng: np.random.Generator, b: int = 2000) -> float:
    """Resample whole passages with replacement and recompute the mean."""
    sums = np.bincount(cid, weights=s)
    sizes = np.bincount(cid)
    idx = rng.integers(0, len(sums), size=(b, len(sums)))
    means = sums[idx].sum(axis=1) / sizes[idx].sum(axis=1)
    return float(means.std(ddof=1))

5.4Reducing variance without changing the question

Once you know which term of Equation 5.1 dominates, you can shrink it. Miller’s third recommendation is to reduce the within-question variance, often the cheap term[3][4].

Resampling K answers per question

If every question is answered K times and you score each question by the mean of its K attempts, the within-question variance of that question falls from pi(1 − pi) to pi(1 − pi)/K. The between-question term is untouched. So the variance of the benchmark mean is

Var(s̄) = 1n ( Var(p) + E[p(1 − p)]K ) (5.5) As K → ∞ the SE approaches a floor of √(Var(p)/n) set entirely by the number of questions.

The right SE is the ordinary SE over the n per-question means (equivalently, a clustered SE with questions as clusters). Pooling all K·n attempts into one list treats repeat attempts as new questions.

resample_k.py draws solve rates from a U-shaped Beta(0.6, 0.4), so both variance terms are 0.12, and builds 4,000 fresh 200-question benchmarks per K:

def theory() -> tuple[float, float]:
    mean = A / (A + B)
    var_between = A * B / ((A + B) ** 2 * (A + B + 1))
    within = mean * (1 - mean) - var_between  # E[p(1-p)] = mu(1-mu) - Var(p)
    return var_between, within

Token probabilities and continuous metrics

If the answer is a single token (a multiple-choice letter, a yes/no), you can skip sampling entirely and score each question by the model’s probability of the correct token, pi. This removes the within-question term exactly: Var(s̄) = Var(p)/n[3], the floor of 0.0245 in the script’s last row, from one forward pass. More generally, continuous metrics such as reference log-probability or bits-per-byte carry more signal per item than 0/1 accuracy, especially early in training[9][10]; 5.11 quantifies this.

Other levers, and one to avoid

  • Checkpoint averaging. When the eval tracks a training run, averaging the scores of the last few checkpoints reduces training noise. Heineman et al. report it raises decision accuracy on their benchmarks[10].
  • Better questions, not more. Removing near-duplicate or broken items and choosing informative ones (IRT, 5.12) shrinks the between term per item.
  • Do not lower the temperature to reduce noise. Miller argues that changing sampling parameters changes the quantity being measured and can move variance into the conditional means rather than remove it[3]. Hochlehnert et al. found temperature 0 vs 1 moved AIME scores by up to about 15 points and top-p by up to about 8[1]. Use each model’s intended decoding parameters, report them, and resample instead.

In Inspect, Task(..., epochs=Epochs(5, "mean")) runs each sample five times and reduces per sample before computing metrics: the per-question-mean design[7].

5.5Comparing two models

Most evals ask whether a new model, prompt or checkpoint beats the old one. The efficient answer uses the fact that both answered the same questions.

Paired differences and the correlation term

For each question compute di = sA,i − sB,i and treat the di as your data:

SEpaired = sd(d)√n = √( SEA² + SEB² − 2 · SEA · SEB · Corr(sA, sB) ) (5.6) The unpaired SE of the difference is √(SEA² + SEB²), which is Equation 5.6 with the correlation set to zero.

Models agree about which questions are hard, so the correlation term usually removes a large share of the variance[3]; measure and report it. At ρ = 0.5 the paired SE is 71% of the unpaired one; at 0.8, 45% (Figure 5.3).

Three tests for the same question

  • McNemar’s test for binary outcomes. Only the discordant questions carry information about the difference: let b be the number A gets right and B wrong, and c the reverse. Under “no difference”, b ~ Binomial(b + c, 0.5). Use that exact binomial test (or χ² = (|b − c| − 1)²/(b + c) for large counts).
  • Paired permutation (sign-flip) test. Under the null, the sign of each di is arbitrary. Flip signs at random many times, recompute the mean, and report the share of flips at least as extreme as the observed mean. It works for any score, not just binary.
  • Paired bootstrap. Resample question indices with replacement, recompute the mean difference with the same indices for both models, and take the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles. This is the classic MT significance test of Koehn (2004), studied further by Berg-Kirkpatrick et al.[11][12] It also works for non-additive metrics such as corpus BLEU or F1, where you recompute the metric on each resample.

Dror et al. give a decision tree for choosing a test[13]; for a mean metric on a few hundred questions, the paired z-test, permutation test and paired bootstrap agree.

5.6Power analysis and sample size

Before running an eval, ask whether it can detect the effect you care about. An underpowered eval’s “no significant difference” is read as “no difference”, and its occasional significant result overstates the effect (the winner’s curse). Card et al. found many NLP comparisons underpowered; a typical 2,000-sentence MT test set has only about 75% power to detect a 1-BLEU difference[14].

Miller’s sample-size formula

For a two-sided test at level α with power 1 − β to detect a true difference δ, Miller gives[3]:

n = (zα/2 + zβ)² · ω² + σA²/KA + σB²/KBδ²,     δMDE = (zα/2 + zβ) · √( (ω² + σA²/KA + σB²/KB) / n ) (5.7) ω² = Var(xA) + Var(xB) − 2 Cov(xA, xB) is the between-question variance of the paired difference of the models’ question-level means x; σ² is each model’s within-question variance and K its number of samples per question. For α = 0.05 and 80% power, zα/2 = 1.960 and zβ = 0.8416. The second expression is the minimum detectable effect (MDE) for a given n.

Worked example. Detect a 3-point gain (δ = 0.03) at accuracy near 50%, so each model’s score variance is 0.25, with one greedy sample per question (σ² = 0):

  • Uncorrelated models: ω² = 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50, n = 2.8016² × 0.50 / 0.0009 = 4,361 questions.
  • ρ = 0.5: ω² = 0.25, n = 2,181.
  • ρ = 0.8: ω² = 0.10, n = 873.

Miller’s own worked example assumes ω² = 1/9 (from his uniform-difficulty setup; at 50% accuracy this corresponds to ρ ≈ 0.78) and σ² = 0, giving 2.8016² × (1/9) / 0.03² ≈ 969 questions, hence his rule of thumb that new evals should contain at least 1,000 questions[3]. def n_required(delta: float, omega2: float, sa2: float = 0.0, sb2: float = 0.0, ka: int = 1, kb: int = 1) -> int:
    """Miller: n = (z_{a/2} + z_b)^2 (omega^2 + sA^2/KA + sB^2/KB) / delta^2, rounded up."""
    return math.ceil((Z_A + Z_B) ** 2 * (omega2 + sa2 / ka + sb2 / kb) / delta**2)


def mde(n: int, omega2: float, sa2: float = 0.0, sb2: float = 0.0, ka: int = 1, kb: int = 1) -> float:
    return (Z_A + Z_B) * math.sqrt((omega2 + sa2 / ka + sb2 / kb) / n)


def correlated_pairs(rng: np.random.Generator, n: int, pa: float, pb: float, rho: float) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Binary score pairs with given means and Pearson correlation (via the 2x2 joint table)."""
    cov = rho * math.sqrt(pa * (1 - pa) * pb * (1 - pb))
    p11 = pa * pb + cov
    p10, p01 = pa - p11, pb - p11
    u = rng.random(n)
    a = (u < p11 + p10).astype(float)
    b = ((u < p11) | ((u >= p11 + p10) & (u < p11 + p10 + p01))).astype(float)
    return a, b

5.7Multiple comparisons and leaderboards

Run one test at α = 0.05 and you accept a 5% chance of a false positive. Run 20 — one per benchmark in a model card, or one per subtask — with no true difference anywhere, and the chance of at least one “significant” result is 1 − 0.9520 = 0.64. Two families of correction exist:

  • Family-wise error rate (FWER): control P(any false positive). Bonferroni tests each of m hypotheses at α/m (0.0025 for 20). Holm’s step-down version sorts the p-values and compares the i-th smallest with α/(m − i + 1), stopping at the first failure; it controls the same error rate and is uniformly more powerful.
  • False discovery rate (FDR): control the expected share of false positives among the rejections. Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) finds the largest i with p(i) ≤ i·q/m and rejects the i smallest. It suits screening many model pairs or subtasks, where a few false leads are acceptable.
def holm(p: np.ndarray, alpha: float = ALPHA) -> np.ndarray:
    """Step-down: compare the i-th smallest p (i = 0..m-1) with alpha / (m - i); stop at first failure."""
    m = len(p)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    reject = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool)
    for i, j in enumerate(order):
        if p[j] <= alpha / (m - i):
            reject[j] = True
        else:
            break
    return reject


def benjamini_hochberg(p: np.ndarray, q: float = ALPHA) -> np.ndarray:
    """Step-up: find the largest i with p_(i) <= i q / m and reject p_(1..i)."""
    m = len(p)
    order = np.argsort(p)
    thresh = q * np.arange(1, m + 1) / m
    below = np.nonzero(p[order] <= thresh)[0]
    reject = np.zeros(m, dtype=bool)
    if len(below):
        reject[order[: below[-1] + 1]] = True
    return reject

Simultaneous intervals for rankings

“Model 3 is ranked third” is a statement about many pairwise differences at once. Chatbot Arena builds simultaneous intervals for its Bradley–Terry scores from a sandwich-robust covariance and gives each model a rank range: 1 plus the number of models whose lower bound exceeds its upper bound[16]. In Scenario 3 (Bonferroni over 28 pairs), the second model’s range widens from 1–3 to 1–5.

The worst multiple-comparisons problems are the ones nobody counts. Dehghani et al. show that which benchmarks and aggregation a paper chooses can decide which method looks state of the art[17]. Trying ten prompts, five checkpoints or three seeds and reporting the best on the test set is a hidden multiple comparison with a built-in upward bias; Hochlehnert et al. found this kind of selection among the reasons published reasoning gains shrank under re-evaluation[1]. The fixes are procedural: pre-register the primary metric, select on a dev split, and report every configuration you tried.

The same bias shows up on public leaderboards: Singh et al. found that Chatbot Arena providers privately tested many variants and published only the best[56] (see Chapter 2’s Leaderboard Illusion aside). If N variants have equal true skill and scores with standard error σ, the published maximum is inflated by about σ × E[max of N standard normals]: about 1.16 SE for N = 5 and 1.54 SE for N = 10.

Adding seeds, epochs or questions until p < 0.05, or re-checking a running eval after every batch, is a multiple comparison over time. (2) If you must monitor, use a method that is valid under monitoring: Bonferroni over a fixed number of looks (α/L, conservative, as the last line shows), or an anytime-valid confidence sequence, which holds simultaneously at every sample size[18][19]. Hsu and Shekhar build confidence sequences for a new LLM’s accuracy on a fixed question set and choose which questions to query next so the sequence shrinks quickly[20]. Chapter 9 covers the same trap for online A/B tests (Section 9.15).

5.8The bootstrap toolbox

Many eval statistics are not means: medians, F1, corpus BLEU, Bradley–Terry ratings, the IQM of 5.13. The bootstrap handles them all: resample the n items with replacement B times, recompute the statistic on each resample, and use the spread of the B replicates.

def intervals(x: np.ndarray, stat, rng: np.random.Generator, b: int = 10_000, alpha: float = 0.05) -> dict[str, tuple[float, float]]:
    theta = stat(x)
    t = boot_stats(x, stat, rng, b)
    lo_q, hi_q = np.quantile(t, [alpha / 2, 1 - alpha / 2])
    out = {"percentile": (lo_q, hi_q), "basic": (2 * theta - hi_q, 2 * theta - lo_q)}
    # BCa: bias correction z0 from the share of replicates below theta, acceleration from the jackknife
    z0 = norm_ppf(min(max((t < theta).mean(), 1e-6), 1 - 1e-6))
    jack = np.array([stat(np.delete(x, i)) for i in range(len(x))])
    dj = jack.mean() - jack
    acc = (dj**3).sum() / (6 * ((dj**2).sum()) ** 1.5 + 1e-300)
    qs = []
    for z in (norm_ppf(alpha / 2), norm_ppf(1 - alpha / 2)):
        qs.append(norm_cdf(z0 + (z0 + z) / (1 - acc * (z0 + z))))
    out["BCa"] = tuple(np.quantile(t, qs))
    return out

5.9Stochastic agents: pass@k, pass^k and Bayes@N

Two questions about a stochastic agent have different answers: can it solve the task given several tries, and will it solve it every time? Chapter 2 derived the first metric in full (Section 2.5) and Chapter 6 applies both to agent benchmarks (Section 6.6). This section is about their statistics.

Two unbiased estimators

Run each task n times and count c successes. With per-task success probability p:

pass@k = Etask[ 1 − (1 − p)k ],   estimated per task by 1 − C(n − c, k)C(n, k) (5.8)
pass^k = Etask[ pk ],   estimated per task by C(c, k)C(n, k) (5.9) pass@k (Chen et al. 2021) is the chance that at least one of k attempts succeeds; pass^k (Yao et al. 2024, τ-bench) is the chance that all k succeed[25][26]. Both estimators are unbiased because they count, among all size-k subsets of the n trials, the share with at least one success or with only successes. Both are averaged over tasks.

The reference implementations are short. HumanEval uses a numerically stable product instead of large binomial coefficients, and τ-bench computes pass^k with math.comb:

    def estimator(n: int, c: int, k: int) -> float:
        """
        Calculates 1 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k).
        """
        if n - c < k:
            return 1.0
        return 1.0 - np.prod(1.0 - k / np.arange(n - c + 1, n + 1))
    pass_hat_ks: dict[int, float] = {}
    for k in range(1, num_trials + 1):
        sum_task_pass_hat_k = 0
        for c in c_per_task_id.values():
            sum_task_pass_hat_k += comb(c, k) / comb(num_trials, k)
        pass_hat_ks[k] = sum_task_pass_hat_k / len(c_per_task_id)

Both quoted verbatim from the current repositories[27][28]. In τ-bench, gpt-4o’s retail pass^1 is about 61% but its pass^8 is below 25%[26].

Why pass^k is not (pass@1)k

pass^k averages pik, so it depends on the whole distribution of per-task success rates. By Jensen’s inequality mean(pik) ≥ (mean p)k, with equality only when all tasks are equally hard, so equal pass@1 can hide very different reliability. Take ten tasks: Agent A succeeds 60% of the time on every task; Agent B always solves six tasks and never solves the other four. Both have pass@1 = 0.6.

def pass_at_k(n: int, c: int, k: int) -> float:
    """Unbiased pass@k (Chen et al. 2021), numerically stable product form from openai/human-eval."""
    if n - c < k:
        return 1.0
    return float(1.0 - np.prod(1.0 - k / np.arange(n - c + 1, n + 1)))


def pass_hat_k(n: int, c: int, k: int) -> float:
    """Unbiased pass^k (Yao et al. 2024): probability that k draws without replacement are all successes."""
    return comb(c, k) / comb(n, k)

Bayes@N: a posterior instead of a point estimate

Hariri et al. replace pass@k at small N with a posterior over each task’s success probability (Dirichlet, or Beta for binary outcomes), reported as a posterior mean with a credible interval. Under a uniform prior it ranks models like avg@N, and on AIME and HMMT it gives more stable rankings than pass@k; code in mohsenhariri/scorio[29]. passk.py does the binary version: per-task Beta(c + 1, n − c + 1) posteriors, sampled jointly, give [0.489, 0.670] for Agent A’s suite mean.

For a user-facing agent: pass^k at a k matching realistic retries (often k = 1 and k = 4 or 8), with its interval and n per task. For a model used inside a search or verifier loop: pass@k. For tracking a training run: avg@N (equivalently the Bayes@N posterior mean), because it is the lowest-variance of the three.

5.10Nondeterminism and reproducibility

With questions, model and decoding fixed, numbers still move: by design (sampling) and by accident (the serving stack).

Where the noise comes from

  • Sampling seeds. At temperature > 0 each run is a different draw. Hochlehnert et al. measured standard deviations of 5–15 points in AIME’24 Pass@1 across seeds, and found the variance of the mean stabilises around K ≈ 30 seeds on AIME-sized sets[1].
  • “Deterministic” decoding is not deterministic. Atil et al. ran five LLMs at temperature 0 with fixed seeds, ten runs each over eight tasks, and saw accuracy vary by up to 15% across runs, with a gap of up to 70% between best-possible accuracy (a question counts as right if any of the 10 runs got it right) and worst-possible accuracy (right only if every run did)[2]; seeds_nondeterminism.py prints the same pair for its mock (“best-possible (right in any run) 0.516 vs worst-possible (right in all runs) 0.494”). They define two agreement metrics: TARr@N, the share of questions where all N raw output strings are identical, and TARa@N, the share where all N parsed answers agree. Their code is published as llm-stability[30].
  • Batch invariance. Thinking Machines Lab argue that the main cause of temperature-0 nondeterminism in served LLMs is that kernels are not batch invariant: a request’s numerics depend on how many other requests share its batch, which depends on server load. They released batch-invariant kernels (batch_invariant_ops) that give bitwise reproducible vLLM inference at some speed cost[31]. Their demonstration: 1000 temperature-0 completions from Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 gave 80 unique outputs, identical for the first 102 tokens and diverging at token 103; with batch-invariant kernels all 1000 were identical. As of November 2026 you can switch this on in the engine instead of installing the separate repo: vLLM has a built-in batch-invariant mode (export VLLM_BATCH_INVARIANT=1; beta, and it may cost throughput)[54], and SGLang has a --enable-deterministic-inference flag built on batch-invariant operations[55].
  • Hardware and software drift. In the Sober Look study, running the same model on five compute clusters moved AIME’24 Pass@1 by up to 8 points for OpenRS-1.5B and 6 for DeepSeek-R1-Distill-7B. Even with torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True), fixed CUDA seeds and cuDNN benchmarking disabled, OpenThinker2-7B scored 53.0 ± 4.6 on A100 versus 57.1 ± 5.2 on H100. Switching evaluation framework usually moved scores by 1–2 points but moved S1.1-7B by 4.5 (22.2 with lighteval vs 17.7 with evalchemy)[1]. If the baseline ran last month on a different stack, part of your “improvement” is the stack.
  • Sandbox resources (agent evals). Anthropic found that on Terminal-Bench 2.0, moving from strict per-task CPU/RAM limits to uncapped resources raised scores by 6 points (p < 0.01), similar to the gaps between top models. Under strict limits, 5.8% of tasks failed from infrastructure or pod errors; a 3× headroom ceiling cut that to about 2.1% without a significant score change (p = 0.40)[53]. Count infra errors as their own outcome, report the infra error rate, and state whether such tasks were retried or excluded. Chapter 9 makes the same point about recording container limits (Section 9.5).

def greedy_run(logits: np.ndarray, batch_size: int, batch_invariant: bool) -> list[str]:
    """Argmax decode. Non-invariant kernels add a perturbation that depends on the batch size."""
    if batch_invariant:
        pert = np.zeros_like(logits)
    else:
        pert = np.random.default_rng(batch_size).normal(0, 0.02, size=logits.shape)
    choice = np.argmax(logits + pert, axis=1)
    # raw text also varies in formatting on near ties (e.g. trailing whitespace from a different token path)
    margin = np.sort(logits + pert, axis=1)[:, -1] - np.sort(logits + pert, axis=1)[:, -2]
    fmt_rng = np.random.default_rng(0 if batch_invariant else batch_size + 1000)
    tail = np.where((margin < 0.15) & (fmt_rng.random(len(choice)) < 0.5), " ", "")
    return [f"Answer: {'ABCD'[c]}{t}" for c, t in zip(choice, tail)]


def tar(outputs: list[list[str]]) -> float:
    """Total agreement rate: fraction of questions where all N runs give the identical value."""
    return float(np.mean([len(set(col)) == 1 for col in zip(*outputs)]))

5.11Benchmark quality: signal, noise and decision accuracy

How often does a benchmark that picks between two small-scale recipes pick the one that wins at scale? Heineman et al. at AI2 frame this with two quantities[10]:

signal = maxj,k |mj − mk|m̄,    noise = sd(final checkpoints of one model)mean,    SNR = signal / noise (5.10) Signal is the relative spread of scores across models trained with similar compute; noise is the relative standard deviation of a single model’s last few checkpoints. Decision accuracy is the share of model pairs whose small-scale ranking matches their large-scale ranking.

Across 465 models and 30 benchmarks they find that SNR correlates with decision accuracy (R = 0.791) while signal or noise alone barely does, and three interventions raise SNR: filtering out low-SNR subtasks (MMLU decision accuracy +2.6%), averaging checkpoints (+2.4%), and using bits-per-byte instead of accuracy (decision accuracy improved on 90% of benchmarks)[10]. Madaan et al. add seed standard deviation and monotonicity (Kendall correlation of score with training step); MMLU in cloze format has monotonicity 0.95 against 0.09 for standard MMLU early in training[9].

def evaluate(n_items: int, metric: str, seed: int, reps: int = 200) -> tuple[float, float, float, float]:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    true_skill = np.linspace(-0.3, 0.3, N_MODELS)          # large-scale ranking = this order
    b = rng.normal(0.0, 1.5, size=n_items)                 # a fixed benchmark
    sig, noi, da = [], [], []
    for _ in range(reps):
        # final checkpoints jitter around the model's skill (training noise)
        ck = true_skill[:, None] + rng.normal(0, 0.04, size=(N_MODELS, N_CKPT))
        sc = np.stack([scores(rng, ck[:, t], b, metric) for t in range(N_CKPT)], axis=1)
        m = sc.mean(axis=1)
        sig.append((m.max() - m.min()) / m.mean())
        noi.append(np.mean(sc.std(axis=1, ddof=1) / sc.mean(axis=1)))
        last = sc[:, -1]
        agree = [np.sign(last[i] - last[j]) == np.sign(true_skill[i] - true_skill[j])
                 for i, j in itertools.combinations(range(N_MODELS), 2)]
        da.append(np.mean(agree))
    s, n_ = float(np.mean(sig)), float(np.mean(noi))
    return s, n_, s / n_, float(np.mean(da))

5.12Psychometrics: IRT and efficient evaluation

Questions are not interchangeable: some are trivial, some mislabelled, some sharply separate strong from weak models. Item response theory (IRT) from educational testing models this. The two-parameter logistic (2PL) model says

P(model l answers item i correctly) = σ( ai (θl − bi) ) (5.11) θl is the model’s ability, bi the item’s difficulty (the ability at which P = 0.5) and ai its discrimination (the slope there). tinyBenchmarks uses a multidimensional version, pil = 1/(1 + exp(−αiTθl + βi))[33].

A 2PL fit and 50-item estimates in numpy

irt_tiny.py generates responses of 200 “training” mock models to 1,000 items from a 2PL model with sharp items (median discrimination 3.3). It fits a, b and θ by joint maximum likelihood, then estimates the full 1,000-item accuracy of 100 new models from only 50 items, four ways:

def fit_2pl(y: np.ndarray, iters: int = 1500, lr: float = 0.05) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Joint MLE of theta (models), a (discrimination), b (difficulty) by gradient ascent."""
    n_m, n_i = y.shape
    theta = np.zeros(n_m)
    a = np.ones(n_i)
    b = -np.log(y.mean(axis=0).clip(0.02, 0.98) / (1 - y.mean(axis=0).clip(0.02, 0.98)))
    for _ in range(iters):
        r = y - sigmoid(a[None, :] * (theta[:, None] - b[None, :]))
        g_theta = (r * a[None, :]).sum(axis=1) / n_i
        g_a = (r * (theta[:, None] - b[None, :])).sum(axis=0) / n_m
        g_b = -(r * a[None, :]).sum(axis=0) / n_m
        theta += lr * 20 * g_theta
        a = np.clip(a + lr * g_a * 5, 0.05, 6.0)
        b += lr * g_b * 5
        mu, sd = theta.mean(), theta.std()
        theta = (theta - mu) / sd            # fix the scale: theta ~ (0, 1)
        b = (b - mu) / sd
        a = a * sd
    return theta, a, b


def fit_theta(resp: np.ndarray, a: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray, iters: int = 200) -> float:
    """MAP ability for one model given fixed items (N(0,1) prior keeps all-correct finite)."""
    t = 0.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        p = sigmoid(a * (t - b))
        grad = (a * (resp - p)).sum() - t
        hess = -(a * a * p * (1 - p)).sum() - 1.0
        t -= grad / hess
    return t

The efficiency results are for scoring a finished model cheaply. 5.13Statistics for RL

RL has every problem in this chapter, made worse by expense: papers report 3 to 10 runs per task, and task scores span orders of magnitude. Agarwal et al. showed how badly point estimates mislead there[22]: for one Atari 100k algorithm, going from 5 to 100 runs shifted the median by +0.03, about 36% of its claimed improvement. Earlier work had shown that seeds, codebases and hyperparameters change RL conclusions[39], and Colas et al. compared the false-positive rates of common tests on non-normal RL returns[40]. Chapter 7 applies them to RL environments (Section 7.8).

The rliable metrics

  • Interquartile mean (IQM): the mean of the middle 50% of all run × task scores. Robust like the median, but uses half the data, so its CI is tighter.
  • Optimality gap: γ − mean(min(score, γ)), the average shortfall from a target score γ (often 1 = human-normalised). Scores above the target earn no credit, so one superhuman task cannot hide failures elsewhere.
  • Probability of improvement: for each task, P(X > Y) for a random run of algorithm X against a random run of Y, estimated by the Mann–Whitney U statistic divided by the number of run pairs, then averaged over tasks.
  • Performance profiles: the share of runs whose normalised score exceeds τ, as a function of τ (a survival curve), with CI bands.
  • Stratified bootstrap CIs for all of the above: resample runs within each task.

The reference implementations are compact:

def aggregate_iqm(scores: np.ndarray):
  """Computes the interquartile mean across runs and tasks.
  ...
  """
  return scipy.stats.trim_mean(scores, proportiontocut=0.25, axis=None)


def aggregate_optimality_gap(scores: np.ndarray, gamma=1):
  """Computes optimality gap across all runs and tasks.
  ...
  """
  return gamma - np.mean(np.minimum(scores, gamma))


def probability_of_improvement(scores_x: np.ndarray, scores_y: np.ndarray):
  """Overall Probability of imporvement of algorithm `X` over `Y`.
  ...
  """
  num_tasks = scores_x.shape[1]
  task_improvement_probabilities = []
  num_runs_x, num_runs_y = scores_x.shape[0], scores_y.shape[0]
  for task in range(num_tasks):
    if np.array_equal(scores_x[:, task], scores_y[:, task]):
      task_improvement_prob = 0.5
    else:
      task_improvement_prob, _ = scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu(
          scores_x[:, task], scores_y[:, task], alternative='greater')
      task_improvement_prob /= (num_runs_x * num_runs_y)
    task_improvement_probabilities.append(task_improvement_prob)
  return np.mean(task_improvement_probabilities)

def trim_mean(x: np.ndarray, cut: float = 0.25) -> float:
    """Same convention as scipy.stats.trim_mean: drop int(cut * n) values from each end."""
    s = np.sort(np.ravel(x))
    g = int(cut * len(s))
    return float(s[g: len(s) - g].mean())


def optimality_gap(scores: np.ndarray, gamma: float = 1.0) -> float:
    return float(gamma - np.mean(np.minimum(scores, gamma)))


def mann_whitney_u(x: np.ndarray, y: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """U = #{(i, j): x_i > y_j} + 0.5 #{ties}."""
    d = x[:, None] - y[None, :]
    return float((d > 0).sum() + 0.5 * (d == 0).sum())


def prob_improvement(sx: np.ndarray, sy: np.ndarray) -> float:
    """P(X > Y) per task from the U statistic, averaged over tasks (scores are runs x tasks)."""
    return float(np.mean([mann_whitney_u(sx[:, t], sy[:, t]) / (sx.shape[0] * sy.shape[0]) for t in range(sx.shape[1])]))


def stratified_bootstrap(scores: np.ndarray, stat, rng: np.random.Generator, reps: int = 2000) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Resample runs independently within each task (column), recompute the aggregate."""
    r, t = scores.shape

5.14Mixing judge and human labels

LLM judges are cheap and biased; human labels are expensive and closer to unbiased. Chapter 4 treats PPI in detail (Section 4.11); here are the statistics.

The judge as a variance source. A stochastic judge adds within-item noise exactly like model sampling in Equation 5.1: re-judging each answer Kj times and averaging divides that term by Kj. It does nothing for the judge’s bias. With TPR and TNR measured on a human-labelled calibration set (Section 4.3), the judged pass rate is q = TPR·θ + (1 − TNR)(1 − θ), which is pulled toward the judge’s error rates: upward when the true accuracy θ is low, downward when it is high[43]. Inverting it gives the Rogan–Gladen correction from epidemiology[44]:

θ̂ = q̂ + TNR − 1TPR + TNR − 1, clipped to [0, 1] (5.12) It needs TPR + TNR > 1 (a judge better than chance). For a CI, bootstrap the judged set and the calibration set together and recompute θ̂; Lee et al. derive the analytic interval and how to split labels between the two sets[43]. 
def rogan_gladen(q: np.ndarray, tpr: np.ndarray, tnr: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Correct a judged pass rate q for judge error rates (needs TPR + TNR > 1); clip to [0, 1]."""
    return np.clip((q + tnr - 1.0) / (tpr + tnr - 1.0), 0.0, 1.0)

Prediction-powered inference. You have N items scored only by the judge f, and n items scored by both the judge and a human (Y). The PPI estimate of the mean quality is

θ̂PPI = 1N Σj f(X̃j) − 1n Σi ( f(Xi) − Yi ),    Var(θ̂PPI) ≈ σf²N + σf−Y²n (5.13) The second term, the rectifier, estimates the judge’s bias on the human-labelled items and subtracts it. The estimate is unbiased however biased the judge is; it is tighter than human-only when the judge’s errors are small (small σf−Y)[45].

Boyeau et al. report effective human sample sizes up to 50% larger with GPT-4 judges[46]; the library is aangelopoulos/ppi_py[45]. ppi_stats.py uses a judge that passes 40% of true failures, 5,000 judged items and 200 human-labelled ones.

def estimates(rng: np.random.Generator, flip: float) -> dict[str, tuple[float, float]]:
    _, f_u = draw(rng, N_UNLAB, flip)
    y_l, f_l = draw(rng, N_LAB, flip)
    judge = (f_u.mean(), 1.96 * f_u.std(ddof=1) / math.sqrt(N_UNLAB))
    human = (y_l.mean(), 1.96 * y_l.std(ddof=1) / math.sqrt(N_LAB))
    rect = f_l - y_l
    ppi_mean = f_u.mean() - rect.mean()
    ppi_half = 1.96 * math.sqrt(f_u.var(ddof=1) / N_UNLAB + rect.var(ddof=1) / N_LAB)
    return {"judge only": judge, "human only": human, "PPI": (ppi_mean, ppi_half)}

The judge-only interval is narrow and wrong (centred at 0.787, 0% coverage). PPI covers 95.3% with a half-width of 0.055 against human-only’s 0.064, worth roughly 35% more human labels, but with a judge that flips 15% of verdicts it is wider than humans alone. This is plain PPI (λ = 1). Power-tuned PPI++ estimates λ* = Cov(Y, f) / ((1 + n/N) Var f) from the data, and ppi_py does this by default with lam=None. For a weak judge λ* shrinks toward 0, so the interval falls back toward human-only and is never meaningfully wider: in the last output block the width ratio at flip rates 0.15 and 0.30 is 0.90 and 0.97, against 1.07 and 1.29 for plain PPI (see Section 4.11). Rogan–Gladen also removes the bias (94.1% coverage) but is wider here (0.095), because dividing by TPR + TNR − 1 amplifies the noise in the estimated error rates. Its advantage is that it needs only TPR and TNR, and Lee et al. show their corrected estimator stays unbiased when the calibration set’s mix differs from the test set’s[43].

5.15Hierarchical Bayesian evaluation

Agentic evals are often small and nested: a few task families, a handful of tasks each. Per-family accuracies are too noisy; one pooled number hides differences. A hierarchical model sits in between: family rates come from a shared population distribution learned from all families, so small families are shrunk toward the population mean and large ones keep their own estimate. UK AISI’s HiBayES framework applies multilevel Bayesian GLMs to exactly this setting (responses within items within categories within models, and fewer than 20 data points per eval), with a package at UKGovernmentBEIS/hibayes[47][48]; Bowyer et al. recommend a hierarchical Beta–Binomial model for clustered questions[8].

θj ~ Beta(μκ, (1 − μ)κ),    kj ~ Binomial(nj, θj),    μ ~ Uniform(0, 1),   log κ ~ Uniform (5.14) μ is the population mean success rate and κ the concentration (large κ = families are similar, strong pooling). Integrating out θj gives a beta-binomial likelihood B(μκ + kj, (1 − μ)κ + nj − kj) / B(μκ, (1 − μ)κ) per family, so the posterior over (μ, κ) can be computed exactly on a grid; given (μ, κ), each θj has a conjugate Beta posterior. 
def grid_posterior(k: np.ndarray, n: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Posterior over (mu, kappa) on the grid, from the product of beta-binomial likelihoods."""
    a = MU[:, None] * KAPPA[None, :]
    b = (1 - MU[:, None]) * KAPPA[None, :]
    logp = np.zeros_like(a)
    for kj, nj in zip(k, n):
        logp += log_beta(a + kj, b + nj - kj) - log_beta(a, b)   # binomial coefficient is constant
    logp -= logp.max()
    post = np.exp(logp)
    return post / post.sum()   # uniform prior on mu, log-uniform on kappa (grid is log-spaced)


def sample(post: np.ndarray, k: np.ndarray, n: np.ndarray, rng: np.random.Generator, draws: int = 20_000):
    flat = rng.choice(post.size, size=draws, p=post.ravel())
    mi, ki = np.unravel_index(flat, post.shape)
    mu, kappa = MU[mi], KAPPA[ki]
    theta = rng.beta(mu[:, None] * kappa[:, None] + k[None, :], (1 - mu[:, None]) * kappa[:, None] + (n - k)[None, :])
    return mu, kappa, theta

5.16Reporting checklist and template

The checklist consolidates Miller’s and Anthropic’s recommendations, the Sober Look practices, lm-eval’s lessons, rliable and the reproducibility checklists of Dodge et al., Reimers and Gurevych, and Bouthillier et al.[3][4][1][32][22][49][50][51]

  1. Sizes. Report n (questions or tasks), the number of clusters, K (samples per question), the number of seeds or training runs, and the decoding parameters.
  2. Uncertainty on every number. Mean ± SE or a 95% interval; clustered SE when items are grouped; Wilson or a Bayesian interval when n is below a few hundred. Name the method.
  3. Paired comparisons. Report the per-question paired difference, its SE or interval (clustered when tasks are grouped, on per-task means when K > 1), and the correlation between the models; not two separate intervals.
  4. Power and stopping rule up front. State the MDE of the design, or the power for the effect you care about, before running. Stopping rule declared before running (fixed n/K/seeds, or a sequential method named).
  5. Lower-variance metrics where valid. Token probabilities or continuous metrics; average over K samples; checkpoint averaging for training curves.
  6. Agent reliability. pass^k alongside pass@1 and pass@k, with n trials per task.
  7. RL aggregates. 10+ runs where possible; IQM and optimality gap with stratified-bootstrap CIs; performance profiles; per-run scores released.
  8. Multiplicity. A pre-declared primary metric, or Holm/BH corrections over the family of tests; all configurations tried, not just the best.
  9. The stack. Container or environment (including container CPU/RAM limits and timeouts, and the infra error rate), inference engine and version, GPU type, harness commit, prompt template, parser; seeds for everything, including the bootstrap.
  10. Raw data. Release prompts, per-item outputs and per-item scores so others can recompute every statistic.

  • An eval score is an estimate of a super-population mean; its variance splits into within-question (sampling, judge, infrastructure) and between-question parts, and they respond to different fixes.
  • 124/200 is 62% ± 6.7 points; 12/30 is ±17.5. Use Wilson or a Beta posterior below a few hundred items, and clustered SEs whenever questions share a passage, repo or environment.
  • Compare models with paired differences on the same questions; overlapping intervals are not a test. Pairing at ρ = 0.8 cuts the questions needed for a 3-point gain from 4,361 to 873.
  • Run a power or MDE calculation before the eval; most few-hundred-item benchmarks cannot see 1–3 point gains.
  • Reduce variance with K samples per question (SE on per-question means, never pooled K·n), token probabilities and continuous metrics, not by lowering the temperature.
  • For agents report pass^k with the unbiased combinatorial estimator next to pass@1 and pass@k; equal pass@1 can hide opposite reliability.
  • Temperature 0 is not deterministic; pin and report the stack (including sandbox limits), run the baseline on the same stack, and report mean ± sd over seeds as well as the CI over questions.
  • For RL and multi-task results use IQM, optimality gap and probability of improvement with stratified-bootstrap CIs; seed every bootstrap and correct for multiple comparisons.
