Chapter 01

What Is a Packet, and Why Bypass the Kernel?

You will be able to work out how many nanoseconds and CPU cycles a single packet gets at any link speed, explain why the normal Linux receive path cannot keep up, name the five techniques DPDK uses instead, and run your first DPDK program.

1What is a packet, on the wire?

You already know that an application hands data to a UDP socket and the kernel wraps it in UDP, IP and Ethernet headers. What leaves the network card is an Ethernet frame: a run of bytes sent one bit after another down a copper pair, a fibre or a radio. Software sees most of these bytes. A few it never sees, but they still use up the link's time, and that matters for every calculation in this chapter.

Three numbers to remember:

  • 64 B is the smallest legal frame (header 14 + payload 46 + FCS 4). A short UDP datagram is padded up to it. With 20 B of IPv4 header and 8 B of UDP header, 18 B of data is the most that still fits in a 64-byte frame.
  • 84 B of wire time per minimum frame, because of the 8 B preamble+SFD and the 12 B gap. Every line-rate figure you will see (14.88 Mpps at 10 GbE, and so on) uses 84, not 64.
  • 1518 B is the largest standard frame (1500 B payload + 18). Jumbo frames (up to about 9 KB) exist but must be configured on every hop.

Line-rate arithmetic

The line rate is the fastest a link can carry frames back to back. For a frame of S bytes on a link of R bits per second:

packets per second   pps    = R / ((S + 20) × 8)          (20 = preamble/SFD 8 + gap 12)
time per packet      t      = 1 / pps
cycles per packet    cycles = t × f_cpu                    (f_cpu = clock frequency of the core)

At 10 Gbit/s with 64-byte frames: 10×109 / 672 = 14.88 million packets per second, one every 67.2 ns. The cores in this course's VM run at 2.7 GHz (the TSC rate that hello_dpdk prints below), so that is 181 cycles for everything you want to do with a packet: receive it, look at its headers, decide where it goes, send it, and free its buffer. At 100 GbE the budget shrinks to 6.72 ns, or 18 cycles. A single cache miss to main memory costs more than that.

Small packets are the hard case: the same link carries 18 times more of them per second than full-size ones. Real traffic is a mix (a TCP flow sends big data segments one way and 64-byte ACKs the other way), but a router, firewall or load balancer has to survive a flood of small packets, so the 64-byte numbers are what people design for.

2What the kernel does with each packet

The Linux network stack is a fine piece of engineering, but it is general purpose: it has to serve thousands of sockets, enforce firewall rules, support every protocol, protect processes from each other, and share the CPU fairly. Each of those duties costs time per packet. Here is the path of one received UDP packet, from wire to your recv():

  1. DMA. The NIC writes the frame into a buffer in RAM that the driver gave it earlier, and marks the receive descriptor (a small record in a ring the NIC and driver share) as done. No CPU work yet.
  2. Interrupt (IRQ). The NIC raises an interrupt. The CPU stops whatever it was running, saves its state and jumps into the kernel's interrupt handler.
  3. NAPI softirq. The handler schedules a softirq; the driver's NAPI poll function then reads descriptors and, for each packet, allocates an sk_buff (the kernel's packet metadata structure, a few hundred bytes) and refills the ring.
  4. Protocol stack. GRO, netfilter hooks (iptables/nftables), routing decision, IP checks, UDP checksum, socket lookup.
  5. Socket queue. The sk_buff is queued on the socket's receive queue (with a lock), and the sleeping reader is woken.
  6. Context switch + system call. The scheduler switches to your process; recv() enters the kernel (crossing the user/kernel boundary, with its speculative-execution mitigations), dequeues the sk_buff...
  7. Copy. ...and copies the payload into your buffer, frees the sk_buff, and returns to user space.

Sending is the mirror image: a system call, a copy into a new sk_buff, the stack, the queueing discipline (qdisc), the driver, a doorbell write to the NIC, and later a "transmit complete" interrupt to free the buffer. Interrupt moderation, NAPI batching, GRO and recvmmsg() all reduce these costs, and the kernel can move a lot of traffic, but the per-packet overhead stays in the hundreds of nanoseconds to microseconds. Against a 67 ns budget, that is too much.

Watch it: the two-lane race

Now many packets. The top lane is the kernel path; the bottom lane is DPDK. Each lane's speed comes from the two measurements in section 3: about 829 ns per packet for the kernel socket path and about 9.5 ns for the DPDK burst path. The animation runs in slow motion (1 second on screen = 2 µs of real time). Raise the offered rate above the kernel's capacity (about 1.2 Mpps) and watch its backlog fill and drop packets, while the DPDK lane takes packets in bursts: each rx_burst() call grabs everything waiting in the receive ring, up to the burst size. Then set the rate to about 80 Mpps and the burst to 1: now even DPDK cannot keep up (it tops out near 58 Mpps), because it pays the per-call cost for every single packet. Set the burst back to 32 and the drops stop. That is technique 5 in action. Above about 106 Mpps even bursts of 32 are not enough for one core on this VM: that is when you add cores and queues (ch15).

3Measuring it on this machine (peek ahead)

Claims are cheap, so let us measure. code/bypass_bench.c times two things on one core:

  • kernel: 200 000 iterations of sendto() + recv() of a 64-byte UDP datagram over the loopback interface. Two system calls, two copies, the UDP/IP stack twice, no real NIC.
  • DPDK: 5 million packets received with rte_eth_rx_burst() and sent back with rte_eth_tx_burst() on a net_null port, 32 at a time.

Each measurement is repeated 5 times and the program prints the median with the minimum and maximum. This is a peek ahead: you are not expected to understand every line yet (you will be able to write it yourself after ch8). Here are the ten lines that matter:

code/bypass_bench.c (excerpt)C
static double dpdk_run(uint16_t port, unsigned int burst, double hz)
{
	struct rte_mbuf *pkts[MAX_BURST];
	uint64_t rx = 0;
	uint64_t t0 = rte_rdtsc();
	while (rx < DPDK_N) {
		uint16_t n = rte_eth_rx_burst(port, 0, pkts, burst);
		uint16_t sent = rte_eth_tx_burst(port, 0, pkts, n);
		/* tx_burst may accept fewer than n: we still own the rest */
		if (sent < n)
			rte_pktmbuf_free_bulk(&pkts[sent], n - sent);
		rx += n;
	}
	return (rte_rdtsc() - t0) / hz * 1e9 / rx;
}
  • Line 78. rte_eth_rx_burst(port, queue, array, n) fills an array with up to n packet pointers and returns how many it got. It is an ordinary function call in your own process: no system call, no interrupt, no copy.
  • Line 79. rte_eth_tx_burst() queues the same packets for sending and returns how many it accepted.
  • Lines 81-82. Whatever transmit did not accept still belongs to you and must be freed, or the pool leaks. You will meet this rule again in every forwarding loop.

Read this number honestly

net_null is a software driver that does no real I/O: its receive function just takes mbufs (packet buffers) from a pool and sets their length to 64, and its transmit function frees them (drivers/net/null/rte_eth_null.c, eth_null_rx / eth_null_tx). So the DPDK figure is the cost of the framework: the burst call, the driver dispatch and the buffer pool. It is not a NIC benchmark. The kernel figure, on the other hand, skips the NIC, the interrupt and the softirq entirely (loopback), so a real kernel receive path costs more. Real NIC numbers need real hardware and the method of ch22.

/* SPDX-License-Identifier: BSD-3-Clause
 * bypass_bench.c - PEEK AHEAD for chapter 01 (you can write this yourself
 * after ch8). Run it and look at the numbers; do not worry about every API.
 *
 * Question: what does it cost to move ONE small packet
 *   (a) through the Linux kernel: sendto() + recv() of a 64-byte UDP
 *       datagram on the loopback interface (two system calls, two copies,
 *       the whole UDP/IP stack, socket queues), versus
 *   (b) through DPDK: rte_eth_rx_burst() + rte_eth_tx_burst() on a net_null
 *       port (no syscalls, no copies, packets handled in bursts)?
 *
 * HONEST CAVEAT: net_null is a software PMD that does no real I/O. Its Rx
 * just takes mbufs from the mempool and its Tx frees them. So (b) measures the
 * DPDK *framework* cost (burst dispatch + mempool), not a NIC. Real NIC
 * numbers come in ch22.
 *
 * Each measurement is repeated RUNS times; we print median, min and max.
 *
 * Usage: ./bypass_bench <EAL args> --vdev=net_null0 [-- burst1 burst2 ...]
 *        default burst list: 32
 */
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>
#include <unistd.h>
#include <arpa/inet.h>
#include <sys/socket.h>

#include <rte_eal.h>

#include <rte_ethdev.h>
#include <rte_mbuf.h>
#include <rte_cycles.h>

#define RUNS      5
#define KERNEL_N  200000    /* datagrams per kernel run */
#define DPDK_N    5000000   /* packets per DPDK run     */
#define MAX_BURST 256

static int cmp_double(const void *a, const void *b)
{
	double x = *(const double *)a, y = *(const double *)b;
	return (x > y) - (x < y);
}

/* print "label: median ns/pkt (min..max), cycles/pkt, Mpps" */
static void report(const char *label, double ns[RUNS], double hz)
{
	qsort(ns, RUNS, sizeof(double), cmp_double);
	double med = ns[RUNS / 2];
	printf("%-34s %8.1f ns/pkt  (min %.1f, max %.1f)  %7.0f cycles/pkt  %7.2f Mpps\n",
	       label, med, ns[0], ns[RUNS - 1], med * hz / 1e9, 1e3 / med);
}

/* (a) kernel path: one sendto() and one recv() per 64-byte datagram */
static double kernel_run(int tx, int rx, struct sockaddr_in *dst, double hz)
{
	char buf[64] = {0}, in[2048];
	uint64_t t0 = rte_rdtsc();
	for (int i = 0; i < KERNEL_N; i++) {
		if (sendto(tx, buf, sizeof(buf), 0, (struct sockaddr *)dst,
			   sizeof(*dst)) != sizeof(buf))
			rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "sendto failed\n");
		if (recv(rx, in, sizeof(in), 0) != sizeof(buf))
			rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "recv failed\n");
	}
	return (rte_rdtsc() - t0) / hz * 1e9 / KERNEL_N;
}

/* (b) DPDK path: receive a burst from net_null, send the same burst back */
static double dpdk_run(uint16_t port, unsigned int burst, double hz)
{
	struct rte_mbuf *pkts[MAX_BURST];
	uint64_t rx = 0;
	uint64_t t0 = rte_rdtsc();
	while (rx < DPDK_N) {
		uint16_t n = rte_eth_rx_burst(port, 0, pkts, burst);
		uint16_t sent = rte_eth_tx_burst(port, 0, pkts, n);
		/* tx_burst may accept fewer than n: we still own the rest */
		if (sent < n)
			rte_pktmbuf_free_bulk(&pkts[sent], n - sent);
		rx += n;
	}
	return (rte_rdtsc() - t0) / hz * 1e9 / rx;
}

int main(int argc, char **argv)
{
	int ret = rte_eal_init(argc, argv);
	if (ret < 0)
		rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "rte_eal_init failed\n");
	argc -= ret;
	argv += ret;
	double hz = rte_get_tsc_hz();
	double ns[RUNS];

	printf("TSC %.2f GHz, %d runs per measurement, 64-byte packets\n",
	       hz / 1e9, RUNS);

	/* ---------- (a) the kernel path ---------- */
	int rx = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_DGRAM, 0);
	int tx = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_DGRAM, 0);
	struct sockaddr_in a = { .sin_family = AF_INET,
				 .sin_addr.s_addr = htonl(INADDR_LOOPBACK) };
	socklen_t alen = sizeof(a);
	if (rx < 0 || tx < 0 || bind(rx, (struct sockaddr *)&a, sizeof(a)) ||
	    getsockname(rx, (struct sockaddr *)&a, &alen))  /* learn our port */
		rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "socket setup failed\n");
	for (int r = 0; r < RUNS; r++)
		ns[r] = kernel_run(tx, rx, &a, hz);
	report("kernel sendto()+recv() 127.0.0.1", ns, hz);
	close(tx);
	close(rx);

	/* ---------- (b) the DPDK path ---------- */
	if (rte_eth_dev_count_avail() < 1)
		rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "need a port: --vdev=net_null0\n");
	uint16_t port = 0;
	struct rte_mempool *mp = rte_pktmbuf_pool_create("bb_pool", 8191, 256,
				0, RTE_MBUF_DEFAULT_BUF_SIZE, rte_socket_id());
	struct rte_eth_conf conf;
	memset(&conf, 0, sizeof(conf));
	if (mp == NULL ||
	    rte_eth_dev_configure(port, 1, 1, &conf) != 0 ||
	    rte_eth_rx_queue_setup(port, 0, 512, rte_socket_id(), NULL, mp) != 0 ||
	    rte_eth_tx_queue_setup(port, 0, 512, rte_socket_id(), NULL) != 0 ||
	    rte_eth_dev_start(port) != 0)
		rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "port setup failed\n");

	int nb = argc > 1 ? argc - 1 : 1;
	for (int i = 0; i < nb; i++) {
		unsigned int burst = argc > 1 ? (unsigned int)atoi(argv[i + 1]) : 32;
		char label[64];
		if (burst < 1 || burst > MAX_BURST)
			rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "burst must be 1..%d\n", MAX_BURST);
		dpdk_run(port, burst, hz);  /* warm-up: fill the mempool cache */
		for (int r = 0; r < RUNS; r++)
			ns[r] = dpdk_run(port, burst, hz);
		snprintf(label, sizeof(label), "dpdk rx+tx burst=%u (net_null)", burst);
		report(label, ns, hz);
	}

	rte_eth_dev_stop(port);
	rte_eth_dev_close(port);
	rte_mempool_free(mp);
	rte_eal_cleanup();
	return 0;
}

4DPDK's five techniques

The Data Plane Development Kit is a set of C libraries and drivers that lets an ordinary Linux process own a network card and move packets itself. In the words of its programmer's guide, its goal is "to provide a simple, complete framework for fast packet processing in data plane applications" (doc/guides/prog_guide/overview.rst). It gets its speed from five techniques. None is magic; each removes one of the costs you just counted.

  1. User-space drivers. The NIC is detached from its kernel driver and handed to vfio-pci, a small kernel module whose only job is to let a process map the device's registers into its address space and let the device DMA into the process's memory, safely, through the IOMMU. A DPDK poll mode driver (PMD) in your process then programs the NIC directly. No system call sits between your code and the packets. (Binding devices: ch9 sidebar; VFIO and PMD internals: ch23.)
  2. Polling instead of interrupts. A dedicated core calls rte_eth_rx_burst() in a tight loop. There are no interrupts, no context switches and no wake-up latency. The price is that this core runs at 100% even when no traffic arrives. Section 5 measures that price.
  3. Hugepages. All packet memory comes from 2 MB or 1 GB pages instead of 4 KB ones. A few TLB entries then cover every buffer, so address translation stays cheap, and the memory does not move, so the NIC can DMA into it (ch4).
  4. Lock-free rings and pools with per-core caches. Packet buffers are preallocated in a mempool, and each core keeps a small private cache of free buffers, so getting and freeing a buffer needs no lock and usually no atomic operation. Cores hand packets to each other through rte_ring, a lock-free FIFO of pointers (ch5, ch6).
  5. Bursts, cache awareness and SIMD. Every fast-path call works on an array of up to 32 (or more) packets, so its fixed cost is shared by all of them. Data structures are cache-line aligned, drivers prefetch the next descriptors, and many PMDs have vector (SSE/AVX/NEON) receive paths that handle 4 to 8 descriptors per instruction (ch7, ch22, ch23).

Two habits complete the picture: core pinning (each DPDK thread, an lcore, is fixed to one CPU and ideally isolated from the kernel scheduler, ch3, ch29) and NUMA awareness (allocate memory on the socket the NIC and core sit on, ch4, ch22).

One more fact about packet movement: when the application has finished with a burst, rte_eth_tx_burst() writes the descriptors and then one doorbell register write tells the NIC "there is new work". One expensive device write per burst instead of per packet: another way bursts pay.

What DPDK does not give you

DPDK has no TCP/IP stack and no sockets. rte_eth_rx_burst() hands you raw Ethernet frames, and you parse the headers yourself (ch10 shows how). If you need TCP on top of DPDK, use a project that adds one: F-Stack (FreeBSD's stack), Seastar, mTCP or the VPP host stack. The capstone (ch30) shows the other common pattern: DPDK handles the fast path and the Linux kernel keeps everything else, such as ARP, SSH and routing daemons.

5Polling vs interrupts: the price of speed (peek ahead)

Polling wins on latency and throughput because the core is always ready. But it burns a whole core when the link is idle. The opposite design sleeps until an interrupt says a packet has arrived: almost free when idle, but every packet now pays a wake-up. Linux's NAPI is a hybrid (interrupt first, then poll while busy), and DPDK can do the same: many PMDs, including net_tap and virtio, support Rx interrupts ("Rx interrupt = Y" in doc/guides/nics/features/tap.ini).

code/intr_vs_poll.c runs one net_tap port in either mode for 4 seconds while code/inject.py writes frames into the Linux side of the tap interface at a fixed rate. Each frame carries the sender's clock, so the program can also measure the latency from send() in Python to rte_eth_rx_burst() in C. The interesting part of the loop:

code/intr_vs_poll.c (excerpt)C
	while (!quit && rte_rdtsc() < end) {
		uint16_t n = rte_eth_rx_burst(port, 0, b, 32);
		polls++;
		pkts += n;
		if (n != 0) {
			uint64_t t_rx = now_ns();
			for (uint16_t i = 0; i < n; i++)
				sample_latency(b[i], t_rx);
		}
		rte_pktmbuf_free_bulk(b, n);       /* we only count packets */
		if (n != 0)
			continue;
		empty++;
		if (use_intr) {
			struct rte_epoll_event ev;
			rte_eth_dev_rx_intr_enable(port, 0);   /* arm   */
			rte_epoll_wait(RTE_EPOLL_PER_THREAD, &ev, 1, 100); /* sleep */
			rte_eth_dev_rx_intr_disable(port, 0);  /* disarm */
			wakeups++;
		}
	}

In poll mode the loop just spins. In intr mode, after an empty poll it arms the queue's interrupt, sleeps in rte_epoll_wait() for up to 100 ms, then disarms it and polls again. The full lab (with rte_power and the l3fwd-power heuristics) is in ch22.

One 4-second run per setting. Poll mode costs a full core (about 100% CPU) at every rate, idle included, and makes tens to hundreds of millions of empty polls. Intr mode uses a few percent at most, even at 10 000 pkt/s, and its idle wake-ups are just the 100 ms timeout (40 in 4 s). It pays in latency: compare the medians, especially at low rates where every packet finds the core asleep. ivcsw counts involuntary context switches: how often the scheduler took the core away. A few per run are normal on a shared VM; hundreds would mean another busy thread shares the core and every number is suspect (core isolation: ch3, ch29).

A PMD on top of a kernel interface is not kernel bypass

Look at poll mode with no traffic: most of its CPU time is sys, kernel time. net_tap reads packets from the tap file descriptor with readv(), a system call (pmd_rx_burst in drivers/net/tap/rte_eth_tap.c). Until the first SIGIO signal says data is waiting, it calls readv() on every poll; once traffic has started, it skips the call while no new signal has arrived, which is why sys drops in the runs with traffic. net_tap, net_af_packet and net_af_xdp are very useful for testing and for talking to the kernel (the course uses them a lot), but only a PMD that drives real hardware, or shared memory (net_memif, vhost/virtio, ch24), skips the kernel completely.

7Your first DPDK program

Enough theory. The smallest useful DPDK program starts the EAL (Environment Abstraction Layer, the part of DPDK that sets up cores, memory and devices), prints what it found, and shuts it down again.

code/hello_dpdk.cC
/* SPDX-License-Identifier: BSD-3-Clause
 * hello_dpdk.c - the smallest useful DPDK program (chapter 01).
 *
 * What it does:
 *   1. rte_eal_init()        : start the Environment Abstraction Layer (EAL).
 *                              It parses the EAL options (-l, --vdev, -m ...),
 *                              reserves memory, starts lcore threads and probes
 *                              devices. It returns how many argv entries it used.
 *   2. rte_version()         : which DPDK we linked against.
 *   3. rte_lcore_count()     : how many lcores (EAL worker threads) exist.
 *   4. rte_eth_dev_count_avail(): how many Ethernet ports were probed.
 *      With --vdev=net_null0 --vdev=net_ring0 we get two software ports.
 *   5. rte_rdtsc()/rte_get_tsc_hz(): the CPU time-stamp counter, the clock
 *      every DPDK benchmark in this course uses.
 *   6. rte_eal_cleanup()     : release everything the EAL took.
 *
 * Run: ./hello_dpdk -l 0 --no-pci --no-huge -m 64 --file-prefix=ch01-hello \
 *        --vdev=net_null0 --vdev=net_ring0
 */
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>

#include <rte_eal.h>      /* rte_eal_init, rte_eal_cleanup */
#include <rte_debug.h>    /* rte_exit */
#include <rte_version.h>  /* rte_version */
#include <rte_lcore.h>    /* rte_lcore_count, rte_get_main_lcore */
#include <rte_ethdev.h>   /* rte_eth_dev_count_avail, RTE_ETH_FOREACH_DEV */
#include <rte_cycles.h>   /* rte_rdtsc, rte_get_tsc_hz */

int
main(int argc, char **argv)
{
	/* Everything before "--" on the command line belongs to the EAL. */
	int ret = rte_eal_init(argc, argv);
	if (ret < 0)
		rte_exit(EXIT_FAILURE, "rte_eal_init failed\n");
	argc -= ret;   /* skip what the EAL consumed; the rest is ours */
	argv += ret;

	printf("Hello from %s\n", rte_version());
	printf("lcores: %u (main lcore %u)\n", rte_lcore_count(),
	       rte_get_main_lcore());

	/* Each --vdev became one ethdev port, numbered from 0. */
	uint16_t nb_ports = rte_eth_dev_count_avail();
	printf("Ethernet ports available: %u\n", nb_ports);

	uint16_t port;
	RTE_ETH_FOREACH_DEV(port) {
		char name[RTE_ETH_NAME_MAX_LEN];
		struct rte_eth_dev_info info;

		rte_eth_dev_get_name_by_port(port, name, sizeof(name));
		if (rte_eth_dev_info_get(port, &info) == 0)
			printf("  port %u: %-10s driver=%s\n", port, name,
			       info.driver_name);
	}

	/* The TSC ticks at a fixed rate; that rate is our "cycles" unit. */
	uint64_t hz = rte_get_tsc_hz();
	uint64_t best = UINT64_MAX;
	for (int i = 0; i < 1000; i++) {       /* cheapest of 1000 tries */
		uint64_t t0 = rte_rdtsc();
		uint64_t t1 = rte_rdtsc();
		if (t1 - t0 < best)
			best = t1 - t0;
	}
	printf("TSC: %.2f GHz, so 1 ns = %.1f cycles\n", hz / 1e9, hz / 1e9);
	printf("Two back-to-back rte_rdtsc() calls: %lu cycles apart (best of 1000)\n",
	       (unsigned long)best);

	/* Give memory, hugepage files and threads back. */
	rte_eal_cleanup();
	return 0;
}

Walking through it:

  • rte_eal_init(argc, argv) (line 34) does a lot: it parses the EAL options, detects CPUs and NUMA nodes, reserves memory, creates one pinned thread per extra lcore, scans the buses and probes devices. It returns the number of arguments it consumed (or a negative value on error), so lines 37-38 shift argv to reach your own options after --. ch2 and ch3 take this apart.
  • rte_version() returns a string such as "DPDK 26.11.0"; rte_lcore_count() counts the lcores you asked for with -l.
  • rte_eth_dev_count_avail() (line 45) counts Ethernet ports. Here they are virtual: every --vdev=net_null0, --vdev=net_ring0 creates one. RTE_ETH_FOREACH_DEV loops over port numbers.
  • rte_rdtsc() reads the CPU's time-stamp counter, and rte_get_tsc_hz() says how fast it ticks. This is the "cycles" unit used for every number in this chapter.
  • rte_eal_cleanup() (line 73) gives the memory and threads back. Call it before exiting.

The skeleton x-ray: the loop this course fills in

Here is the heart of DPDK's basic forwarding sample, examples/skeleton/basicfwd.c. It is a complete forwarder: every packet received on port 0 goes out of port 1 and the other way round. Chapters 3 to 8 open with this loop and explain one more piece each time ("x-ray" style): ch3 the lcore it runs on, ch4 the memory, ch5 and ch6 the pool behind the buffers, ch7 the rte_mbuf, and ch8 the port setup and the burst calls.

$DPDK_SRC/examples/skeleton/basicfwd.cC
	for (;;) {
		/*
		 * Receive packets on a port and forward them on the paired
		 * port. The mapping is 0 -> 1, 1 -> 0, 2 -> 3, 3 -> 2, etc.
		 */
		RTE_ETH_FOREACH_DEV(port) {

			/* Get burst of RX packets, from first port of pair. */
			struct rte_mbuf *bufs[BURST_SIZE];
			const uint16_t nb_rx = rte_eth_rx_burst(port, 0,
					bufs, BURST_SIZE);

			if (unlikely(nb_rx == 0))
				continue;

			/* Send burst of TX packets, to second port of pair. */
			const uint16_t nb_tx = rte_eth_tx_burst(port ^ 1, 0,
					bufs, nb_rx);

			/* Free any unsent packets. */
			if (unlikely(nb_tx < nb_rx)) {
				uint16_t buf;
				for (buf = nb_tx; buf < nb_rx; buf++)
					rte_pktmbuf_free(bufs[buf]);
			}
		}
	}

8The DPDK architecture map

DPDK 26.11 is about sixty libraries plus drivers. They stack in layers: the EAL at the bottom talks to the operating system; core libraries (rings, pools, packet buffers) build on it; device-class libraries such as ethdev (Ethernet) or cryptodev define one generic API per kind of device; drivers implement those APIs for specific hardware or software devices; and applications sit on top. Click (or tab to) any box.

DPDK layer cakeinteractive
Select a box to see what it does, its main header and the chapter that teaches it.

Two patterns repeat everywhere and are worth noticing now:

  • Dependencies run downwards. A packet buffer (rte_mbuf) lives in a pool (rte_mempool), whose default storage is a ring (rte_ring), which lives in memory the EAL reserved. That is exactly the order of ch4 to ch7.
  • Every device class works the same way. A generic library (lib/ethdev, lib/cryptodev, ...) defines the API; each driver registers itself with a bus (PCI or vdev), and the EAL's bus probe at start-up creates the devices. Fast-path functions such as rte_eth_rx_burst() are static inline wrappers that jump straight into the driver's burst function through a table of function pointers (lib/ethdev/rte_ethdev.h, lib/ethdev/rte_ethdev_core.h).

9Where DPDK came from

  • About 2010: started inside Intel to show that ordinary x86 servers could do packet processing that used to need network processors. 6WIND contributed early on.
  • 2013: 6WIND launched dpdk.org as an open community with a public mailing list.
  • 2016: version numbers became YY.MM (16.04); 16.11 was the first LTS.
  • April 2017: DPDK became a Linux Foundation project with a governing board and a technical board.
  • Since then: meson replaced make (20.11), eventdev, rte_flow, cryptodev, dmadev, graph and many more arrived, and DPDK runs on x86, Arm, POWER, RISC-V and LoongArch, on Linux, FreeBSD and Windows.
  • Licence: BSD-3-Clause for libraries and drivers, so it can be used in closed-source products. That is one reason vendors adopted it so widely.

The lines are blurry: DPDK itself has an net_af_xdp and an net_af_packet driver, so a DPDK application can run on top of kernel sockets when you cannot give it the whole NIC. XDP processes packets inside the kernel driver with eBPF programs, before an sk_buff exists (Høiland-Jørgensen et al., "The eXpress Data Path", CoNEXT 2018).

When not to use DPDK

  • The packet rate is modest. A web server or a database that needs TCP and handles a few hundred thousand packets per second is better off with the kernel (plus epoll, io_uring or SO_REUSEPORT).
  • You need the kernel's TCP/IP stack, tools and firewall. With DPDK, ip, tcpdump and iptables no longer see the port (you use dpdk-dumpcap, telemetry and testpmd instead, ch13, ch16).
  • You cannot dedicate cores or the NIC. Polling cores show 100% CPU, and a NIC bound to vfio-pci disappears from Linux. AF_XDP keeps the NIC shared.
  • Power or idle cost matters more than latency, and traffic is bursty (see section 5; ch22 shows the power-saving options).
  • The team is small and has no measured performance problem. DPDK is a big toolkit with its own operational model (hugepages, device binding, versions; ch29).

Common pitfalls

  • Calculating line rate with 64 B instead of 84 B. You get 19.5 Mpps at 10 GbE instead of 14.88. Always add the 20 bytes of preamble, SFD and gap.
  • Binding the NIC that carries your SSH session to DPDK. The interface vanishes from Linux and your session dies. On this course's machine eth0 is never touched; all examples use virtual devices.
  • "My DPDK app uses 100% CPU while idle, is it broken?" No: polling cores always look busy. Measure useful work with DPDK's own counters (ch13), not top.
  • Treating net_null numbers as NIC numbers. They measure framework overhead only. Likewise, net_tap and net_af_packet make system calls, so they are not kernel bypass.
  • Forgetting --file-prefix. Two DPDK processes with the default prefix fight over /var/run/dpdk/rte/; the second one may fail or attach to the first as a "secondary" process (ch16).
  • Losing output when a sample is killed. When stdout is a file or pipe, it is fully buffered; a program stopped by timeout loses what it printed. Run it under stdbuf -oL, as run.sh does for dpdk-skeleton.
  • Expecting sockets. DPDK gives you raw frames; there is no connect() or accept().

Exercise 1 easy

Compute packets per second, ns per packet and cycles per packet for 25 GbE with 128-byte frames on a 3.0 GHz core. If one DRAM miss costs about 80 ns, how many fit in the budget? Check your answer with the calculator above.

Hint

Wire size is 128 + 20 = 148 bytes = 1184 bits.

Solution

25×109 / 1184 = 21.1 Mpps; 47.4 ns per packet; 142 cycles at 3.0 GHz; 47.4 / 80 = 0.59, so not even one DRAM miss per packet. code/solutions/ex1_budget.c prints the whole table; its output is in code/solutions/ex1_output.txt.

Exercise 2 medium

Run bypass_bench with bursts of 1, 8 and 32 (./bypass_bench <EAL args> -- 1 8 32; the program reads burst sizes after --). Plot cycles per packet against burst size. Why does the cost per packet fall as the burst grows, and why does it not fall in proportion?

Hint

Split the cost of one call into a fixed part (the call, the driver dispatch, reading queue state) and a part per packet (getting and freeing each buffer).

Solution

Measured on this machine (section 3 of expected_output.txt):

dpdk rx+tx burst=1 (net_null)          17.3 ns/pkt  (min 17.3, max 18.0)       47 cycles/pkt    57.80 Mpps
dpdk rx+tx burst=8 (net_null)           6.8 ns/pkt  (min 6.7, max 7.1)       18 cycles/pkt   147.92 Mpps
dpdk rx+tx burst=32 (net_null)          9.3 ns/pkt  (min 9.3, max 9.3)       25 cycles/pkt   107.24 Mpps

The fixed cost of each pair of calls is shared by n packets, so it shrinks per packet as 1/n. The per-packet part (taking an mbuf from the pool cache in Rx, returning it in Tx) does not shrink, so the curve flattens. Going from 1 to 8 is the big win. Between 8 and 32 the result on this VM changed from one process to the next (in other runs burst 8 took 13.0 ns and burst 32 11.9 ns), so it is within the noise of where buffers and arrays land in the caches: measure on your own machine before you tune. On a real NIC larger bursts also mean fewer doorbell writes.

Summary
  • A minimum Ethernet frame is 64 B but takes 84 B of wire time; at 10 GbE that is 14.88 Mpps and 67.2 ns per packet (181 cycles at 2.7 GHz), and only 18 cycles at 100 GbE.
  • The kernel path pays for an interrupt, a softirq, an sk_buff, the protocol stack, a socket queue, a context switch, a system call and a copy. Measured here, a loopback UDP sendto() + recv() costs about 829 ns; a DPDK burst rx+tx on net_null about 9.5 ns.
  • DPDK's five techniques: user-space drivers (VFIO + PMDs), polling, hugepages, lock-free rings and pools with per-core caches, and bursts with cache-aware and SIMD code.
  • Polling costs a full core even when idle; interrupts save CPU but add wake-up latency. DPDK supports both (ch22).
  • Work can be organised as run-to-completion, pipeline or event-driven; DPDK has no TCP stack and no sockets.
  • rte_eal_init() starts everything, rte_eth_dev_count_avail() counts ports, rte_rdtsc() measures cycles, rte_eal_cleanup() releases it all.
  • Use DPDK when you need the highest packet rate and can dedicate cores and NICs; otherwise consider AF_XDP, io_uring or plain sockets.
