    A Guided Tour of step and forward

    You have stepped thousands of worlds with one call. Now we open that call. mjw.step is a fixed sequence of stages, about one hundred small kernel launches, that turns the state of every world into forces, accelerations and the next state. By the end of this chapter you can name every stage, call them yourself, audit the forces they produce, count and classify the kernels they launch, and you will build the Part I checkpoint: a dashboard over thousands of bouncing balls.

    Intermediate About 2.5 h· Prerequisites: Ch 6 (and through it Ch 4, Ch 5)

    You will learn

    • The stage order of forward and which Data fields each stage writes.
    • How to call the stages by hand and reproduce forward exactly, with the right factorize pairing.
    • The force budget qfrc_smooth = qfrc_passive - qfrc_bias + qfrc_actuator + qfrc_applied (+ JT xfrc) as a checkable identity.
    • How many kernels one step launches, and the five pipeline-wide launch shapes (atlas layouts 1, 2, 6, 7, 8).
    • How disable flags switch stages off, what @event_scope and EventTracer measure, and what a CUDA graph is, in one picture.

    Before you start: recap

    • An MjModel is copied to the device with mjw.put_model; mjw.make_data(mjm, nworld=..., nconmax=..., njmax=...) allocates the batched state (Ch 5 §4).
    • Every Data array has a leading nworld axis: d.qpos is (nworld, nq), d.xpos is (nworld, nbody) of vec3. The contact pool is shared: (naconmax,) with a worldid column (Ch 6).
    • Change state in place with .assign() / wp.copy(); replacing an array object breaks captured graphs (Ch 6).
    • A kernel launch with dim=(nworld, n) starts one thread per (world, element); inside, worldid, i = wp.tid(). 32 lanes run in lockstep; branches serialize; contiguous reads coalesce (Ch 4 §4).
    • A rigid body has 6 DOF: position + orientation (quat), linear + angular velocity (Ch 3 §4). Semi-implicit Euler updates the velocity first and then the position with the new velocity (Ch 3 §2).

    1step = forward + one integrator

    Every simulation step in MuJoCo, and in MJWarp, has two halves. The first half, forward dynamics, looks at the current state of each world and computes everything that follows from it: where the bodies are, what touches what, which forces act, and finally the acceleration qacc. It does not change the state. The second half, the integrator, uses that acceleration to move the state forward by one timestep. Here is the whole of mjw.step in the 4.0.1 wheel:

    @event_scope
def step(m: Model, d: Data):
  """Advance simulation."""
  forward(m, d)

  if m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.EULER:
    euler(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.RK4:
    rungekutta4(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator in (IntegratorType.IMPLICITFAST, IntegratorType.IMPLICIT):
    implicit(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.DISCRETE:
    discrete(m, d)
  else:
    raise NotImplementedError(f"integrator {m.opt.integrator} not implemented.")

    Three things in these lines matter for the whole course:

    • The branch is Python. m.opt.integrator is a plain int, read every time you call step. Changing it between calls is fine; it just selects other kernels. (Inside a recorded CUDA graph this choice is frozen; Ch 24.)
    • All integrators end in _advance. It launches _next_activation, _next_velocity and _next_position over (nworld, nactuator), (nworld, nv) and (nworld, njnt), inserts control history, advances d.time and writes the d.overflow bits (_next_time_builder), and finally copies qacc into qacc_warmstart so the next solve starts from this one.
    • RK4 runs forward four times. step calls forward once (with sensors), then rungekutta4 calls _forward(m, d, skip_sensor=True) three more times at perturbed states and combines the four accelerations with weights 1/6, 1/3, 1/3, 1/6. Toggle RK4 in the figure to see the belt loop.

    What exactly is "the state"? The Data docstring lists the state fields first. The state is what the integrator advances: time, qpos, qvel, act (plus history and plastic for delays and plasticity). The inputs are what you set: ctrl, qfrc_applied, xfrc_applied, mocap_pos/quat, eq_active. One field is carried between steps without being either: qacc_warmstart, the solver's starting guess, which _advance copies from qacc (Ch 20); likewise tree_asleep carries sleep state (Ch 21). Everything else (positions of geoms, contacts, mass matrix, forces, qacc) is a derived output that forward recomputes from scratch every step.

    2Inside forward: the stage list

    mjw.forward(m, d) is a thin wrapper around _forward. The list below is read from the installed 4.0.1 wheel, not from memory or from the C documentation. The groups are the stations of the pipeline breadcrumb you will see at the top of every chapter up to Ch 23.

    1. [sleep only] sleep.wake, sleep.update_sleep (Ch 21).
    2. Position: fwd_position(m, d, factorize=False) runs fwd_kinematics (kinematics, com_pos, camlight, flex, tendon), then smooth.crb and tendon_armature (the mass matrix M), then collision_driver.collision, constraint.make_constraint, [sleep: island.island] and smooth.transmission.
    3. Position sensors: d.sensordata.zero_(), sensor_pos, _energy_pos.
    4. Velocity: fwd_velocity: actuator and tendon velocities, com_vel, passive, rne, tendon_bias.
    5. Velocity sensors: sensor_vel, _energy_vel.
    6. Control callback: m.callback.control(m, d) if you set one and actuation is enabled (Ch 8).
    7. Actuation: fwd_actuation: actuator_force, qfrc_actuator.
    8. Acceleration: fwd_acceleration(m, d, factorize=True): sum the forces into qfrc_smooth, then factor M and solve for qacc_smooth in one fused call (smooth.factor_solve_i).
    9. Constraint solve: solver.solve turns qacc_smooth into qacc and qfrc_constraint.
    10. [when requested] smooth.rne_postconstraint, then acceleration sensors sensor_acc.

    Version note: wheel vs dev tree

    In the 4.0.1 wheel, fwd_position builds the mass matrix (crb, tendon_armature, optional factor_m) before collision. In the dev tree at commit 8f5b9f1 the order is collision and make_constraint first, then crb. Colab installs the wheel, so this course, the breadcrumb and every example follow the wheel. Other details differ too (sleep wake-up moves into fwd_position, com_vel may run early for connect/weld equalities, contacts may be sorted); this course follows the wheel.

    Calling the stages yourself

    Every stage is a public function, so you can run the pipeline by hand. That is how you inspect intermediate results, insert your own work between stages, or skip stages you do not need. Example 7.1 does exactly what forward does, in five calls, and checks that the final qacc is identical.

    Read the two last arrays together. Before the solve, qacc_smooth says the ball (dof 2) and the box (dof 8) fall at -9.81\,\mathrm{m/s^2}: gravity with nothing holding them. After the solve, qacc is zero for both: they rest on the floor, and the contact forces cancel gravity. The arm (dof 12) touches nothing, so its acceleration is the same before and after. 40 contacts over 8 worlds means 5 per world, the same count C MuJoCo reports for this scene.

    Example 7.2 makes the same walk visible: it starts from a fresh Data, where every derived field is zero, copies in a settled state, and prints a few fields after each stage.

    A few things to notice. The free ball's 6×6 block of M is diagonal: mass 1 kg on the three translations and \tfrac25 m r^2 = 0.004 on the rotations, so the 13 stored nonzeros of M are just its diagonal. qfrc_bias on the ball's z dof is +9.81: the bias force is defined with the opposite sign of the force it represents, which is why the force budget subtracts it (§5). The solve needed a single iteration: a ball and box at rest give a small, nearly quadratic problem that Newton solves in one step. This Data started with qacc_warmstart = 0, so warm-starting is not the reason.

    3One pitfall: the factorize pairing

    Solving M\,\ddot q = f needs a factorization of the mass matrix M. The factor (qLD, qLDiagInv) can be computed in two places, and the public stage functions and _forward make opposite choices:

    Who callsPosition stageAcceleration stage
    _forward (inside step)fwd_position(factorize=False): no factorfwd_acceleration(factorize=True): fused factor_solve_i
    You, with public defaultsfwd_position() = factorize=True: factor_m writes qLDfwd_acceleration() = factorize=False: solve_m with that qLD

    Pitfall

    Calling stages by hand needs exactly one factorization per new qpos. Use either fwd_position() + fwd_acceleration() (public defaults) or fwd_position(factorize=False) + fwd_acceleration(factorize=True) (the _forward pairing). Never fwd_position(factorize=False) + fwd_acceleration(): that solves with a factor from an old qpos and gives a wrong qacc without any error. The full story and the stale-factor experiment belong to Ch 11 §9.

    Going deeper: when is qacc_smooth skipped altogether?

    The 4.0.1 wheel has an experimental option m.opt.run_qacc_smooth (default True, set in io.py::put_model). smooth.skip_qacc_smooth returns True only when it is False and the solver is Newton, warmstart is on, there are no noslip iterations, iterations > 0 and njmax > 0. Then the Newton solver starts every world from qacc_warmstart, and fwd_acceleration leaves qacc_smooth stale and skips the factorization. step1 uses the same test to decide whether to factor (fwd_position(m, d, factorize=not smooth.skip_qacc_smooth(m, d))). Leave it at the default unless a later chapter tells you otherwise.

    Note the factor_m row: the table above lists qLD too, but here only qLDiagInv changes. In the playground every block of M is compact (diagonal), so qLD has zero columns and factor_m only fills qLDiagInv; in scenes with coupled DOFs it writes qLD too.

    Two details are worth a second look. euler reads efc.Ma: the Euler integrator solves (M + h\,D)\,\ddot q = M\ddot q_{\text{solver}} to treat joint damping implicitly, and the solver left M\ddot q in efc.Ma (Ch 13). And flex and tendon change nothing in a scene without flexes or tendons: those launches have an empty dim (§8).

    5The force budget

    Before the constraint solver runs, every force that is not a constraint force is added into one vector per world, qfrc_smooth, in joint space (one entry per DOF). The kernel forward.py::_qfrc_smooth computes, for each (worldid, dofid):

    qfrc_smooth[w, i] = qfrc_passive[w, i] - qfrc_bias[w, i] + qfrc_actuator[w, i] + qfrc_applied[w, i]

    and then support.py::xfrc_accumulate adds the Cartesian wrenches in xfrc_applied, mapped to joint space by the transposed body Jacobian, J^T\,\mathrm{xfrc}. The passive term is itself a sum, qfrc_passive = qfrc_spring + qfrc_damper + qfrc_gravcomp + qfrc_fluid + qfrc_adhesion (gravity compensation is included unless an actuator applies it). Finally M qacc_smooth = qfrc_smooth gives the unconstrained acceleration.

    An identity now, physics later

    Here we treat the budget as bookkeeping you can check to the last bit. What each term means physically (why the bias force carries gravity with a minus sign, what a moment arm is) is Ch 11 and Ch 12.

    The residual is exactly zero, not "about 10^{-6}": the script adds the same float32 numbers in the same order as the kernel. Look at the box rows: a 5 N push along world x shows up on box x, and the 0.5 N·m twist about world z is spread over rx, ry, rz. That is J^T at work: the rotational DOFs of a free joint are expressed in the body frame, and the box has tumbled, so a world-z torque has components on all three body axes. m.dof_damping is an array field, so assigning it at runtime is enough to switch on hinge damping: qfrc_passive on the hinge is -0.2\,\dot q.

    Pushing a body: xfrc_applied

    d.xfrc_applied is the friendliest input for "push this thing": a (nworld, nbody) array of spatial_vector, laid out as force (x, y, z) then torque (x, y, z) in world coordinates, applied at the body's center of mass. You never compute a Jacobian yourself: the acceleration stage does it. Example 7.9 pushes the box with a different force in every world.

    The force arrives in qfrc_smooth unchanged (the push acts at the center of mass along a world axis, and the free joint's translational DOFs are world-aligned). Below \mu m g = 23.5\,\mathrm{N} the box stays put: the solver finds a friction force that cancels the push. Because the contact is soft and regularized, it does not hold perfectly: at 20 N, below the threshold, the box still creeps about 6 mm. Above the threshold the box slides, and its velocity after 0.5 s stays within about 10% of the rigid Coulomb estimate (F - \mu m g)\,t / m, sometimes below it (30, 40, 60 N) and sometimes above it (50 N: 1.796 vs 1.653 m/s; 70 N: 3.062 vs 2.904 m/s). The deviation has no fixed sign: it comes from MuJoCo's soft constraints (Ch 9) and from the pyramidal friction cone, which approximates the circular Coulomb cone (Ch 19). We lowered the friction to 0.3 at runtime because a box pushed at its center tips over once F > m g, which with \mu = 1 is the same threshold as sliding.

    6The constraint solve: the last big stage

    After fwd_acceleration, each world has an unconstrained acceleration qacc_smooth, the motion you would get if nothing touched anything. Contacts, joint limits, friction loss and equality constraints are rows of a constraint system built by make_constraint: each efc row has a Jacobian row efc.J, a regularizer efc.D and a reference acceleration efc.aref. solver.solve then looks for the acceleration closest to qacc_smooth (in the metric of M) that the constraints allow, which is a convex optimization problem (Ch 19). The default solver is Newton (m.opt.solver = 2, up to m.opt.iterations = 100 iterations, each with an exact linesearch of up to ls_iterations = 50 steps).

    One relation ties the solve to the force budget: M qacc = qfrc_smooth + qfrc_constraint. You saw it in Example 7.2: for the resting ball, qfrc_constraint on dof 2 is exactly +9.81, which cancels the -9.81 in qfrc_smooth, so qacc is 0. If Data was allocated with no room for constraint rows (d.njmax == 0) or nv == 0, solve just copies qacc_smooth into qacc; otherwise the solver runs even for worlds with nefc == 0.

    Not in MJWarp

    The C flags MIDPHASE and AUTORESET have no DisableBit member (the class comment in types.py says "unsupported: MIDPHASE, AUTORESET"). There is no automatic reset on NaN: Ch 8 adds one.

    Each row is what you would predict. Without gravity, nothing falls and the arm turns only under its motor. Without contacts, the ball and box fall through the floor: z = 1 - g\,h^2\,N(N+1)/2 = 1 - 9.81 \cdot 10^{-4} \cdot 5050 = -3.954 after N = 100 steps of semi-implicit Euler (velocity first, then position). Without actuation, the arm swings under gravity alone. CONSTRAINT removes contacts too, so its row equals the CONTACT row in this scene, and the counters nacon and nefc drop to 0.

    Flags change which kernels exist

    Many kernels are built per configuration with wp.static(...) branches, so a new flag combination may compile new kernels the first time (a pause of seconds). And because the flag is read in Python, a CUDA graph recorded before the change keeps running the old pipeline: recapture after changing any m.opt integer field (Ch 24). Array options such as m.opt.timestep or m.opt.gravity are different: they are read from device memory on every launch.

    8How many kernels, and what shape

    MJWarp is MuJoCo rewritten as a few hundred small kernels. A single mjw.step issues about a hundred of them, one after the other. Example 7.5 counts them by wrapping wp.launch and wp.launch_tiled (the real functions still run) and records the launch dim of each.

    Why so many? Because each kernel does one simple, uniform job over a regular index space, which is what GPUs are good at (Ch 4). A model feature you do not use still costs a launch call from Python: 30 of the 100 launches have an empty dim (no tendons, no flex, no limits) and Warp returns before doing anything (wp.launch only runs the kernel when the total dim size is positive). The humanoid, with 4 times as many bodies and twice the DOFs, needs fewer launches (95) than the playground (100): the count depends on which features a model uses, not on its size. Size goes into the dim.

    Example 7.6 records the kernels in order and tags each with its breadcrumb station by walking up the Python call stack. The order you see is the stage order.

    The launch-shape atlas

    A launch is not always "one thread per world". The dim says how the work is cut into threads, and MJWarp uses a handful of recurring shapes. This chapter owns the launch-shape atlas and introduces the five that appear all over the pipeline; the other three belong to one algorithm each and are added by the chapter that teaches it. The recorded dims in the panel are the real values from Example 7.5.

    Source

    Layout 2 and 8 kernels start with a guard, because the launch is sized for the worst case: collision_primitive.py::_primitive_narrowphase has if tid >= ncollision_in[0]: return, and constraint.py::_efc_contact_init has if conid >= nacon_in[0]: return, then reserves a contiguous block of rows in its own world with wp.atomic_add(nefc_out, worldid, ndim). Layout 6 kernels loop for efcid in range(tid, nefc, wp.block_dim()): lane tid of the world's block takes rows tid, tid + block_dim, ... (solver.py::_linesearch_iterative_kernel). A comment in solver.py notes that wp.block_dim() is 1 on the CPU backend, so there lane 0 covers every row.

    9Where the time goes: timing stages, event_scope and EventTracer

    The simplest way to time a stage is a stopwatch with a synchronize on both sides. Kernel launches return to Python immediately on a GPU (the work is queued), so without wp.synchronize() you would time the queueing, not the work. Example 7.8 times the five stages and the Euler integrator.

    What holds on every run: at nworld=8 on CPU, step time does not scale with nv. The humanoid (27 DOFs) and the playground (13 DOFs) are within CPU run-to-run noise of each other, and in both fwd_position and solve take the largest shares. Small-nworld CPU timings are dominated by the fixed cost of each kernel launch, not by the physics, and they are noisy: on a shared machine the same script can give a different ratio between the two models each time, which is why the script reports medians. Keep that in mind for §10.

    @event_scope and EventTracer

    Most pipeline functions carry the decorator warp_util.event_scope. When no tracer is active it just calls the function (one if of overhead). Inside a warp_util.EventTracer it records a CUDA event before and after the call and nests the results, so you get a tree: step > forward > fwd_position > fwd_kinematics > kinematics... tracer.trace() returns {name: ((ms per call, ...), {child: ...})}. Only one tracer can run at a time, and scopes inside capture_while/capture_if bodies are not recorded.

    CUDA only

    Constructing an EventTracer works on CPU, but the first event_scope inside it creates wp.Event(enable_timing=True) and fails with RuntimeError: Device cpu is not a CUDA device. _src/cli.py shows the enabled= switch (driven by its --event_trace flag); the course passes enabled=ON_GPU so the tracer is off on CPU and the trace is empty. private API EventTracer lives in mujoco_warp._src.warp_util and may change between versions.

    10A teaser: CUDA graphs

    You now know the cost structure of an eager step: about 100 launches, each one a Python call, argument packing and a driver call, a few microseconds each, before the GPU does any work. For a small model at moderate nworld the GPU finishes each kernel faster than Python can issue the next, so it sits idle between launches. The solver adds a second cost: deciding whether to run another iteration needs the "how many worlds are still solving" counter, which lives on the GPU, so an eager solver loop copies it to the host and waits, every iteration.

    A CUDA graph removes both costs. You run one step under wp.ScopedCapture(): the launches are recorded instead of executed. Afterwards one call, wp.capture_launch(graph), replays all of them, with no Python in between. And the solver loop becomes a conditional node: solver.py calls wp.capture_while(nsolving, while_body=_solver_iteration, ...), so the GPU itself repeats the iteration until nsolving, the number of unconverged worlds, reaches 0.

    Remember this line

    A CUDA graph records launches once and replays them; conditional nodes loop on device. Two consequences you already know from this chapter: Python-level choices (integrator, disable flags, which kernels exist) are frozen into the graph, and arrays must be changed in place (.assign()), never replaced, because the graph holds their addresses.

    11Part I checkpoint: a 10k bouncing-balls dashboard

    Time to use everything from Part I in one program. The task: drop 10,240 balls (64 on CPU) from random heights with random velocities, simulate 2 seconds, and report per-world statistics, all without pulling data to the host inside the loop. The ingredients:

    • one Model, a batched Data with explicit contact budgets (nconmax=2, njmax=8: one contact with a 3-D pyramidal cone is 4 rows) (Ch 5, Ch 6);
    • per-world initial state written with .assign() (Ch 6);
    • a custom kernel count_bounces, one thread per world, that detects "falling, then rising, at the floor" on the device (Ch 4), launched right after each mjw.step, which is exactly where your own work plugs into the pipeline;
    • a second tiny kernel that stores every 10th height into a preallocated (frames, nworld) array, so the only device-to-host copies happen after the loop (Ch 5);
    • an overflow check and a world-steps/s number labeled with the device it came from.

    The ball is the M1 model with a bouncier contact: solref="0.02 0.1" keeps the default time constant but lowers the damping ratio, so the contact spring gives back much of the energy.

    Read the dashboard like an engineer. The correlation between start height and bounce count is strongly negative (-0.85): a ball dropped from 2 m spends most of its 2 seconds in long flights, while a ball dropped from 0.3 m makes many short hops. The deepest penetration, 1.1 cm for a 10 cm ball, tells you the contact is soft but reasonable. And the world-steps/s number is a CPU number: on Colab, run the notebook, raise NWORLD and watch how much the per-step time grows (on a GPU it grows far slower than linearly until the GPU is full; Ch 25).

    14Summary

    Key takeaways

    • step = forward + integrator. forward recomputes every derived field from the state and inputs; the integrator's _advance moves qpos, qvel, act, time and copies qacc into qacc_warmstart.
    • Stage order in the 4.0.1 wheel: kinematics → crb → collision → make_constraint → [island] → transmission → sensor_pos → velocity → sensor_vel → control callback → actuation → acceleration → solve → [rne_post] → sensor_acc → integrate.
    • Factorize pairing: _forward factors in fwd_acceleration(factorize=True); the public defaults factor in fwd_position(). One factorization per qpos.
    • qfrc_smooth = qfrc_passive - qfrc_bias + qfrc_actuator + qfrc_applied + JT xfrc_applied, to the last bit; then M qacc_smooth = qfrc_smooth and the solver adds qfrc_constraint.
    • m.opt.disableflags and m.opt.integrator are Python ints read at dispatch: change them freely in eager mode, recapture graphs after.
    • About 100 launches per step (95 + 5 tiled for the playground, 89 + 6 for the humanoid); count depends on features, size goes into the dim. Five pipeline-wide shapes: (world, element) grids, flat pool slots, tiled block-per-world, 3-D grids, row per contact.
    • Time stages with wp.synchronize() on both sides; EventTracer gives a nested GPU-only tree (guard with enabled=ON_GPU); CUDA graphs remove per-launch overhead and let the solver loop on the device.

    Next, Ch 8 uses the control-callback socket and the step1/step2 split, and Ch 9 opens the collision and constraint stations to size their buffers.
