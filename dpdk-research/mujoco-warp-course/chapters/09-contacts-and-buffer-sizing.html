    Part II · Chapter 9

    Contacts, Constraint Budgets and Overflow

    Every contact of every world lands in one shared, fixed-size pool, and every contact becomes a few rows in a fixed-size per-world constraint table. When either fills up, MJWarp does not stop: it drops the extra contacts, sets a bit, and keeps simulating, so boxes sink through floors without an error. This chapter shows how to read contacts, how the budgets work, how to decode the overflow bits, and how to size the buffers from measurements instead of guesses.

    • Detect and decode overflow with d.overflow and mjw.OverflowType; recognise silent dropping and starvation.
    • Size buffers from a measurement rollout and estimate the memory they cost.
    • Compute contact forces with mjw.contact_force.

    Before you start: recap

    • mjw.step runs fwd_position = kinematics, crb, collision, make_constraint, then velocity, actuation, acceleration, the solver and the integrator (Ch 7 §2, the pipeline breadcrumb).
    • Most Data arrays are shaped (nworld, ...), but a few are pooled across worlds and tagged with a worldid (Ch 6 §8, pooled-array intuition).
    • wp.atomic_add on an integer counter hands each thread a unique slot, in no fixed order on a GPU (Ch 4 §9).
    • solref = (timeconst, dampratio) of a spring-damper; solimp shapes impedance; contacts are soft, so a resting body penetrates a little (Ch 2 §4, Ch 3 §8).
    • A kernel launch has a fixed dim; threads past the useful range return early but still cost a launch slot (Ch 7 §8, launch-shape atlas layouts 2 and 8).

    1Where contacts come from

    A contact in MuJoCo is a record that says "these two geoms are this close, here, along this direction". MJWarp makes contacts in two stages inside fwd_position, right after kinematics has placed every geom:

    1. The broadphase finds candidate geom pairs whose bounding volumes overlap. Each candidate takes one slot in a scratch list; the counter is d.ncollision.
    2. The narrowphase runs the exact geometry test for each candidate and writes 0, 1 or several contacts (a box on a plane gives 4, one per corner) into d.contact; the counter is d.nacon.

    Then make_constraint turns each touching contact into a few constraint rows in the per-world table d.efc, whose counter per world is d.nefc. The solver computes a force for every row; mjw.contact_force turns the rows of one contact back into a force. Figure 1 shows the flow and the three capacities you choose when you call make_data.

    Recall from Ch 7

    The collision and constraint stations sit between crb and transmission in the breadcrumb above. With sleeping enabled, collision runs twice (a full pass and an incremental pass for newly woken bodies); Ch 21 covers that.

    Pitfall: overflow is reported by step, not by forward

    The NEFC, BROADPHASE and NARROWPHASE checks live in the _next_time kernel that forward.py::_advance launches at the end of the integrator. Calling only mjw.forward (or mjw.collision) on an undersized Data drops contacts but leaves d.overflow at 0. Verified on CPU: the six-box scene of §6 with nconmax=4, njmax=8 gives nacon=[32] and overflow=[0 0] after forward, and the bits appear after one step. After a bare forward, compare the counters with the capacities yourself. (Bits set inside collision, such as CCD, HFIELD, EPA_HORIZON and the flex collision checks, can appear after forward.)

    2The Contact struct and the shared pool

    d.contact is a mjw.Contact dataclass (types.py::Contact). Each field is one Warp array, and every array has the same first dimension: naconmax. There is no world axis. Instead the contacts of all worlds sit side by side in one flat contact pool, and each contact remembers its world in worldid. The table lists every field, with the shape and meaning from the docstring.

    Pitfall: never write flex/elem/vert without a flex

    make_data allocates these three with shape (0,) when m.nflex == 0. A custom kernel that writes them anyway writes out of bounds: it segfaults the CPU process and silently corrupts GPU memory. Use wp.config.mode = "debug" while you develop such kernels.

    Reading the pool

    Example 9.1 builds a sphere, a box and a capsule on a plane in 4 worlds. World 2 lifts the box, so it has 3 contacts instead of 7. The table is sorted by slot index (cid), not by world.

    ContactType lives in mujoco_warp._src.types and is not exported by mujoco_warp, hence the private-API badge: the import path may change in a later release.

    Three things to notice in that output:

    • The counters are global. d.nacon and d.ncollision have shape (1,): one number for all worlds. d.nefc has shape (nworld,).
    • efc_address restarts at 0 in every world, because each world has its own row table. The world-0 sphere and the world-1 sphere both own rows 0..3, of different tables.
    • On CPU the pool looks sorted by world. That is an accident of the CPU running threads in order. On a GPU, threads reserve slots in whatever order they reach the atomic_add, so worlds interleave and the order changes from step to step. Never rely on slot order.

    How a contact gets its slot

    The narrowphase kernels all end in the same helper, collision_core.py::write_contact. It decides whether the contact counts (dist < margin + gap, a strict <), reserves a slot with an integer atomic add on d.nacon, and writes the fields only if the slot is below naconmax. The counter keeps counting past the end, so d.nacon can be larger than the pool. Step through it with three worlds and a pool of 6 slots:

    Grouping contacts per world

    Almost every use of contacts (rewards, observations, sizing) starts by grouping the pool by world. Read the counter once, clip it to the capacity, slice every field to [:n], and group by worldid. np.bincount counts per world; np.minimum.at is the unbuffered "min per group" that handles repeated indices correctly (plain fancy-index assignment would keep only the last value per world).

    Tip: keep it on the device

    The NumPy version reads the pool to the host, which is fine for analysis. Inside a training loop, do the same grouping in a Warp kernel with one thread per pool slot and an atomic add per world. That is Exercise 9.2.

    Version note: deterministic contact order

    m.opt.deterministic with DeterminismType.CONTACTS asks the driver to sort the pool. In the 4.0.1 wheel collision_driver._sort_contacts is a stub (pass, "TODO(team): Implementation contact sorting"), so the order stays as the atomics left it. The dev tree has a real sort in sort.py (dev tree only, not in 4.0.1). Ch 22 covers determinism.

    3Which way the normal points

    Two rules fix the geometry of every contact:

    1. geom[0] has the lower GeomType. The broadphase stores each pair with the lower type first (collision_driver.py::_add_geom_pair: if type1 > type2 swap). The order of geoms in the XML and their ids do not matter.
    2. frame[0], the normal, points from geom[0] to geom[1] (the docstring of Contact.frame). Moving geom[1] along +normal separates the pair, and a positive normal force pushes geom[1] along +normal.

    GeomType order: PLANE 0, HFIELD 1, SPHERE 2, CAPSULE 3, ELLIPSOID 4, CYLINDER 5, BOX 6, MESH 7, SDF 8. Example 9.3 lists the ball before the plane, and puts a small sphere ("pebble") on top of a box with a higher geom id. The pebble still comes first, because SPHERE < BOX, so the normal of that pair points down.

    4Friction cones, rows per contact and contact forces

    What is a friction cone?

    A contact can push (normal force f_n \ge 0) and it can resist sliding with friction (tangential force f_t). Coulomb's law caps friction: |f_t| \le \mu f_n. Draw every allowed total force as an arrow from the contact point and you get a cone around the normal with half-angle \arctan \mu, the friction cone. If the force needed to hold an object still fits inside the cone, it sticks; if not, it slides.

    Try it in Figure 6: a block rests on a slope of angle \theta. To stay still, the floor must push straight up against gravity, which is \theta away from the normal. The block sticks while \tan\theta \le \mu.

    How many rows does a contact cost?

    The solver cannot handle a round cone directly in its row-by-row form, so MuJoCo offers two encodings, chosen with <option cone="pyramidal|elliptic"/> (m.opt.cone):

    • Pyramidal (the default) replaces the cone by a pyramid with edges n \pm \mu_i t_i. Each edge is one row with a non-negative force, so a contact with condim c > 1 costs 2(c-1) rows.
    • Elliptic keeps the real cone and costs c rows (one normal row and one per friction direction).
    \text{rows}(c) = \begin{cases} 1 & c = 1 \\ 2(c-1) & \text{pyramidal} \\ c & \text{elliptic} \end{cases}

    with condim c \in \{1, 3, 4, 6\}.

    So the default condim 3 contact costs 4 rows. That single fact explains most NEFC overflows: a box resting on a plane has 4 contacts and costs 16 rows. Treat the formula as a black box for now; Ch 19 derives it. The size of contact.efc_address follows from it: nmaxpyramid = max(1, 2 * (nmaxcondim - 1)) (io.py::make_data), where nmaxcondim is the largest condim in the model.

    Rows are reserved per world in constraint.py::_efc_contact_init, a kernel launched with one thread per pool slot (dim=d.naconmax). Each thread skips empty slots, non-constraint contacts and contacts that are not yet touching (dist >= includemargin), then reserves ndim consecutive rows of its own world with wp.atomic_add(nefc_out, worldid, ndim). Rows past njmax get efc_address = -1:

    From contacts to rows (dense Jacobian path, simplified)

    constraint.py::_efc_contact_initIllustrative data
    conid = wp.tid()

  return

    The real kernel also handles adhesion, the sparse Jacobian (it first reserves non-zeros with _reserve_efc_nnz, which can set NJMAX_NNZ) and the Newton block bookkeeping; the pseudo-code keeps only the dense pyramidal path. Like nacon, d.nefc[w] keeps counting past njmax.

    Contact forces: mjw.contact_force

    After the solver, each row has a force in d.efc.force. mjw.contact_force(m, d, contact_ids, to_world_frame, force) (support.py::contact_force) launches one thread per requested contact id and writes a wp.spatial_vector per id: force (3) then torque (3). For a pyramidal contact it decodes the edge forces (_decode_pyramid): the normal force is the sum of all edge forces and each friction component is \mu_i (f_{2i} - f_{2i+1}). The normal component then has the contact's adhesion subtracted (contact_force_fn reports the net interface force). With to_world_frame=True it rotates the result out of the contact frame. Ids at or past nacon are skipped, so the output array keeps whatever it held there: start from wp.zeros. In the 4.0.1 wheel the kernel only skips ids >= nacon, never checking the pool size, so after a pool overflow pass ids below min(nacon, naconmax), as Example 9.4 does with arange(naconmax); newer source also clips to the pool size.

    Example 9.4 settles the PLAYGROUND scene and checks Newton's third law: the floor must hold up the 1 kg ball with m g = 9.81 N and the 8 kg box (density 1000, 0.2 m cube) with 78.48 N. It also copies world 0 into an MjData with get_data_into, which keeps only that world's contacts, and compares with C MuJoCo's mj_contactForce.

    Note: what get_data_into exports

    io.py::get_data_into clips nacon to naconmax and nefc to njmax ("nacon and nefc can overflow"), then keeps the pool slots whose worldid equals world_id and whose type has the CONSTRAINT or PASSIVE bit. So mjd.ncon is the per-world count (5 here: 1 ball + 4 box corners), never the pool size (40).

    Pitfall: nconmax is per world, but it is not a per-world guarantee

    The docstring says it plainly: "Contacts exist in large heterogeneous arrays: one world may have more than nconmax contacts." nconmax only multiplies into the shared pool size. njmax is the opposite: "no world may have more than njmax constraints". §6 shows what that difference does.

    The default ladder

    If you pass nothing, _default_nconmax and _default_njmax make a guess from the model and round it up to the next value of a fixed ladder of "valid sizes" 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128, ... 8192 ((2 + k % 2) * 2 ** (k // 2 + 3)):

    nconmax = max(nv * 0.35 * (nhfield > 0) * 10 + 45, 256 * has_flex, 64 * has_sdf, mjd.ncon if mjd else 0)
njmax   = max(nv * 2.26 * (nhfield > 0) * 18 + 53, 512 * has_flex, 256 * has_sdf, mjd.nefc if mjd else 0)

    Read the first term carefully: without a heightfield it is just 45 and 53, so every model without a heightfield, flex or SDF geom gets nconmax=48, njmax=64, whatever its size (an SDF geom lifts the floor to 64 and 256, a flex to 256 and 512). A humanoid and a single ball get the same budget. With a heightfield the guess jumps (the TERRAIN model of the stretch exercise gets 192 and 1536). put_data passes the host mjd, so a settled MjData raises the defaults to at least its ncon/nefc; a fresh one has 0 contacts and does not help. The docstring is honest about all of this: the guess "may need to be manually raised if MJWarp reports ncon overflow, or lowered in order to get the very best performance."

    Padding: what is really allocated

    Two more numbers come from io.py::_get_padded_sizes. Dense constraint arrays are padded for the tiled kernels: njmax_pad = round_up(njmax, 16). The DOF dimension is padded too: nv_pad = round_up(nv, 4) when nv <= 48 (one-tile Cholesky), else round_up(nv + 1, 16) with Newton (the +1 is an augmented row) or round_up(nv, 16) without. The dense Jacobian d.efc.J is (nworld, njmax_pad, nv_pad); the sparse one (used when nv > 32 under jacobian=AUTO, io.py::is_sparse) is (nworld, 1, njmax_nnz) plus J_rownnz/J_rowadr/J_colind.

    6Starvation: a shared pool is not fair

    Read ahead: the overflow bits

    d.overflow holds one bitmask per world. The bit names used here mean: BROADPHASE, more candidate pairs than pool slots; NARROWPHASE, more contacts than pool slots; NEFC, more rows than njmax in that world. The two pool bits are set in every world at once. §7 decodes all of the bits and shows where each is set.

    Six boxes rest on a plane in two identical worlds: 12 box-plane pairs and 48 contacts. Example 9.5 gives them nconmax=4 (a pool of 8 slots) and njmax=8. Watch the counters:

    • The broadphase finds 12 pairs but only 8 fit (ncollision=12 > 8: BROADPHASE).
    • Those 8 pairs make 32 contacts, of which 8 are stored (nacon=32 > 8: NARROWPHASE).
    • On CPU all 8 stored contacts belong to world 0. They ask for 32 rows, 8 fit (nefc[0]=32 > 8: NEFC). World 1 gets no contacts and no rows at all: its boxes fall through the floor.

    Note what was not shared: rows. World 1 could have used its own 8 rows, but it never got a contact to put in them. The pool is first come, first served. On a GPU "first" is decided by thread timing, so the victims change from step to step and from run to run: the simulation is wrong and nondeterministic at the same time.

    Overflow is silent

    No exception is raised and the simulation keeps running. The only signals are a wp.printf line from the device (which you may not see, §10) and the bits in d.overflow. A training run with an undersized pool learns on worlds where boxes fall through floors. Check d.overflow.

    7Detecting and decoding overflow

    d.overflow is an int array of shape (nworld,). Each world's value is a bitmask of mjw.OverflowType flags (types.py::OverflowType, an enum.IntFlag). A kernel that runs out of room ORs its bit into the world it was working on; nothing ever clears a bit except mjw.reset_data. The bits are sticky: they tell you "this happened at some point since the last reset", not "this is happening now".

    Where each bit comes from (rows are generated from the enum members in the ex6 dump; the source column is the kernel that sets the bit in the 4.0.1 wheel):

    BitValueSet inMeaning

    Three of them, the ones this chapter is about, are checked in one place for rigid models: the _next_time kernel built by forward.py::_next_time_builder, launched with dim=d.nworld at the end of every step. (A model with a flex also sets BROADPHASE and NARROWPHASE per world inside collision_flex.py, during collision.) NEFC compares the world's own nefc with njmax. BROADPHASE and NARROWPHASE compare the global counters with naconmax, so they are set in every world at once, even in a world with no contacts. Only world 0 prints the message for those two:

    Decoding: generate the catalogue, never hand-write it

    A hand-written list of bit names goes stale the day a release adds a bit (the enum has grown to 12 bits; LS_ITERATIONS and TACTILE are the newest two). OverflowType is an IntFlag, so loop over it. Since Python 3.11, iterating a flag class yields only the single-bit members: NONE (0) and the alias ALL are skipped.     Tip: check periodically, not every step

    Reading d.overflow syncs the device. In a training loop, check it every N steps or at the end of each episode; the bits are sticky, so nothing is missed. After a reset (reset_data clears the bit of every reset world) the next check starts clean.

    8A sizing recipe

    Defaults are a guess and a guess is either too small (wrong physics) or too big (wasted memory and time). Measure instead:

    1. Measure the worst case. Run a representative rollout with generous budgets (or run C MuJoCo on a few starts) and record, per world, the largest contact count (np.bincount of contact.worldid) and the largest nefc.
    2. Add margin. 25% is a sensible start; more if your randomization can make scenes denser than the measured ones.
    3. Round up to the ladder (optional, but it keeps sizes tidy and matches what the defaults do).
    4. Check periodically in the real run: d.overflow.numpy().any() or describe_overflow(d).

    The recommendation nconmax=32 is smaller than the default 48 while njmax=128 is twice the default 64: the defaults are wrong in both directions for this model. C MuJoCo's unperturbed tower peaks at 20 contacts and 80 rows. Seven of the eight perturbed MJWarp worlds reach the same 20/80, but world 0 reaches 23/92, which is why we size from the randomized rollout and not from the nominal scene.

    Measuring at scale: mjwarp-testspeed --measure_alloc

    The benchmark CLI has a flag for this: --measure_alloc "print a report of contacts and constraints per step" (testspeed.py), and --nconmax/--njmax/--njmax_nnz override the sizes (_src/cli.py). It refuses to run on CPU (ValueError: testspeed available for gpu only, testspeed.py) GPU only; Ch 25 uses it.

    9What oversizing costs: memory and time

    Why not just pass nconmax=1024, njmax=4096 and forget about it? Two costs grow with the budgets, not with the real counts:

    • Memory. The dense Jacobian is nworld * njmax_pad * nv_pad floats, the pool is a fixed number of bytes per slot times naconmax, and Newton's Hessian workspace is nworld * nv_pad^2 floats, plus an equal-size hfactor when nv > 48 (unless inplace_cholesky aliases it) (allocated per solve call, not part of Data).
    • Time. Many kernels launch over the capacity, not the count: the primitive narrowphase (collision_primitive.py::primitive_narrowphase, dim=d.naconmax, atlas layout 2), _efc_contact_init (dim=d.naconmax, layout 8) and many solver kernels (dim=(d.nworld, d.njmax)). Threads past nacon or nefc return at once, but they still have to be scheduled, so the cost follows the budget, not the scene.

    Example 9.9 turns the memory part into formulas, checks them against the real allocation, and extrapolates. Warp arrays have no .nbytes; the example uses a.size * wp.types.type_size_in_bytes(a.dtype) and walks the nested Data dataclass.

    The estimate matches the allocation to the byte, and the dense Jacobian dominates: 41% of the whole Data at njmax=128. Every doubling of njmax doubles it.

    10Silencing warnings without losing the bits

    The overflow messages are wp.printf calls inside kernels. On a GPU they are flushed at sync points; on CPU they are C printf, which buffers separately from Python's print. In a script, they can appear long after the Python lines around them (in our runs: all at exit); in Jupyter they may show up late or only in the runtime's log. And with thousands of worlds, NEFC prints once per overflowing world per step.

    m.opt.warn_overflow is a bitmask of the same OverflowType flags, default ALL (4095, set in io.py::put_model). It only gates the printing; the bits in d.overflow are always recorded. Clear one bit to silence one message, or set it to 0 to silence all. The setter (types.py::Option.warn_overflow) also takes a bool: False means 0, True means ALL. Example 9.10 flushes the C buffer after every step, so the text appears in the real order:

    Pitfall: changing warn_overflow recompiles

    Kernels read it through wp.static (forward.py: _next_time_builder(int(m.opt.warn_overflow)), and the same pattern in collision, solver, island and sensor kernels), so every new value builds new kernel variants: a JIT pause on the next step. Set it once, before the first step and before any CUDA graph capture (a captured graph keeps the variant it recorded). Ch 24's knob board lists it with the other compile-time options.

    11Not every contact is a constraint: ContactType bits

    contact.type is a bitmask of ContactType (types.py::ContactType): CONSTRAINT = 1 (a contact for the solver), SENSOR = 2 (for a collision sensor: GEOMDIST, GEOMNORMAL, GEOMFROMTO) and PASSIVE = 4 (a passive flex contact for the discrete integrator's effective metric, Ch 23). write_contact sets CONSTRAINT when the pair may collide and is within margin + gap, and SENSOR when a collision sensor asked for this pair. A pair that only a sensor wants (for example two geoms whose contype/conaffinity exclude each other, or that are far apart) still lands in the pool, with SENSOR alone.

    Sensor contacts matter for sizing: they occupy pool slots (count them in nconmax) but produce no rows (leave them out of njmax). Example 9.11 adds two <distance> sensors:

    The ball-ball contact has a positive dist (0.3 m apart) and efc_address = -1: the sensor reads its distance, the solver never sees it. The floor-ball_a contact carries both bits: one record serves the solver and the sensor.

    The last field of the struct, surfacevel, is filled only when some geom has a surface velocity (m.flg_surfacevel): collision_driver.py::_contact_surfacevel runs after the narrowphase over dim=d.naconmax and writes (tangent1, tangent2, spin) from m.geom_surfacevel (a conveyor belt, for example). Ch 16 has an example. Last, m.callback.contactfilter, if set, runs right after that, so your own kernel can edit or drop contacts before make_constraint.

    12Check yourself

    Quiz

    What shapes do d.nacon and d.nefc have?

    One global counter for the shared pool; one row counter per world for the per-world efc tables.

    A world has 5 resting contacts with condim 3 and the default pyramidal cone. How many efc rows do they need?

    Pyramidal: 2(c-1) = 4 rows per condim-3 contact, so 20.

    You call make_data(mjm, nworld=1024, nconmax=16). Can world 7 hold 40 contacts?

    naconmax = 16 * 1024 is shared. "One world may have more than nconmax contacts" (docstring), and one busy world can also starve the others.

    A collision sensor makes a contact with only the SENSOR bit. What does it cost?

    Count sensor contacts in nconmax, not in njmax (Example 9.11).

    After step 160 of Example 9.6, nefc = 16 < njmax = 64, but d.overflow still has NEFC. Why?

    The kernels only OR bits in; reset_data sets overflow_out[worldid] = 0.

    The pool overflows (nacon > naconmax). Which worlds get the NARROWPHASE bit?

    _next_time compares the global nacon with naconmax in every world's thread.

    You only call mjw.forward on a Data with a too-small pool, for a rigid model of primitive geoms (no flex, mesh or heightfield). What is in d.overflow?

    Contacts are still dropped. (Bits set during collision itself, such as CCD, HFIELD, EPA_HORIZON and the flex checks, can appear after forward.) Compare d.nacon with d.naconmax yourself after a bare forward.

    Which statement about m.opt.warn_overflow &= ~mjw.OverflowType.NEFC is true?

    Set it before the first step and before graph capture.

                   worldid: wp.array(dtype=int), counts: wp.array(dtype=int)):

    14Summary

    Key takeaways

    • d.contact is one flat pool of naconmax slots shared by all worlds; each contact has a worldid. Read n = min(d.nacon[0], d.naconmax) and slice [:n].
    • geom[0] has the lower GeomType and the normal frame[0] points from geom[0] to geom[1].
    • A condim-c contact costs 2(c-1) rows (pyramidal) or c (elliptic) in its world's table of njmax rows.
    • Defaults are nconmax=48, njmax=64 for every model without a heightfield, flex or SDF geom: often too small for rows, too big for contacts. Measure, add 25%, round up to the ladder.
    • Overflow drops contacts or rows silently, sets sticky OverflowType bits at the end of step (BROADPHASE/NARROWPHASE in every world), and can starve whole worlds.
    • Decode bits by looping over mjw.OverflowType; silence prints with m.opt.warn_overflow (a recompile); only reset_data clears bits.
    • Oversizing costs memory (J is nworld * njmax_pad * nv_pad floats) and launch time; mjw.contact_force turns rows back into forces.

    The running project gains describe_overflow(d); the snapshot after this chapter is examples/09-contacts-and-buffer-sizing/my_env_step09.py (the notebook writes it as my_env.py).
