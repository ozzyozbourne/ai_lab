    Capstone: GPU-Native Reinforcement Learning at Scale

    Everything in this course meets here. You will build reinforcement-learning environments that live entirely on the device: physics substeps, rewards, terminations, resets and domain randomization are recorded once into a CUDA graph and replayed with one call per control step, while a PyTorch policy reads the observations through zero-copy views.     Before you start: recap

    • mjw.reset_data(m, d, reset=mask) resets only the worlds whose mask entry is True and does not recompute derived fields (Ch 8 §1); a done-kernel plus a masked reset is the auto-reset pattern (Ch 8 §10).
    • Fields without derived quantities (friction, gains, damping) can be re-drawn inside a graph by a kernel; mass needs mjw.set_const, and its derived outputs must be batched too (Ch 10 §11, §7).
    • A CUDA graph records launches once and replays them; it bakes in array addresses and Python-level decisions, so you change contents in place and never replace arrays (Ch 24 §3).
    • wp.to_torch(arr) is a zero-copy view of the same memory; actions go back with an in-place copy so d.ctrl keeps its address (Ch 28 §1, §3).
    • Contacts live in one shared pool of naconmax slots, rows in per-world blocks of njmax; size both from a measurement rollout and check d.overflow (Ch 9 §8).

    1RL in 15 minutes

    Reinforcement learning (RL) trains a controller by trial and error. The problem is written as a Markov decision process (MDP): at each control step the environment is in a state s_t, the agent picks an action a_t, the environment moves to s_{t+1} and returns a scalar reward r_t. The agent is a policy \pi_\theta(a \mid s), here a small neural network with parameters \theta. For us the "environment" is MJWarp: the state is qpos, qvel and friends, the action is written into d.ctrl, and the transition is one or more mjw.step calls.

    Return, discount and value

    The agent wants a large return G_t = r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \gamma^2 r_{t+2} + \dots. The discount \gamma < 1 makes rewards far in the future count less; a rough rule is that the agent "looks ahead" about 1/(1-\gamma) steps. The value function V(s) predicts the expected return from state s; PPO learns it with a second network (the critic). Play with \gamma below: at a control period of 0.02 s, a 4-second episode has 200 steps.

    Advantage and GAE, in one picture

    To improve the policy we ask, for each action taken: was it better or worse than usual from that state? That is the advantage A_t. The one-step estimate is the TD error \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t): "what I got plus what I expect next, minus what I expected now". Generalized advantage estimation (GAE) blends many such estimates with a second factor \lambda:

    A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\delta_{t+1} + (\gamma\lambda)^2\,\delta_{t+2} + \dots

    which is computed backwards in time with one line per step:

    A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,A_{t+1}

    Section 7 steps through this recursion on a real buffer, including what happens at an episode boundary.

    On-policy sampling: why thousands of worlds

    PPO is on-policy: every update uses samples collected by the current policy, and they are thrown away after the update. One PPO iteration needs T \times N fresh transitions (here T = 32 steps in each of N worlds). With N = 4096 that is 131,072 transitions per iteration, collected in 32 batched control steps. This is why a simulator that steps thousands of worlds in one launch changes what RL can do.

    With thousands of worlds, episodes end at different times in different worlds: they are asynchronous. Waiting for all worlds to finish would waste most of the batch, so each world is reset on its own, inside the step, on the device. That is the job of the masked reset from Ch 8.

    Pitfall: negative rewards and early termination

    If every step costs reward (say r = -x²), the fastest way to a high return is to end the episode: crash the cart into the rail end. Our first swing-up reward did exactly that in Example 30.13 (the ES baseline learned to terminate at once). Keep per-step rewards non-negative in the states you want the agent to stay in, or add an explicit failure penalty.

    3Environment architecture: one graph per control step

    The rule of a GPU-native env is simple: no host round trip inside the training loop. The policy runs on the GPU in PyTorch, the env runs on the GPU in Warp, and they share memory. One env.step(action) is: an in-place copy of the action tensor into a Warp buffer, then wp.capture_launch(graph). The graph holds every kernel of the step, including the reset.

    Recall from Ch 7 and Ch 24

    A CUDA graph records launches once and replays them; conditional nodes loop on device (Ch 7 §10). What a graph bakes in: array addresses, launch dims, and every decision Python took while recording (integrator, solver, iteration counts, flags). What stays live: array contents (Ch 24 §3).

    Three consequences shape the env code:

    • Allocate everything before capture. Every buffer (action, obs, done masks, seeds, counters) is a member created in __init__. The seed is a one-element device array, so a replay reads its current value.
    • Branch on the device, not in Python. "Reset only the finished worlds" is a mask the kernels read; Python never asks "is anyone done?" (that would be a sync and a decision baked into the graph).
    • Write in place. Actions are copied into env.action; domain randomization writes rows of m.actuator_gainprm; mass DR assigns into m.body_mass. No array is ever replaced after capture.

    4Implementing CartpoleEnv

    The env is built from the running project my_env.py (snapshot my_env_step30.py): the Ch 8 reset pattern, Ch 9 describe_overflow, the Ch 10 seeding scheme, Ch 24 needs_conditional_fallback and the capture idiom, and the Ch 26 habit of writing small per-world kernels. Chapter 30 adds scale_body_mass (eager mass DR, Section 9) to my_env.py; the environments themselves live in ex1_env.py and ex10_humanoid_env.py, which the other examples import.

    Reading the code

    • Sizing. All geoms have contype=conaffinity=0, so there are no contacts: nconmax=0. The only constraint rows are the slider's two limits (range="-1.8 1.8"), and in one world at most one of them can be active at a time: njmax=2 is already generous.
    • Batched fields. put_model(batch_sizes=...) gives one row per world to the fields the DR kernel writes (actuator_gainprm, dof_damping) and to the fields the eager mass DR writes (body_mass, body_inertia) and the derived fields set_const recomputes (my_env.MASS_DERIVED).
    • Why the gain and not the gear? The force of a motor is gain * ctrl along a moment arm scaled by gear. actuator_gear is listed in the set_const docstring because it feeds actuator_acc0 (INERT0); the actuator length range is a separate call, mjw.set_length_range (Ch 10 §8). The FIXED gain gainprm[0] feeds no set_const output at all. The gain is read at runtime by the actuation and derivative kernels (for example forward.py::_actuator_force, and the implicit velocity-derivative kernels in derivative.py that implicitfast uses), so a new value takes effect on the next step with no derived quantity to recompute. That makes it safe inside the graph.
    • Non-finite guard. MJWarp has no mj_checkPos/mj_checkVel (Ch 8); the reward kernel checks wp.isfinite itself, terminates the world with zero reward and zeroes final_obs so a NaN never reaches the learner.
    • Random streams. wp.rand_init(seed[0], worldid * 7919 + episode[worldid] * 104729): one reproducible stream per (seed, world, episode), the Ch 10 scheme. Changing env.seed's content changes every later draw without recapturing.

    Pitfall we hit while writing this chapter

    The first version of the env batched only body_mass and body_inertia. After scale_body_mass, body_subtreemass of the cart was 1.051 in every world: world 0's value. set_const launches over each output's leading size, so an unbatched output gets one shared row (Ch 10 §7). The physics was still right (the crb pass reads body_mass per world), which is why the bug is easy to miss. scale_body_mass now asserts that every field in MASS_DERIVED is batched.

    Eager vs graph: what one call buys

    Both envs finish the same episodes with the same lengths: the graph replays exactly the launches the eager path issues. The gap in throughput is Python dispatch: an eager env step issues 208 launches from Python (99 per mjw.step, counted by wrapping wp.launch as in Example 24.1), the graph issues one. With only 8 small worlds on Warp's CPU device the gap is extreme; on a GPU with thousands of worlds the kernels themselves take longer, so the ratio is smaller but still large. Run the cell on Colab to see your own numbers.

    5Asynchronous episodes and auto-reset

    Example 30.4 runs 16 worlds with a noisy linear controller whose strength grows with the world index, and a 100-step time limit. Weak controllers let the pole fall (terminated); strong ones survive until the limit (truncated). Each world restarts on its own schedule.

    Two details of the output are worth a second look. First, the device-side counter (n_finished, summed by episode_stats) agrees with the host log: the bookkeeping inside the graph is correct, and a training loop can read it once per iteration instead of every step. Second, every truncated episode has length exactly 100, while terminated episodes have every length (one terminated episode also happened to end at step 100, which is why the histogram counts the two kinds separately): a learner that treated both the same would learn that "something bad happens at step 100".

    Why the reset must be on the device

    A host-side reset would read done back every step (a sync that stalls the GPU queue), then launch a reset whose shape depends on the answer. Inside a graph that is impossible anyway: Python is not running during a replay. A masked kernel costs the same whether 0 or 4,000 worlds are done.

    6A baseline first: evolution strategies in NumPy

    Before reaching for PPO, get a number from the simplest method that can work. Evolution strategies (ES) treat the policy as a black box: perturb the parameters, measure returns, move toward the perturbations that did better. With a batched simulator, each candidate policy gets its own world, so a whole population is evaluated in one rollout. The policy is linear, a = \mathrm{clip}(w^\top o + b): six numbers.

    \theta \leftarrow \theta + \frac{\alpha}{P}\sum_{i=1}^{P}\big(F_i^{+} - F_i^{-}\big)\,\epsilon_i

    where F_i^{\pm} is the centred rank of the return of \theta \pm \sigma\epsilon_i among all candidates.

    Thirty iterations of 32 candidates (a few seconds on the CPU dev box) take the mean episode length from 33 to the 200-step limit in 28 of 32 worlds. Look at the learned weights: the largest are on \dot\theta, \dot x and \sin\theta, all positive like the corresponding LQR gains of Ch 28 (push the cart toward the side the pole leans to). ES is also the CPU verification path of this chapter: it needs no torch and no GPU. Its limits show up later: a linear policy is less robust to heavy poles (Section 9) and cannot swing the pole up (Section 11).

    7Reading the reference PPO

    You do not need to write PPO from scratch. ppo_reference.py is about 200 lines of plain PyTorch that work with any env exposing the CartpoleEnv interface. This section is a guided reading: rollout, GAE, the clipped objective, and minibatch epochs. Read the listing once, then follow the diagram and the two widgets below.

    The details that make it work

    • Views are cloned. env.obs is a zero-copy view that the next env.step overwrites; collect stores copies (buf["obs"][t] = self.obs copies into the buffer, self.obs = obs.clone()).
    • Truncation bootstrap. When trunc is True the reward gets + gamma * V(final_obs), and the step is still marked done so GAE does not leak into the next episode. Terminations get nothing added. The code uses torch.where(trunc, V(final_obs), 0) for two reasons. An if trunc.any(): branch would turn a device tensor into a Python bool, a host sync on every env step, so keep data-dependent Python branches on device tensors out of the loop. And multiplying by trunc.float() would turn a NaN in a non-truncated row into NaN (NaN × 0 = NaN), which GAE and the advantage normalization would spread to the whole batch.
    • Observation normalization with a floor. obs_var + 1e-2 in the denominator: our first version used 1e-8, and training collapsed just after the policy got good, because \cos\theta of a balanced pole is almost constant and its normalized value exploded.
    • Separate gradient clipping. Returns are about 100, so the value loss dwarfs the policy loss; one global clip_grad_norm_ would shrink the policy's gradient to nothing. The reference clips the actor and the critic separately.
    • Hyperparameters. lr = 1e-3, 8 epochs, 4 minibatches, clip 0.2, \gamma = 0.99, \lambda = 0.95, initial action std e^{-1} \approx 0.37. These came from a small sweep on the CPU dev box with 128 worlds; with lr = 3e-4 and 4 epochs the evaluation length stayed below about 80 steps for 50 iterations.

    The clipped objective

    PPO maximizes L = \min\big(\rho A,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,A\big) where \rho = \pi_\theta(a|s) / \pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(a|s). When an action was good (A > 0) the objective stops rewarding more probability once \rho > 1+\epsilon; when it was bad, it stops rewarding less probability once \rho < 1-\epsilon. So eight epochs over the same samples cannot drag the policy far from the one that collected them.

    8Training run, logging and checkpoints

    The three-iteration CPU run only proves the pipeline works end to end. To check that the reference PPO actually learns, we also ran 40 iterations with 128 worlds on CPU (second output box): about 10 s of wall clock and mostly 36-43k env-steps/s on a quiet CPU dev box, with the odd slow iteration when another job woke up. These are CPU numbers, not GPU performance. On Colab the same file runs 150 iterations of 4096 worlds and prints your own throughput.

    What a healthy curve looks like

    • Mean length rises to the time limit (200), then the return keeps creeping up as the policy also centres the cart and uses less effort (the 0.25 x² and 0.01 a² terms).
    • A dip after a peak (iterations 18-26 above) is common: the policy changes faster than the value function. If it never recovers, suspect the value targets (bootstrap), the normalizer or the gradient clipping, in that order.
    • Log once per iteration. env.episode_stats() is one host sync per iteration; per-step logging would put a sync back into the loop you worked to keep on the device.
    • Checkpoints are a state_dict plus the observation normalizer (PPO.save); load_policy restores both, on any device.

    On a GPU

    With 4096 worlds an iteration is 131,072 samples, so 150 iterations are about 20M env steps (40M physics steps). The policy and the env share the GPU, and which of them is the bottleneck depends on your GPU and model size (this page does not measure it: it was verified on a CPU). Check with the profiler before you tune (Ch 25): EventTracer around env.step and torch's profiler around update.

    9Evaluation under randomized physics

    Training randomized the motor gain and the slider damping per episode. Evaluation asks a harder question: does the policy survive physics it never saw? We scale the pole mass from 0.5x to 32x. Mass is the textbook field with derived quantities, so this is the eager path: assign, mjw.set_const, then replay the unchanged graph.

    def scale_body_mass(mjm, m, d, body, scale):        # my_env_step30.py (excerpt)
    ...
    mass[:, body] *= scale
    inertia[:, body] *= scale[:, None]
    m.body_mass.assign(mass)                      # same array objects: only contents change
    m.body_inertia.assign(inertia)
    mjw.set_const(m, d)                           # SetConstGroup.ALL (body_mass needs ALL)

    Why mass DR stays outside the graph

    set_const clones d.qpos (and mocap arrays) into temporaries, runs kinematics at qpos0 in every world, and restores. Allocation during capture and a relaunch of every world per reset are exactly what a graph should not contain. Capturing it has not been verified for this course, so we keep it eager, at rollout boundaries, where its cost is paid once per thousands of steps.

    The cart's subtree mass now differs per world (1.05 to 4.5 kg), proof that set_const wrote one row per world. The PPO network keeps every pole up, even at 32 times the training mass; the linear ES policy already misses a few worlds near the training mass, drops to 50% at 8x and fails everywhere from 16x. Robustness was never in the ES objective, and a six-number linear map has little room for it.

    The sweep varies pole mass only, on purpose. Friction is moot here: the cartpole has no contacts. The motor gain is already randomized in every training episode, so a gain sweep would mostly re-test the training distribution. A gear sweep is the remaining axis: actuator_gear feeds actuator_acc0 through mjw.set_const and actuator_lengthrange through mjw.set_length_range, so it would follow the same eager pattern as the mass DR: batch actuator_gear and its derived outputs (actuator_acc0, and actuator_lengthrange if it is recomputed), write the values, call mjw.set_const, then mjw.set_length_range. It is a good extension, not part of Example 30.7.

    Step 1: measure, then size and choose the solver

    Three findings, each one a Part IV-V lesson in practice:

    • The pool is sized by candidates, not contacts. The worst world had 11 contacts, but the broadphase produced up to 61 candidate pairs over 4 worlds. forward.py::_next_time compares ncollision (a total over all worlds) with naconmax and raises BROADPHASE when it is larger. A first version of this example sized nconmax from contacts only (14 per world, 56 total) and every world reported BROADPHASE. Hence naconmax = 1.25 x max(ncollision, total contacts).
    • Iterations are a runtime knob, cone and solver are not. Changing m.opt.iterations reused the compiled kernels; each solver/cone pair compiled its own. With 3 iterations every world hit the cap at least once (ITERATIONS is sticky until reset); with 10, Newton never did.
    • CG needs more and still struggles here. CG used 7.6 iterations on average where Newton used 2.8-3.5, raised LS_ITERATIONS (its linesearch ran out of its 20 iterations) and is not even faster per step on this box. We keep Newton, pyramidal (the model's default), 10 iterations, and the measured sizes. Elliptic would use fewer rows (21 vs 34) at a higher cost per iteration on a GPU (Ch 20).

    Step 2: the environment

    What is new compared with CartpoleEnv:

    • Overflow monitoring that survives auto-reset. reset_data clears d.overflow of the worlds it resets (its reset_nworld kernel writes 0). With auto-reset inside the graph, a world that overflowed and then fell would hide the evidence. So the graph ORs d.overflow into env.overflow_seen right before the reset, the device printf is turned off (m.opt.warn_overflow = 0; the bits are still recorded), and env.overflow() decodes the sticky copy once per rollout with my_env.describe_overflow.
    • The contact budget, per rollout. Overflow bits say a budget was exceeded; they do not say how close a rollout came. After every substep a small kernel (_track_contacts) keeps device-side running maxima of d.nacon (one counter for all worlds) and of d.nefc (worst world), and sums d.solver_niter. It runs inside the graph, so it costs no host sync; env.contact_stats() reads the four counters once per rollout and zeroes their contents (the graph keeps the same arrays). In the zero-torque run above the falls pushed nefc to 38 of the 43 rows: the 120-step sizing rollout saw at most 34. That is the +25% margin doing its job, and the reason to keep watching the margin during training.
    • What the monitor caught. With zero torque the humanoid collapses, and some worlds raised LS_ITERATIONS on the way down although the 120-step sizing rollout never did. It is a convergence warning (the linesearch stopped early; the step completes), not dropped data like NEFC or BROADPHASE. Decide per bit: raise ls_iterations (compile-time) if it is frequent in states that matter, accept it during falls.
    • Observations from qpos, not from sensors. The env adds three torso sensors with MjSpec.add_sensor and sensor_slices (Ch 14) finds them, but the last lines of the output show why the observation kernel ignores them: mjw.step evaluates sensors inside forward, before the integrator moves the state (1.2820 vs 1.2794), and reset_data zeroes sensordata. Reading qpos gives the post-step state, also right after a reset, with no extra mjw.forward.
    • XBODY vs BODY. A framepos sensor on objtype="body" measures the body's inertial frame (its centre of mass); objtype="xbody" measures the body frame, which for the root body is exactly qpos[0:3]. The first draft used BODY and saw a 4.4 cm "lag" that was really the torso's centre-of-mass offset.

    Step 3: train it

    On the CPU box this only proves the pipeline: two iterations cannot teach balance. On Colab the GPU budget is 300 iterations of 2048 worlds (about 20M env steps). What you may see (a hypothesis: the learning curve was not verified here, only the pipeline): the mean episode length first drops a little (the random policy flails), then climbs if the policy learns to brace with hip and knee torques. Whether this reference PPO reaches the full 10 s within that budget is something your run will tell you; if the length stays flat, tune the reward or the budget before you blame the env. If overflow: shows anything other than convergence bits, re-run Example 30.9 with the trained policy's states (they visit different contacts than random actions) and resize.

    Going deeper: pixel observations from the batch renderer (Ch 27)

    The env can return 64x64 depth images instead of (or next to) the state vector. Create the render context once (mjw.create_render_context(mjm, nworld=N, cam_res=(64, 64), render_rgb=False, render_depth=True, cam_active=[...])), and add to _step_device after the reset: mjw.forward (poses of the reset worlds), mjw.refit_bvh, mjw.render and mjw.get_depth(rc, 0, depth_scale, depth_buf). All four launch on the device, so they can join the graph (Ch 27 ex11 captures render in a graph). The policy then needs a small CNN, and every step costs a render: compare learning speed per wall-clock second, not per step. This is the stretch exercise.

    11Stretch: swing-up, scaling, and the ecosystem

    Scaling study

    On Warp's CPU device, 16 times more worlds gave less than 3 times the throughput in this run, so the bigger population got far fewer ES iterations in the same 4 seconds and a worse result. On a GPU you should see the opposite: env-steps/s keeps rising almost in proportion to nworld until the device is full (Ch 25 shows how to measure where that happens on your GPU), so a bigger population is almost free. This is also the first example where the policy itself runs as a Warp kernel: the whole rollout needs one host sync per iteration.

    Swing-up

    Swing-up needs two behaviours, pumping energy and then balancing, so it is a natural PPO task: CartpoleEnv(N, swingup=True) plugs straight into PPO. Try it on Colab with 4096 worlds and max_steps=300.

    Chapter 30 quiz (8 questions)

    A world hits the 200-step time limit with the pole upright. What should the PPO target for that last step be?

    Truncation is not a failure: the MDP continues, we just stopped watching. Bootstrap from the state the episode ended in, which only final_obs still holds after the in-graph reset.

    Why is the reset of finished worlds done on the device, inside the captured graph?

    The masked reset_data(m, d, reset=done) costs the same whether 0 or all worlds are done, needs no sync, and can be recorded in the graph.

    Why does pole-mass randomization run outside the graph while gain and damping randomization run inside?

    body_subtreemass, dof_invweight0, actuator_acc0 ... depend on mass. set_const clones qpos, runs kinematics at qpos0 and restores: eager work for rollout boundaries.

    After scale_body_mass, every world reports the same body_subtreemass. What went wrong?

    set_const launches over each output's leading size (Ch 10 §7). Batch the derived outputs too (my_env.MASS_DERIVED).

    The humanoid's worst world had 11 contacts, yet BROADPHASE overflow fired with nconmax=14 (4 worlds). Why?

    forward.py::_next_time sets BROADPHASE when ncollision[0] > naconmax. Size the pool from the candidates (61 here), not only the contacts.

    Why does HumanoidStandEnv keep its own overflow_seen array?

    The graph ORs d.overflow into overflow_seen right before the masked reset; env.overflow() decodes it once per rollout.

    Why are the humanoid observations computed from qpos/qvel rather than from d.sensordata?

    So sensors lag by one substep and read 0 after a reset until the next forward/step; qpos is exact and free.

    In collect(), why is self.obs = obs.clone() needed?

    Zero-copy cuts both ways: no copy in, but also no private copy. Anything kept across steps must be cloned (or copied into a buffer).

    Accumulate in a typed local (float(0.0), so Warp lets the loop mutate it) and update prev_action in the same loop. Verified on the CPU dev box: maximum difference 0.0.

    @wp.kernel
def action_rate_penalty(action: wp.array2d(dtype=float), prev_action: wp.array2d(dtype=float),
                        k: float, reward: wp.array(dtype=float)):
    worldid = wp.tid()
    s = float(0.0)
    for i in range(action.shape[1]):
        da = action[worldid, i] - prev_action[worldid, i]
        s += da * da
        prev_action[worldid, i] = action[worldid, i]   # remember for the next step
    reward[worldid] = reward[worldid] - k * s

    In the env, reset prev_action rows of finished worlds to zero in _observe, or the first step of a new episode is penalized for the jump from the old episode's last action.

    15Summary

    Key takeaways

    • An on-device env is one captured graph per control step: action copy, substeps, reward/done kernel, masked reset with in-graph DR, observation kernel. The host only copies actions in and reads statistics once per iteration.
    • Report terminated and truncated separately and keep final_obs: auto-reset overwrites the observation, and truncations need a value bootstrap.
    • Fields without derived quantities (friction, actuator gain, damping) can be re-drawn per episode inside the graph; mass goes through eager set_const at rollout boundaries, with the derived outputs batched.
    • Test reward kernels against NumPy, start with a baseline (ES), then train with the reference PPO; read a learning curve before tuning, and tune the boring parts first (normalizer, clipping, learning rate).
    • Contact-rich tasks need measurement: size naconmax from broadphase candidates and contacts, njmax from rows, choose solver and iterations from a sweep, and monitor overflow in a copy that survives auto-reset.
    • Observe from qpos/qvel when you can: sensors are computed before integration and zeroed by reset_data.
