    Part III · Chapter 12

    Forces: Passive, Springs, Bias, Actuators and Tendons

    Chapter 11 gave you the left side of the equation of motion, M\ddot q. This chapter is about everything on the right side that exists before the constraint solver runs: springs, dampers, gravity compensation, fluid drag, the bias force that RNE computes, actuators and the transmissions that connect them to joints, muscles, tendons and the forces you apply by hand. For every term you will see the kernel that computes it, how it is laid out across worlds, and a CPU run that checks it against a formula or against C MuJoCo.

    Advanced About 120 min· Prerequisites: Ch 11 (also uses Ch 8 and Ch 10)

    You will learn

    • How qfrc_passive splits into qfrc_spring + qfrc_damper + qfrc_gravcomp + qfrc_fluid (+ adhesion), including MuJoCo 4 polynomial springs, actuator damping and plastic springs.
    • How MJWarp runs RNE: the gravity trick, a branch-parallel forward pass and a subtree-sum backward pass, and why cacc/cfrc_ext are only real after rne_postconstraint.
    • The actuator model: dynamics + gain + bias through a transmission, with the sparse actuator_moment layout.
    • MuJoCo 4 actuators in 4.0.1: nu vs nactuator, CtrlInput, the DC motor, and what put_model rejects (PID, SO3).
    • Muscles, fixed and spatial tendons with wrapping, fluid models with wind, and applied forces.

    Before you start: recap

    • The equation of motion is M(q)\,\ddot q + c(q,\dot q) = \tau; MJWarp stores c as qfrc_bias and works with spatial vectors (rotation first, then translation) in the com frame (Ch 11).
    • Kinematics walks the body tree one thread per branch (m.body_branches), and a body's subtree is the contiguous range [b, b + body_subtreenum[b]) (Ch 11 §5-§6).
    • Pipeline stages: fwd_position → fwd_velocity → fwd_actuation → fwd_acceleration → solver → integrator (Ch 7).
    • Control callbacks (m.callback.control) and step1/step2 let your code write d.ctrl inside a step (Ch 8).
    • Batched model fields are read as x[worldid % x.shape[0]]; replacing m.opt.density with an nworld array gives each world its own fluid (Ch 10).
    • Derived constants such as actuator_acc0 and actuator_lengthrange are computed by set_const (Ch 10 §6); this chapter says what they mean.

    1The force ledger

    Before the solver adds contact and limit forces, MJWarp adds up every other generalized force in one kernel, forward.py::_qfrc_smooth (one thread per (world, dof)):

    \texttt{qfrc\_smooth} = \texttt{qfrc\_passive} - \texttt{qfrc\_bias} + \texttt{qfrc\_actuator} + \texttt{qfrc\_applied} + \sum_b J_b^\top\,\texttt{xfrc\_applied}_b

    and then solves M\,\ddot q_\text{smooth} = \texttt{qfrc\_smooth} for qacc_smooth, the acceleration the system would have with no constraints. The last term is added by support.py::xfrc_accumulate, which maps each body's Cartesian wrench to joint space. Each of the other four terms is computed by a different part of the pipeline, at a different stage. Figure 1 is the map for this chapter: hover, tap or tab through it.

    The order matters. fwd_position computes tendon lengths and Jacobians (smooth.tendon, inside fwd_kinematics) and, at its end, actuator lengths and moments (smooth.transmission). fwd_velocity then computes actuator_velocity and ten_velocity from those Jacobians, the body velocities (com_vel), all passive forces, and the bias force (rne, then tendon_bias). fwd_actuation turns ctrl into actuator forces. Here is the velocity stage exactly as the 4.0.1 wheel has it:

    def fwd_velocity(m: Model, d: Data):
  """Velocity-dependent computations."""
  wp.launch(_actuator_velocity, dim=(d.nworld, m.nactuator), ...)   # moment row . qvel
  wp.launch(_tendon_velocity, dim=(d.nworld, m.ntendon), ...)       # ten_J row . qvel
  smooth.com_vel(m, d)
  passive.passive(m, d)
  smooth.rne(m, d)
  smooth.tendon_bias(m, d, d.qfrc_bias)
  if m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.DISCRETE:
    derivative.eff_shift(m, d)

    Every term is its own array

    MJWarp keeps the components: d.qfrc_spring, d.qfrc_damper, d.qfrc_gravcomp, d.qfrc_fluid, d.qfrc_passive, d.qfrc_bias, d.qfrc_actuator, d.qfrc_smooth, all shaped (nworld, nv). You can read any of them after mjw.forward, which is how every example in this chapter checks a formula.

    2Passive forces: springs and dampers

    A passive force depends only on the state and the model, never on ctrl. The simplest ones are joint springs and dampers. In MJCF you write them on the joint:

    <joint name="j" type="hinge" stiffness="10" damping="0.5" springref="0.2"/>

    For a hinge or slide joint with position q and velocity v this gives

    \texttt{qfrc\_spring} = -k\,(q - q_\text{ref}), \qquad \texttt{qfrc\_damper} = -b\,v.

    The kernel is passive.py::_spring_damper_dof_passive, launched with dim=(d.nworld, m.njnt): one thread per joint in every world. A thread looks up the joint type and writes 1 (hinge, slide), 3 (ball) or 6 (free) entries of qfrc_spring and qfrc_damper, starting at jnt_dofadr[jntid]. Free and ball joints use the quaternion difference math.quat_sub for the rotational part, so a ball spring pulls back along the shortest rotation to its reference orientation.

    MuJoCo 4: polynomial stiffness and damping

    MuJoCo 4 accepts up to three numbers in stiffness and damping: stiffness="k p1 p2". The first number lands in jnt_stiffness, the other two in jnt_stiffnesspoly (a vec2 per joint); for damping they go to dof_damping and dof_dampingpoly. Both are evaluated by one tiny function:

    @wp.func
def _poly_force(linear: float, poly: wp.vec2, x: float, flg_odd: int) -> float:   # util_misc.py
  x_val = wp.where(flg_odd == 1, wp.abs(x), x)
  res = linear
  res += poly[0] * x_val
  res += poly[1] * x_val * x_val
  return res

    and the kernel multiplies the result by -x:

    \texttt{qfrc\_spring} = -x\,(k + p_1 x + p_2 x^2), \quad x = q - q_\text{ref} \qquad\qquad \texttt{qfrc\_damper} = -v\,(b + p_1 |v| + p_2 v^2)

    Look closely at the spring: flg_odd is 0, so the quadratic term uses x, not |x|. The force -k x - p_1 x^2 - p_2 x^3 is therefore not symmetric when p_1 \ne 0: it stiffens on one side of the reference and softens on the other. That asymmetry holds for hinge and slide joints and for tendons, where x is signed. For ball and free joints _spring_damper_dof_passive evaluates the polynomial on the magnitude r = |\text{quat\_sub}(q, q_\text{ref})| (and on |\Delta p| for the translation), so those springs are symmetric: force = -\Delta\,(k + p_1 r + p_2 r^2). The damper uses |v| (flg_odd = 1), so it always opposes motion. Play with both in Figure 2; Example 12.2 checks the same formula against MJWarp and C MuJoCo.

    Pitfall: springref is an angle in degrees

    For hinge joints, springref (and ref, range) are angles, and MJCF reads angles in degrees unless you write <compiler angle="radian"/>. If you omit angle="radian" in Example 12.2, springref="0.2" becomes 0.0035 rad and every spring force is off by about 2 N. The examples in this chapter all set angle="radian".

    Tendon springs and the dead band

    Tendons have the same three-number stiffness and damping (tendon_stiffness, tendon_stiffnesspoly, ...). A tendon spring has a dead band: springlength="lo hi" (stored in tendon_lengthspring as a vec2) means no force while lo \le L \le hi, and x = L - hi or L - lo outside. The force acts along the tendon, so it is mapped to joints by the tendon Jacobian: \texttt{qfrc\_spring} \mathrel{+}= J_\text{ten}^\top f. _spring_damper_tendon_passive is launched with dim=(d.nworld, m.ntendon, m.max_ten_J_rownnz): one thread per non-zero of the tendon Jacobian, which adds its share with wp.atomic_add (several tendons can touch the same dof). Exercise 12.3 checks this by hand.

    Disable flags

    DisableBit.SPRING and DisableBit.DAMPER switch the two families off. There is a shortcut at the top of passive(): when both are disabled, the function zeroes qfrc_spring, qfrc_damper, qfrc_gravcomp, qfrc_fluid and qfrc_passive and returns. So disabling springs and dampers also removes gravity compensation and fluid forces. DisableBit.GRAVITY zeroes qfrc_gravcomp (there is nothing to compensate).

    Three things to read in that output. In (a) and (b) MJWarp, the NumPy formula and C MuJoCo agree to every printed digit: the three numbers in stiffness really are (k, p_1, p_2). In (c) the residual passive - (spring + damper + gravcomp + fluid) is exactly 0. _qfrc_passive_kernel sums spring + damper + gravcomp (skipped for joints with actuatorgravcomp) + fluid + adhesion; flex passive forces need no term of their own because the flex kernels write into qfrc_spring and qfrc_damper. This model has no adhesion and no actuator gravcomp, so qfrc_passive is precisely the sum of the four named terms, and it matches C MuJoCo to 9e-07 (float32). Figure 3 draws (c) as stacked bars.

    Actuator damping (MuJoCo 4)

    An actuator can also carry a damping="b p1 p2" attribute. Despite living on the actuator, it is a passive force. While computing the damping of a joint, the dof kernel calls util_misc.actuator_target_damping, which adds \text{gear}^2 \cdot (b, p_1, p_2) of every actuator that drives that joint (transmission JOINT or JOINTINPARENT). In short, an actuator's transmission says what it pushes on (here a joint), and its gear scales its force onto that joint; both are defined properly in §6, with the actuator force in §7 and the MuJoCo 4 actuators in §8. The array m.jnt_actuatorid[j] makes this cheap: -1 means no actuator targets the joint, >= 0 is the one actuator to read, and < -1 means "several, scan them all". Tendons do the same through tendon_actuatorid and util_misc.tendon_damping_total. The gear enters squared because the damping is defined on the actuator's own coordinate (\dot\ell = \text{gear}\,\dot q) and its force maps back with another factor of gear.

    Why put damping on the actuator?

    Real motors have back-EMF and gearbox friction that scale with the gear ratio. Writing that damping on the actuator keeps it correct when you randomize actuator_gear per world (Ch 10): the gear * gear factor is read inside the kernel from the batched actuator_gear. The integrators that treat damping implicitly see it too: forward.py (Euler's implicit damping) and derivative.py (implicitfast, implicit) read the total damping through util_misc.dof_damping_total, which includes the actuator contributions (Ch 13).

    Plastic springs

    MuJoCo 4 adds plasticity: a joint or tendon with springyield keeps its spring reference in d.plastic, a state array (m.nplastic numbers per world; the joint's slot is m.jnt_plasticadr[j], -1 if it has none). The dof kernel reads the reference from there:

    ref_scalar = plastic_in[worldid, padr] if padr >= 0 else qpos_spring[qpos_spring_id, qposid]
fdif = qpos_in[worldid, qposid] - ref_scalar
qfrc_spring_out[worldid, dofid] = -fdif * util_misc._poly_force(stiffness, spoly, fdif, 0)

    The reference moves after each step, at the end of forward.py::_advance: if springs are enabled and m.nplastic > 0, the kernel _next_plastic_joint (and _next_plastic_tendon, _next_plastic_flex_*) applies a return mapping. With yield distance y = jnt_springyield:

    x = q - q_\text{ref}: \quad x > y \Rightarrow q_\text{ref} \leftarrow q - y, \qquad x < -y \Rightarrow q_\text{ref} \leftarrow q + y

    So the spring can never be stretched more than y; pushing further drags the reference along and leaves a permanent set when you let go. Ball joints do the same on the quaternion with math.quat_integrate, so a ball joint's reference takes four d.plastic slots (a quaternion) where a hinge or slide takes one.

    Tendons are different. A tendon's plastic state is a dead band [lower, upper], two d.plastic slots at m.tendon_plasticadr[t], which the passive kernel uses in place of tendon_lengthspring. _next_plastic_tendon measures the overshoot past the band and, past the yield distance, shifts the whole band by that overshoot minus the yield (on the stretching side, a lower end that is 0 is left at 0 when upper > 0). The length it tests is not the current one: a fixed tendon uses sum(coef * qpos), a spatial tendon the one-step prediction ten_length + h * (ten_J · qvel). Only the joint case is checked by Example 12.2b below; the tendon and flex cases are read from the 4.0.1 source.

    The reference is part of the state: mjw.State.PLASTIC is a state bit, included in State.PHYSICS (Ch 8), and keyframes carry it as key_plastic.

    Version note: C parity checked on CPU

    Plastic-spring parity with C MuJoCo was checked directly. Example 12.2b steps MJWarp 4.0.1 and MuJoCo 4.0.1 side by side for 1500 steps; the largest difference in qpos or d.plastic was 6.1e-07 (float32 round-off). The flex return mappings (_next_plastic_flex_*) were not checked here; Ch 23 covers flex.

    3Gravity compensation and fluid forces

    Gravity compensation

    A body with gravcomp="1" receives an upward force -\text{gravcomp}\cdot m_b\, g at its center of mass, as if hanging from a balloon. passive.py::_gravity_force computes the joint space version directly: one thread per (world, dof) loops over the bodies of the dof's subtree (the contiguous range again) and adds J_\text{dof}(x_b)\cdot f_b, using support.jac_dof for the Jacobian column. The result is d.qfrc_gravcomp.

    Where it goes next depends on the joint. Normally _qfrc_passive_kernel adds it to qfrc_passive. If the joint has actuatorgravcomp="true" (m.jnt_actgravcomp), the passive kernel skips it and forward.py::_qfrc_actuator_gravcomp_limits adds it to qfrc_actuator instead, where the joint's actuatorfrcrange (jnt_actfrcrange) clamps it together with the actuator forces. This models a robot whose motors, not magic, hold up the arm. Example 12.11 shows both routes.

    Fluid forces

    When the XML has a non-zero density, viscosity or wind, put_model sets the host flag m.has_fluid and passive() calls _fluid. The kernel _fluid_force runs per (world, body), computes a wrench at the body's com and support.apply_ft maps it to d.qfrc_fluid. There are two models, chosen per body:

    Inertia box (default)Ellipsoid (fluidshape="ellipsoid" on a geom)
    ShapeThe box with the same inertia as the body: side \sqrt{6(I_y+I_z-I_x)/m}, ...Each geom's semi-axes (passive.py::geom_semiaxes), summed per body
    Linear drag-\tfrac12 \rho\, A_i |v_i| v_i per box axis + viscous -3\pi d \mu vblunt + slender drag on the projected area, viscous term
    Extra effectsquadratic angular dragMagnus lift, Kutta lift, added mass, angular drag (ellipsoid_max_moment)
    Parametersopt.density, opt.viscosity, body inertia+ fluidcoef="blunt slender angular kutta magnus" in m.geom_fluid
    Model flagm.body_fluid_ellipsoid[b] is True when any geom of body b has geom_fluid[g, 0] > 0 (set by put_model)

    Both models use the body's velocity relative to the wind: the kernel subtracts opt.wind (rotated into the local frame) from the local linear velocity. A ball at rest in a wind therefore feels a push in the wind direction until it moves with the air. Figure 5 lets you compare the two shapes and the relative velocity.

    The four density worlds reach the terminal speed of quadratic drag, v_t = \sqrt{2 m g / (\rho A)} with A the face area of the inertia box, to three decimals; the vacuum world falls freely (the tiny shortfall from g t = 39.24 is the viscous term). The ellipsoid model has a different drag law, so the same ball settles at a different speed. With wind, the ball drifts sideways and its vx approaches the wind speed.

    Recall from Ch 10

    The fluid kernels only run when m.has_fluid is True, and that host flag is computed once by put_model from the XML. If the XML has density 0 and you replace m.opt.density later, nothing happens; put a non-zero density (or viscosity) in the XML of any model you want to randomize (Ch 10 Example 10.2).

    4The bias force and RNE

    qfrc_bias is c(q, \dot q): the generalized force you would need to apply to keep the system at zero acceleration. It holds gravity and the velocity-product terms (Coriolis, centrifugal, gyroscopic). MJWarp computes it with the recursive Newton-Euler algorithm, smooth.py::rne, at zero joint acceleration.

    The gravity trick

    RNE does not add a gravity force to each body. It sets the acceleration of the world body to -g: the whole world accelerates upward at 9.81 m/s². By the equivalence principle a body sitting still in an upward-accelerating frame feels exactly what it would feel under gravity. The kernel is one line:

    @wp.kernel
def _cacc_world(gravity: wp.array[wp.vec3], cacc_out: wp.array2d[wp.spatial_vector]):
  worldid = wp.tid()
  cacc_out[worldid, 0] = wp.spatial_vector(wp.vec3(0.0), -gravity[worldid % gravity.shape[0]])

    (When DisableBit.GRAVITY is set, _rne_cacc_world zeroes cacc instead.) Everything downstream then sees an acceleration of +g inherited from the root, and the force needed to "keep up" with it is the weight. Note also gravity.shape[0]: gravity is a batched option, so each world can have its own (Ch 6).

    The ball's bias force is exactly (0, 0, m g, 0, 0, 0) and its cacc is the world's +g. Spinning the sphere adds no gyroscopic term because a sphere's inertia is isotropic (\omega \times I\omega = 0); a box would show one. On the humanoid at a random pose and velocity, MJWarp's qfrc_bias matches C MuJoCo to 4e-05 in float32.

    The two passes, as MJWarp runs them

    The textbook algorithm (and MuJoCo C's mj_rne) is two serial loops over bodies: a forward loop from root to leaves that computes each body's acceleration from its parent's, and a backward loop from leaves to root that adds each body's force into its parent. A GPU cannot afford to run a serial loop per world, so smooth.py::rne restructures both passes:

    def rne(m: Model, d: Data, flg_acc: bool = False):
  _rne_cacc_world(m, d)                    # cacc[w, 0] = (0, -gravity)
  _rne_cacc_forward(m, d, flg_acc=flg_acc) # _cacc_branch, dim=(nworld, nbranch)
  _rne_cfrc(m, d)                          # _cfrc, _cfrc_subtree, _cfrc_world
  wp.launch(_qfrc_bias, dim=[d.nworld, m.nv], ...)
    1. Forward pass, one thread per branch. _cacc_branch is launched with dim=(d.nworld, m.nbranch). Each thread starts from its branch root's parent and walks body_branches[start:end] root → leaf, accumulating a_b = a_{\text{parent}} + \dot S_b\,\dot q_b\ (+\, S_b\,\ddot q_b) (cdof_dot * qvel, plus cdof * qacc when flg_acc). Shared prefixes (the torso) are recomputed by every branch, the same trade Ch 11 made for kinematics.
    2. Body forces, all at once. _cfrc (dim=(nworld, nbody)) computes each body's own net force f_b = I_b a_b + v_b \times^* (I_b v_b) (math.inert_vec, math.motion_cross_force), minus cfrc_ext after the solver.
    3. Backward pass as subtree sums. Instead of a leaves → root walk, _cfrc_subtree (dim=(nworld, nbody)) lets every body thread add up cfrc_body[j] over its own contiguous subtree range [b, b + body_subtreenum[b]). All threads run at once; the price is redundant additions (a body deep in the tree is added once per ancestor). _cfrc_world then gives the world body the sum over the tree roots.
    4. Projection. _qfrc_bias (dim=(nworld, nv)) dots each dof's motion axis with the internal force of its body: c_i = S_i^\top f^\text{int}_{b(i)}.

    Step through it on the humanoid (the same tree as Example 12.1's dump: 16 bodies, 5 branches). The toggle switches to the serial textbook algorithm for comparison.

    Recall from Ch 11

    Branches come from put_model (one per leaf body) and subtrees are contiguous because bodies are numbered depth first; both ideas are owned by Ch 11 §5-§6. RNE reuses them; nothing new is precomputed for it.

    Going deeper: how much redundant work, and why it is still a win

    On the humanoid the branch walk visits 22 bodies for 16 (the torso five times), and the subtree sums do \sum_{b \ge 1}(\texttt{subtreenum}[b] - 1) = 40 additions where the serial backward loop needs 15 (it skips the torso, whose parent is the world). In exchange the forward pass has a dependency chain of 6 (the longest branch) instead of 16, and the backward pass has no chain at all: every _cfrc_subtree thread is independent, so there are no atomics and no level-by-level launches. The real alternatives each pay in another currency. A level-by-level pass over m.body_tree (MJWarp's per-depth body lists, which RNE does not use) needs one dependent launch per depth level: 7 on the humanoid, with level sizes 1, 1, 4, 3, 4, 2, 2. One serial thread per world needs a single launch, but each thread runs the 16-step chain alone. _rne_cfrc instead launches _cfrc, _cfrc_subtree and _cfrc_world once each and trades extra additions for full parallelism. That is the design rationale; this page does not measure which is faster on a GPU (Ch 25 shows how to measure it). The dev tree after 4.0.1 adds a tiled _cfrc_world_tiled and an optional scratch argument to rne; the algorithm is the same.

    Tendon bias

    Right after rne, fwd_velocity calls smooth.tendon_bias(m, d, d.qfrc_bias). A tendon with armature adds inertia J_\text{ten}^\top a\, J_\text{ten} to M (smooth.tendon_armature in fwd_position), and because J_\text{ten} changes with q, that inertia also produces a velocity-product force J_\text{ten}^\top a\, \dot J_\text{ten} \dot q. tendon_bias computes \dot J_\text{ten} (_tendon_dot) and adds that term to qfrc_bias. Without tendon armature it adds nothing.

    5After the solver: rne_postconstraint

    The RNE call in fwd_velocity runs with flg_acc=False, before any contact force is known. So at that point d.cacc is only "gravity trick + velocity terms", d.cfrc_int is the bias wrench, and d.cfrc_ext is not touched at all. The real accelerations and external forces exist only after the solver. That is the job of smooth.py::rne_postconstraint, which forward calls at the very end:

    if m.opt.run_rne_postconstraint or (
    not skip_sensor and not (m.opt.disableflags & DisableBit.SENSOR) and m.sensor_rne_postconstraint):
  smooth.rne_postconstraint(m, d)

    It rebuilds cfrc_ext from xfrc_applied (_cfrc_ext), equality constraint forces (_cfrc_ext_equality), contact forces (_cfrc_ext_contact, one thread per contact slot) and spatial tendons (_cfrc_ext_spatial_tendon), and then reruns the forward and force passes with flg_acc=True and flg_cfrc_ext=True. It runs automatically only if the model has a sensor that needs it (accelerometer, force, torque, ...: m.sensor_rne_postconstraint is set by put_model); otherwise set m.opt.run_rne_postconstraint = True yourself.

    With the flag off, both worlds report cacc_z = 9.81 and cfrc_ext = 0: the velocity stage cannot know that one ball is falling and the other is held by the floor. With the flag on, cacc becomes what an accelerometer would read (+g when resting, 0 in free fall, because cacc includes the gravity trick), and the resting ball's cfrc_ext is the floor's 9.81 N push.

    Pitfall: stale cfrc_ext

    When rne_postconstraint does not run, d.cfrc_ext keeps whatever was in it, for example values from a step where the flag was on. Example 12.10 zeroes it before the flag-off run so the point is visible. If you read cfrc_ext, cacc or cfrc_int in your own code, set the flag. Ch 14 builds the acceleration-stage sensors on top of this.

    6Transmissions and the sparse actuator moment

    An actuator produces a scalar force along its own coordinate, the actuator length \ell(q). The transmission defines that coordinate. Its derivative is the moment arm row \partial\ell/\partial q, and by the principle of virtual work the joint torque is that row transposed times the force:

    \dot\ell = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial q}\,\dot q \quad(\texttt{actuator\_velocity}), \qquad \texttt{qfrc\_actuator} = \Big(\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial q}\Big)^{\!\top} \texttt{actuator\_force}.

    smooth.py::transmission launches _transmission with dim=(d.nworld, m.nactuator) at the end of fwd_position. Per type:

    TrnTypeMJCFactuator_lengthmoment row (non-zeros)
    JOINTjoint="j"hinge/slide: gear[0] * q; ball: axis-angle · gear[:3]; free: 01, 3 or 6 entries: the gear
    JOINTINPARENTjointinparent="j"as JOINT, gear axis expressed in the parent framegear rotated by the inverse joint quaternion (ball, free)
    SLIDERCRANKcranksite, slidersite, cranklengtha^\top v - \sqrt{(a^\top v)^2 + r^2 - v^\top v}, times gearevery dof that moves either site (chain rule through support.jac_dof)
    TENDONtendon="t"ten_length * gear[0]the tendon's Jacobian row ten_J times gear
    SITEsite="s" (+ optional refsite)0 without refsite; with refsite, the site's pose in the refsite frame · gearJacobian of the site (minus refsite) dotted with the gear wrench

    MuJoCo 4.0.1 also has mjTRN_SO3 (ball-joint orientation actuators). mjw.TrnType in the 4.0.1 wheel has exactly the six types above, and put_model rejects the rest (Example 12.6 (b)).

    The sparse layout

    Most moment rows have only a few non-zeros (a hinge motor touches one dof), so MJWarp stores them sparsely, one CSR-like structure per world:

    ArrayShapeMeaning
    d.moment_rownnz(nworld, nactuator)number of non-zeros in actuator i's row
    d.moment_rowadr(nworld, nactuator)where row i starts in the flat arrays
    d.moment_colind(nworld, nJmom)dof index of each non-zero
    d.actuator_moment(nworld, nJmom)value of each non-zero

    To densify row i of world w:

    nnz, adr = d.moment_rownnz.numpy()[w], d.moment_rowadr.numpy()[w]
col, val = d.moment_colind.numpy()[w], d.actuator_moment.numpy()[w]
dense[i, col[adr[i]:adr[i] + nnz[i]]] = val[adr[i]:adr[i] + nnz[i]]

    forward.py::_qfrc_actuator (dim=(nworld, nactuator)) walks the same row and does wp.atomic_add(qfrc_actuator[worldid], colind, moment * force): several actuators can drive one dof, hence the atomic. _actuator_velocity walks it the other way, a dot product with qvel.

    Deep dive: row order comes from an atomic

    In _transmission each actuator thread reserves its slice with rowadr = wp.atomic_add(moment_nnz, worldid, nnz). On the CPU device threads run in order, so the rows come out as 0, 1, 2 (Example 12.5 prints rowadr [0 1 2]). On a GPU the order in which actuators win the atomic is not fixed, so moment_rowadr can be a permutation, different per world and per run. Every reader goes through rowadr, so the physics is the same; but never assume row i starts at \sum_{k<i} \texttt{rownnz}[k]. The knob for reproducible layouts is m.opt.deterministic: when it includes DeterminismType.ATOMICS, transmission() compiles _transmission in Warp's DeterministicMode.RUN_TO_RUN mode, which is intended to make its atomics reproducible run to run (Ch 22 §3). (Float atomics sum in arbitrary order, so low bits can change; Ch 4 §9, full treatment in Ch 22.)

    Note how the sweep uses worlds as sample points: 61 crank angles in one forward. The slider-crank moment matches the finite difference of actuator_length to 0.001 (the step is 6°), and the length swings between 0.55 and 0.95: the slider position 0.75 \pm 0.2. Figure 10 puts all four transmission kinds side by side.

    7The actuator force pipeline

    Every MuJoCo actuator, from a plain motor to a muscle, is the same three-part model applied to a control signal:

    \dot{a} = \text{dyn}(u, a), \qquad \texttt{actuator\_force} = \text{clamp}\big(\text{gain}(\ell, \dot\ell)\cdot \tilde u + \text{bias}(\ell, \dot\ell),\ \text{forcerange}\big)

    where u is the (clamped) control, a is the actuator's activation state act (if it has one), and \tilde u is ctrl_act in the code: a when the actuator has an activation, u otherwise. forward.py::_actuator_force evaluates all of it, one thread per (world, actuator). Pick a combination in Figure 11 to see the formula each branch of the kernel applies.

    Step by step

    1. Read and clamp the control. The actuator reads ctrl[w, actuator_ctrladr[i]] (§8 explains why it is an address). If actuator_ctrllimited and DisableBit.CLAMPCTRL is not set, it clamps to actuator_ctrlrange. Both are indexed by the control slot, not by the actuator: in MuJoCo 4 ctrlrange has one row per slot (nu rows). With nhistory > 0, fwd_actuation first reads a delayed copy of ctrl (Ch 14).
    2. Dynamics. If the actuator has activation slots (actuator_actadr[i] >= 0), the kernel writes act_dot: ctrl for INTEGRATOR, (ctrl - act) / dynprm[0] for FILTER and FILTEREXACT, util_misc.muscle_dynamics for MUSCLE, the slot equations of §8 for DCMOTOR, 0 for USER (the callback fills it). The integrator advances act later, in _advance (Ch 13), using support.next_act; FILTEREXACT uses the exact exponential there.
    3. Which activation the force sees. Normally ctrl_act = act of the current step. With actearly="true" the kernel predicts the next activation with next_act(...) and uses that, removing one step of delay between ctrl and force.
    4. Gain and bias. FIXED: gainprm[0]. AFFINE: prm[0] + prm[1]·length + prm[2]·velocity for gain or bias. MUSCLE: muscle_gain, muscle_bias (§9). DCMOTOR: K/R and back-EMF (§8). USER: 0, filled by callbacks.
    5. Clamp the force. force = gain * ctrl_act + bias, then forcerange if forcelimited. One case changes ctrl_act before the product: a servo-shaped actuator (FIXED gain, AFFINE bias with gainprm[0] == -biasprm[1], dyn NONE, INTEGRATOR, FILTER or FILTEREXACT) on a ball-joint JOINT/JOINTINPARENT transmission, or on a SITE transmission with a refsite and purely rotational gear. For those, forward.py::_wrap_period returns a period 2\pi\,\lVert\text{gear}\rVert and the kernel wraps the setpoint to the representative nearest the current length (ctrl_act -= period * round((ctrl_act - length) / period)), so a rotational target never takes the long way round. FILTER dynamics wrap their input the same way, and a limited activation is wrapped in _next_activation. (This is the 4.0.1 wheel; the dev source has other line numbers and adds a PID path.) This clamp happens inside _actuator_force, before the callbacks, for every actuator except tendon actuators (it is skipped when ntendon > 0 and the transmission is TENDON). Those are handled in step 6.
    6. Callbacks. After the kernel, fwd_actuation calls m.callback.act_dyn (writes act_dot), act_gain and act_bias (write actuator_force) if you set them. Their exact semantics are owned by Ch 26 §4. Only after the callbacks, if the model has tendons, _tendon_actuator_force sums the actuator forces per tendon and _tendon_actuator_force_clamp scales all actuators on a tendon so their sum respects the tendon's actfrcrange, then applies each tendon actuator's own forcerange. So for tendon actuators the scaling and the clamp act on the forces the callbacks wrote.
    7. To joint space. _qfrc_actuator applies the moment rows (§6), then _qfrc_actuator_gravcomp_limits adds gravity compensation for joints with actuatorgravcomp and clamps each dof to its joint's actuatorfrcrange.

    <position kp="20"> is just gainprm = [20], biasprm = [0, -20, 0] with AFFINE bias, so 20 \cdot 0.5 + (-20) \cdot 0 = 10 in world 0. The velocity servo is clamped by its forcerange to 2 in worlds 0 and 2. The three forces then simply add up in qfrc_actuator, because all three moments are 1.

    Activation dynamics in action

    "filter" follows Euler's 1 - (1 - h/\tau)^n exactly, "filterexact" follows 1 - e^{-t/\tau} exactly, and the actearly actuator already produces force 0.0952 = 1 - e^{-0.1} before the first step. Figure 12 shows how far Euler drifts as h/\tau grows.

    8Actuators in MuJoCo 4: controls, DC motors, and what 4.0.1 rejects

    nu is not nactuator

    In MuJoCo 3 every actuator read exactly one number, ctrl[i], so the count of controls and the count of actuators were the same. MuJoCo 4 lets one actuator read several control slots, for example a position and a velocity setpoint. The model now has two sizes:

    • m.nu: the number of control slots, the width of d.ctrl (and of actuator_ctrlrange).
    • m.nactuator: the number of actuators, the width of d.actuator_force, actuator_length, and the launch dim of every actuator kernel.

    Actuator i reads ctrl[actuator_ctrladr[i] : actuator_ctrladr[i] + actuator_ctrlnum[i]], and actuator_ctrlspec[i] is a bit mask of mjw.CtrlInput saying what each slot means: POS = 1, VEL = 2, FF = 4, VOLTAGE = 8 (and NONE = 16 for "explicitly no inputs"). A classic actuator has ctrlspec = 0 and one plain slot. The model of Example 12.6 has four slots and three actuators:

    Pitfall: code that assumes ctrl[i] drives actuator i

    Policies, sensors and randomization code written for MuJoCo 3 often index ctrl and actuator_force with the same i. That is only right when every actuator has ctrlnum = 1. Use actuator_ctrladr to find an actuator's slots, and size observation and action spaces from m.nu, not m.nactuator.

    The DC motor

    <dcmotor> sets DynType, GainType and BiasType all to DCMOTOR. Its parameters live in gainprm ([0] resistance R, [1] motor constant K, then thermal and controller gains) and dynprm (electrical time constant, thermal, LuGre friction, slew limit). In the simplest form, voltage input and no inductance (t_e = 0), the steady-state current is i = (V - K\dot\ell)/R and the torque is K i:

    \text{gain} = \frac{K}{R}, \quad \tilde u = V, \quad \text{bias} = -\text{gain}\cdot K\,\dot\ell \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \texttt{actuator\_force} = \frac{K}{R}\,(V - K\dot\ell).

    That is the GainType.DCMOTOR and BiasType.DCMOTOR branch of _actuator_force with dynprm[0] <= 0. Each optional feature adds an activation slot, laid out by util_misc.dcmotor_slots: slew-rate limit, PID integral, winding temperature (resistance rises with T, dcmotor_resistance), LuGre bristle state, and current (when t_e > 0). With input="pos vel" and controller="kp ki kd", util_misc.dcmotor_voltage turns setpoints into a voltage V = (R/K)\,\tau^* + K\dot\ell for the desired torque \tau^* = k_p(\ell^* - \ell) + k_d(\dot\ell^* - \dot\ell) + k_i x_I + u_\text{ff}, optionally clamped to Vmax. Plug it into the formula above and the back-EMF cancels: without a voltage limit the force is exactly \tau^*. Cogging torque and LuGre friction are added after the force clamp.

    At 1 V and rest the motor gives 0.05/2 = 0.025; at \dot q = V/K = 20 rad/s the back-EMF cancels the voltage (the no-load speed) and the force is 0. The servo actuator produces exactly the PD torque 1.8, as derived above.

    Version note: SO3 and PID actuators are rejected in 4.0.1

    MuJoCo 4.0.1 compiles two actuator kinds that MJWarp 4.0.1 cannot run. io.py::put_model checks actuator_trntype, dyntype, gaintype and biastype (in that order) with np.isin against MJWarp's own enums and raises NotImplementedError naming the first unsupported MuJoCo values:

    • A ball-joint orientation actuator (gaintype="so3" biastype="so3" on a ball joint) compiles with transmission mjTRN_SO3: NotImplementedError: ['mjTRN_SO3'] not supported. (Example 12.6 (b)). mjw.GainType.SO3 and mjw.CtrlChart exist in the wheel, and mjw.BiasType has no SO3 member, but no model reachable on 4.0.1 can use them because the transmission is rejected first.
    • <pid joint="j" kp="1"/> compiles with mjGAIN_PID: NotImplementedError: ['mjGAIN_PID'] not supported. (Example 12.7). mjw.DynType also lacks mjDYN_PID: with ki > 0 MuJoCo also sets dyntype mjDYN_PID (na = 1), and because put_model checks the dyntype first, the error then names mjDYN_PID instead. The dev tree after 4.0.1 adds PID support; do not rely on it with the wheel.

    A PID without <pid>

    Until the wheel supports it, build PD control from what 4.0.1 has: a <position kp kv> or a <general> with AFFINE bias (Exercise 12.1), or a DC motor with input="pos vel" and controller. An integral term needs a state: use a DC motor with ki > 0 (it gets an integral activation slot), or compute it in a control callback (Ch 8).

    9Muscles

    A <muscle> is dyntype, gaintype and biastype MUSCLE. All three are small functions in util_misc.py, and all of them work on a normalized length. With lengthrange [\ell_0, \ell_1] (computed by set_length_range, Ch 10) and gainprm range [r_0, r_1] (default 0.75, 1.05):

    L_0 = \frac{\ell_1 - \ell_0}{r_1 - r_0}, \qquad L = r_0 + \frac{\ell - \ell_0}{L_0}, \qquad V = \frac{\dot\ell}{L_0\,v_\text{max}}
    \text{gain} = -F_0\,F_L(L)\,F_V(V), \qquad \text{bias} = -F_0\,F_P(L), \qquad \texttt{actuator\_force} = \text{gain}\cdot a + \text{bias}
    • F_L (muscle_gain_length): a smooth bump, 0 outside [l_\text{min}, l_\text{max}], peak 1 at L = 1, made of four half-parabolas.
    • F_V: 0 when shortening at V \le -1, (V+1)^2 while shortening, rising to f_{v\max} when lengthening.
    • F_P (muscle_bias): the passive stretch force, zero below L = 1, half-quadratic and then linear with slope set by f_{p\max}.
    • F_0 = gainprm[2] (force); when it is negative (the default -1) the kernel uses scale / actuator_acc0, where acc0 is the acceleration a unit force produces at qpos0 (a derived constant from set_const).
    • Activation: muscle_dynamics filters u \in [0, 1] with a faster time constant for activation than deactivation, both scaled by the current activation (Millard et al. 2013).

    The forces are negative because a muscle can only pull: it acts to shorten its tendon.

    Pitfall: on CPU, .numpy() is a view

    If you store F = d.actuator_force.numpy()[:, 0], then set act = 0 and call forward again for the passive part, the "active" force F - passive comes out as 0 everywhere. On the CPU device wp.array.numpy() returns a zero-copy view of the same memory, so the second forward overwrites F. On a GPU, .numpy() copies, and the bug does not show up. Example 12.8 avoids it with .copy(). Call .copy() on any host array you keep across steps.

    10Tendons

    A tendon is a scalar length L(q) with a Jacobian row J_\text{ten} = \partial L/\partial q. Actuators can pull on it (TENDON transmission), springs and dampers can act along it (§2), and limits and equality constraints can be put on it (Ch 19). smooth.py::tendon runs inside fwd_kinematics and fills d.ten_length (nworld, ntendon) and d.ten_J:

    • Fixed tendons are linear combinations of joint positions, L = \sum_j c_j q_j, so J_\text{ten} is just the coefficients. _joint_tendon runs one thread per (world, joint wrap) and adds c_j q_j to the length with wp.atomic_add.
    • Spatial tendons are paths through sites, optionally wrapping around spheres and cylinders. _spatial_site_tendon handles straight site-to-site segments (one thread per consecutive site pair) and _spatial_geom_tendon the wrapped segments (one thread per wrapping geom), which call util_misc.wrap (2-D tangent construction in the wrap plane, wrap_circle, wrap_inside). Each segment adds its length and its Jacobian \big(J(p_2) - J(p_1)\big)^\top \hat d to the tendon. Segments after a pulley are multiplied by wrap_pulley_scale (length and Jacobian). _spatial_tendon_wrap finally writes the path points (d.wrap_xpos, ten_wrapadr, ten_wrapnum) for rendering.

    d.ten_J is sparse with a pattern that is fixed at put_model time: row t has m.ten_J_rownnz[t] entries starting at m.ten_J_rowadr[t], in the dofs m.ten_J_colind[...]; only the values d.ten_J[w, k] change per world and step. (Compare the actuator moment, whose pattern lives in Data.) C MuJoCo 4 keeps the same pattern on mjModel too, which is why Example 12.9 reads mjm.ten_J_rownnz.

    The elbow shows the key fact about wrapping. While the tendon runs straight (negative angles), its moment arm changes with the angle. Once it touches the cylinder (from about +0.1 rad on; the touch point lies between 0.0 and 0.1 in the sweep), the wrapped part grows by exactly r\,\Delta\theta, so ten_J is pinned at the radius, 0.06. That is why biomechanical models put wrapping cylinders at joints: they keep the muscle's lever arm realistic over the whole range. Watch it in Figure 15.

    Tendon armature, actuators and force limits

    A tendon can have armature (rotor inertia reflected onto the tendon); smooth.tendon_armature adds J_\text{ten}^\top a J_\text{ten} to M and tendon_bias adds the matching velocity term (§4). A tendon actuator's moment row is ten_J * gear (§6). With actuatorfrclimited on the tendon, _tendon_actuator_force sums all actuator forces on it and _tendon_actuator_force_clamp scales them so the total stays in tendon_actfrcrange.

    11Applied forces, gravity compensation and subtree velocities

    Two arrays are yours to write: d.qfrc_applied (nworld, nv), added as is, and d.xfrc_applied (nworld, nbody) of spatial_vector: a force (first three numbers) and a torque (last three) in world coordinates at each body's center of mass. mjw.step never clears them; they stay until you change them (or reset the data). fwd_acceleration maps xfrc_applied into qfrc_smooth with support.xfrc_accumulate, which calls apply_ft(..., sparse=True): one thread per (world, body) scatters its wrench to the body's ancestor dofs, and bodies with a zero wrench cost a single load. The same wrench also appears in cfrc_ext after rne_postconstraint (§5).

    Which one to use?

    Use qfrc_applied for a torque on a joint (a disturbance, a hand-written controller) and xfrc_applied for a push in space (wind on one body, a perturbation in an RL environment, a mouse drag in the viewer). For a force not at the com, add the torque (p - x_\text{com}) \times f yourself.

    With gravcomp="1" the ball does not move at all; with 0.5 it falls at half of g (drift 0.6156 vs 1.2312 m). On the arm, the same gravcomp torque moves from qfrc_passive to qfrc_actuator when actuatorgravcomp="true", where the joint's actuatorfrcrange clamps it from −2.45 to −1.0: the motors are too weak to hold the arm.

    The last part of the example is a different kind of trap. d.subtree_linvel and d.subtree_angmom are allocated in every Data, but forward only fills them when a sensor needs them (m.sensor_subtree_vel, used by sensor_vel). Otherwise they stay 0 until you call mjw.subtree_vel(m, d). Its three kernels follow the RNE pattern: per-body momenta, then _subtree_momentum sums each body's contiguous subtree, then _subtree_momentum_world handles the world body.

    12Check yourself

    Quiz

    After mjw.forward on a model with contacts and no sensors, you read d.cfrc_ext. When does it hold the contact forces?

    _forward calls smooth.rne_postconstraint only when m.opt.run_rne_postconstraint or m.sensor_rne_postconstraint is set (Example 12.10).

    A model has one <dcmotor input="pos vel" .../> and one <motor/>. What are m.nu and m.nactuator?

    The servo reads 2 slots (actuator_ctrlnum = 2, ctrlspec = POS|VEL), the motor 1: d.ctrl is 3 wide, while actuator_force has 2 columns.

    You model a propeller as a thrust along a site's x axis on a drone body. Which TrnType is it?

    A SITE transmission turns the gear (a wrench in the site frame) into a moment row through the site Jacobian, like the "thrust" actuator of Example 12.5.

    What does _cacc_world write into cacc[w, 0] under standard gravity?

    wp.spatial_vector(wp.vec3(0.0), -gravity): the gravity trick (Figure 7, Example 12.1).

    A hinge has stiffness="10 5 0" and spring reference 0. What is qfrc_spring at q = -0.2?

    -x(k + p_1 x) = 0.2\,(10 + 5\cdot(-0.2)) = 1.8. On a hinge the spring is softer on the negative side (_poly_force with flg_odd = 0 on the signed displacement); a ball or free joint would use the magnitude and be symmetric.

    A joint with no damping is driven by <general joint="j" gear="2" damping="0.3"/>. Where does the damping show up, and how big is it?

    util_misc.actuator_target_damping adds damping * gear * gear to the joint's damping in _spring_damper_dof_passive (Example 12.7b).

    Why does _cfrc_subtree do more additions than the serial leaves-to-root loop?

    On the humanoid: 40 additions instead of 15, but all 16 body threads run at once (stepper, Figure "RNE in three phases").

    You set both DisableBit.SPRING and DisableBit.DAMPER on a model with gravcomp bodies and fluid drag. What is qfrc_passive?

    The first lines of passive.py::passive: if both flags are set, zero qfrc_spring, qfrc_damper, qfrc_gravcomp, qfrc_fluid, qfrc_passive and return.

    Why is a muscle's actuator_force negative?

    See util_misc.muscle_gain ("make it negative") and muscle_bias; Example 12.8 prints −838 N near the optimum.

def drag(qvel: wp.array2d(dtype=float), c: float, qfrc_passive: wp.array2d(dtype=float)):
    worldid, dofid = wp.tid()
    qfrc_passive[worldid, dofid] -= c * qvel[worldid, dofid]

def my_passive(m, d):
    wp.launch(drag, dim=(d.nworld, m.nv), inputs=[d.qvel, C], outputs=[d.qfrc_passive])

m.callback.passive = my_passive
# CPU run (free 1 kg ball, 800 steps of 0.01 s): vz = -12.2426, terminal -m g / c = -12.2625

    The callback only changes qfrc_passive; integrators that treat damping implicitly read the damping coefficients from the model (dof_damping_total), so they do not know about your drag.

    14Summary

    Key takeaways

    • qfrc_smooth = qfrc_passive − qfrc_bias + qfrc_actuator + qfrc_applied + Jᵀ xfrc_applied, and every term is a separate (nworld, nv) array you can read.
    • qfrc_passive = qfrc_spring + qfrc_damper + qfrc_gravcomp + qfrc_fluid + qfrc_adhesion (gravcomp skipped for actuatorgravcomp joints; flex forces are already inside spring and damper). MuJoCo 4 springs: stiffness="k p1 p2" gives -x(k + p_1x + p_2x^2); actuator damping adds \text{gear}^2 b to the joint's damper; springyield makes a plastic reference in d.plastic, updated in _advance.
    • RNE uses the gravity trick (cacc[world] = −g), a branch-parallel forward pass and a subtree-sum backward pass; cacc, cfrc_int and cfrc_ext are only real after rne_postconstraint.
    • An actuator is dyn + gain + bias through a transmission: force = clamp(gain·ctrl_act + bias), qfrc_actuator = momentᵀ force, with the moment stored as sparse rows per world.
    • MuJoCo 4: nu control slots vs nactuator actuators (actuator_ctrladr/ctrlnum/ctrlspec, CtrlInput); the DC motor runs in 4.0.1; PID and SO3 actuators are rejected by put_model.
    • Tendons: fixed = linear in q, spatial = site paths with wrapping; ten_J is sparse with a model-level pattern. A wrapped tendon's moment arm is the wrap radius.
    • Fluid forces have two models (inertia box, ellipsoid), see the wind as relative velocity, and only run when m.has_fluid.

    Stuck? See Troubleshooting or the course map.
