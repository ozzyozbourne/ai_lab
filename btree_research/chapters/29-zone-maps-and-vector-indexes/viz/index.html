A zone map stores one (min, max) pair per chunk of a column. A scan asks every chunk's pair one question before it reads any row. Nothing is sorted, so every chunk is asked; the saving is in the rows that are never read.

1. One scan, chunk by chunk

Each bar is the value range of one chunk of 100 rows; chunks are listed in storage order. The shaded band is the set of values the filter accepts. Pick a physical layout and a filter, then step through the scan. Drag the slider to move the filter and watch the verdicts change.

2. The same filter on three layouts

The three tables hold the same kind of data; only the order in which rows were stored differs. This picture is the whole story of when a zone map can stand in for an index.

What to take away

  • Three answers. "No row can match" skips the chunk. "Every row matches" also skips reading it: the rows are counted, not compared. Only "maybe" costs row comparisons.
  • No navigation. A B+tree's inner node holds the same kind of summary, but sorted and non-overlapping, so a binary search finds the one child. Here all 20 summaries are tested.
  • It depends on the data. On sorted rows the zones do not overlap and almost everything is decided without reading rows. On shuffled rows every zone spans nearly the whole domain and nothing is.
