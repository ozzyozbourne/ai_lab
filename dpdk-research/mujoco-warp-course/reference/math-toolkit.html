    Reference · Math toolkit

    The Math Toolkit

    Every piece of mathematics the course leans on, collected in one place: what an SPD matrix is and how Cholesky factors it, how MJWarp stores the mass matrix, the three ways downhill (gradient descent, Newton, conjugate gradient), the exact linesearch on a piecewise quadratic, and the two kinds of 3-D bookkeeping: quaternions and spatial vectors. Each part says where MJWarp uses it and links to the chapter that owns it.

    IntermediateAbout 45 min·Uses: Ch 3, Ch 11, Ch 19 §2, Ch 20

    On this page

    • Drag a 2x2 matrix and watch it become (or stop being) SPD; see its Cholesky factor.
    • Race gradient descent, Newton and CG on the same quadratic as Ch 19, and step the exact linesearch.
    • Rotate a box with a quaternion, and see where the velocity of a point on a spinning body comes from.

    Before you start: recap

    • A rigid body has 6 DOF: position + orientation (quat), linear + angular velocity (Ch 3 §4).
    • MuJoCo quats are (w, x, y, z) (Ch 2 §3).
    • The equation of motion is M(q)\,\ddot q = \tau - c(q,\dot q) + J^\top f; MJWarp never forms M^{-1} (Ch 11 §1).
    • The constraint solve minimizes one convex cost over qacc (Ch 19 §3, Ch 20 §1).

    1Quadratics, SPD matrices and convexity

    A quadratic in n variables is

    f(x) = \tfrac12\, x^\top A x - b^\top x, \qquad \nabla f(x) = A x - b, \qquad \nabla^2 f = A .

    A is symmetric positive definite (SPD) when A = A^\top and x^\top A x > 0 for every x \ne 0; equivalently, all its eigenvalues are positive. Then the level sets of f are ellipses, f is strictly convex, and there is exactly one minimum, where the gradient vanishes: A x^* = b. The ratio \kappa = \lambda_{max}/\lambda_{min} (the condition number) says how stretched the bowl is.

    Why this matters for MJWarp: the mass matrix M is SPD, the Gauss term of the constraint problem is exactly a quadratic with A = M, and every other row of the constraint cost adds a convex piece. A sum of convex functions is convex, so "downhill" always leads to the one global minimum. That is why each world can stop on its own as soon as its gradient is small (Ch 19 §2).

    2Cholesky, LDL and the CSR mass matrix

    An SPD matrix has a Cholesky factorization A = L L^\top, with L lower triangular and a positive diagonal. Solving A x = b is then two triangular solves, L y = b and L^\top x = y, each a single pass. Computing L is the expensive part, so it is done once per new matrix and reused for every right-hand side. The algorithm also tests SPD: it needs the square root of a positive number at every diagonal step, and fails exactly when the matrix is not SPD (the widget above shows where).

    L_{jj} = \sqrt{A_{jj} - \textstyle\sum_{k<j} L_{jk}^2}, \qquad L_{ij} = \frac{A_{ij} - \sum_{k<j} L_{ik} L_{jk}}{L_{jj}} \quad (i > j)

    Two spellings appear in the course and they are the same thing: Ch 19 writes A = L L^\top; Ch 11 and Ch 20 write M = U^\top U with U = L^\top upper triangular (MJWarp's scalar path stores U row-major). An LDL factorization splits out the diagonal instead (L D L^\top with a unit diagonal on L) and needs no square roots.

    How MJWarp stores and factors M

    The mass matrix is never stored dense. d.M has shape (nworld, nC): only the lower triangle, row after row, in CSR form. Three index arrays, computed by MuJoCo's compiler and copied into mjw.Model, say where each row starts (M_rowadr), how many entries it has (M_rownnz) and which column each entry is in (M_colind: ancestors in increasing order, the diagonal last). A DOF couples only with its ancestors in the kinematic tree, so the zeros of M are exactly where two DOFs are on different branches.

    factor_m factors M block by block (one block per kinematic tree) and writes d.qLD and d.qLDiagInv; solve_m back-substitutes with them. The block router picks one of four paths per block: compact (a diagonal block: only qLDiagInv = 1/M_ii), scalar Cholesky (up to 6 DOFs, one thread per block), tile Cholesky (a thread block per tile, Ch 26 owns tiles), and sparse LDL for big trees (Ch 11 §10). The rule that matters in practice: exactly one factorization per new qpos; solving with an old factor gives a wrong qacc without any error (Ch 11 §9).

    CSR read-back and mul_m in NumPy (illustrative values)
    import numpy as np

# 3-hinge chain: lower triangle of M stored row by row (CSR), diagonal last in each row
M_rownnz = np.array([1, 2, 3])
M_rowadr = np.array([0, 1, 3])
M_colind = np.array([0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 2])
dense = np.array([[3.0, 1.0, 0.5],
                  [1.0, 2.0, 0.4],
                  [0.5, 0.4, 1.0]])
M = np.array([dense[i, j] for i in range(3) for j in M_colind[M_rowadr[i]:M_rowadr[i] + M_rownnz[i]]])
print("d.M[w] =", M)

def mul_m(M, vec):                         # res = M vec from the lower triangle
    res = np.zeros_like(vec)
    for i in range(len(vec)):
        for adr in range(M_rowadr[i], M_rowadr[i] + M_rownnz[i]):
            j = M_colind[adr]
            res[i] += M[adr] * vec[j]
            if j != i:
                res[j] += M[adr] * vec[i]     # mirror the upper triangle
    return res

v = np.array([1.0, -2.0, 0.5])
print("mul_m:", mul_m(M, v), "| dense:", dense @ v)
L = np.linalg.cholesky(dense)              # dense = L L^T; MJWarp's scalar path stores U = L^T
print("Cholesky L:\n", L.round(4))

    In the Newton solver the same idea is applied to a bigger matrix, H = M + J^\top D J, which is SPD for the same reason (§4). It is stored dense per world, padded to nv_pad, and factored with Warp tiles (Ch 20 §6).

    3Three ways downhill: gradient descent, Newton, CG

    All three start from a point x_0 and repeat: pick a direction, pick a step length. They differ in the direction.

    • Gradient descent steps against the gradient, x \leftarrow x - s\,\nabla f. Each step is cheap, but on a stretched bowl it zigzags, and the number of steps grows with the condition number. MJWarp does not use it; it is the baseline.
    • Newton uses the curvature, x \leftarrow x - (\nabla^2 f)^{-1} \nabla f. On a quadratic it lands on the minimum in one step, at the price of a factorization (§2).
    • Conjugate gradient picks each direction A-conjugate to the previous ones (p_i^\top A p_j = 0) and minimizes exactly along it. On an n-dimensional quadratic it is exact in at most n steps, using only products with A.
    from examples/19-constraints-as-optimization/ex12_math_toolkit.py (excerpt)
    # Part 1 of ex12_math_toolkit.py (Ch 19): one SPD quadratic, three ways down
A = np.array([[4.0, 1.4], [1.4, 1.0]])           # symmetric positive definite
b = np.array([1.0, 2.0])
x0 = np.array([-2.0, -1.0])
x_star = np.linalg.solve(A, b)                     # the unique minimizer: A x = b

def grad(x):
    return A @ x - b

step = 1.0 / np.linalg.eigvalsh(A).max()          # gradient descent, fixed step
newton = [x0.copy(), x0 - np.linalg.solve(A, grad(x0))]   # exact in one step

x = x0.copy(); r = -grad(x); p = r.copy()          # conjugate gradient: exact in n = 2 steps
for _ in range(2):
    alpha = (r @ r) / (p @ A @ p)                  # exact 1-D minimizer along p
    x = x + alpha * p
    r_new = r - alpha * (A @ p)
    beta = (r_new @ r_new) / (r @ r)
    p = r_new + beta * p
    r = r_new

    4Newton in MJWarp: H = M + J^\top D J

    The constraint cost is not one quadratic but a piecewise quadratic: each constraint row adds \tfrac12 D_i x_i^2 only while it is active. Inside one piece (no row changes state) the cost is an ordinary quadratic with Hessian

    H = M + \sum_{i \in \text{QUADRATIC}} D_i\, J_i^\top J_i \;(+\text{cone terms for elliptic})

    M is SPD and each added term is positive semidefinite, so H is SPD and has a Cholesky factor. The Newton direction is s = -H^{-1} g; a full step jumps to the minimum of the current piece. MJWarp builds and factors one H per world per iteration (or updates it incrementally), choosing among three Cholesky paths by nv: one tile up to 48, blocked 16x16 tiles up to 96, sparse tiles above (Ch 20 §6, Ch 20 §7). The same solve also yields the Newton decrement g^\top H^{-1} g, one of the stopping tests (Ch 20 §9).

    5CG in MJWarp: preconditioned by M

    MJWarp's SolverType.CG preconditions with the mass matrix: instead of -g it starts from -M^{-1} g (Mgrad), which costs one triangular solve with the factor qLD that fwd_acceleration already computed. For an unconstrained world this is the exact answer in one step. Each new direction mixes in the old one with the Polak-Ribière coefficient, clamped at zero (a restart):

    s_{k+1} = -M^{-1} g_{k+1} + \beta_k s_k, \qquad \beta_k = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{g_{k+1}^\top (M^{-1} g_{k+1} - M^{-1} g_k)}{\max(\epsilon,\ g_k^\top M^{-1} g_k)}\right)

    On a single quadratic all classic \beta formulas agree (the NumPy snippet above uses Fletcher-Reeves). On the piecewise cost they differ, and the clamp matters because the old direction stops being useful each time a row changes state. CG never forms H, so it is the fallback when Newton's nv^2 memory per world is too much (Ch 20 §5).

    Version note: dev tree vs 4.0.1

    The 4.0.1 wheel computes the Polak-Ribière \beta above. The dev tree at 8f5b9f1 replaces it with a Hager-Zhang \beta clamped at zero plus a restart test (Ch 20 §5).

    6The exact linesearch on a piecewise quadratic

    Both solvers hand the same 1-D job to the linesearch: given the current point and a direction s, find the step \alpha that minimizes \varphi(\alpha) = f(a + \alpha s). Along a ray each row's residual is linear in \alpha, so \varphi is a convex piecewise quadratic with a kink wherever a row switches state. Its slope is continuous and increasing; the minimizer is where the slope crosses zero. Inside one piece a single Newton step \alpha \leftarrow \alpha - \varphi'/\varphi'' is exact; if it lands in another piece, repeat from there. Each probe costs one pass over the rows.

    MJWarp's _linesearch_iterative is a guarded version of this idea: a Newton probe from 0, then up to ls_iterations bracket rounds that evaluate three candidates (a Newton step from each bracket end and the midpoint) in one pass over the rows, accepting the first with a small enough slope (Ch 20 §8). ls_iterations is a compile-time constant of that kernel, so changing it recompiles; ls_tolerance is a live per-world array.

    7Quaternions

    A unit quaternion encodes a rotation by angle \theta about a unit axis u:

    q = \big(\cos\tfrac\theta2,\; u \sin\tfrac\theta2\big) = (w, x, y, z), \qquad v' = q \otimes (0, v) \otimes q^*, \quad q^* = (w, -x, -y, -z).

    The product q_1 \otimes q_2 composes rotations (apply q_2 first, in the frame of q_1). q and -q are the same rotation. Four numbers for three degrees of freedom: that is why a free joint has 7 qpos slots but 6 qvel slots (Ch 13 §2).

    Pitfall: the order of the four numbers

    MuJoCo and MJWarp use (w, x, y, z) everywhere: qpos, xquat, mocap_quat, MJCF. Warp's built-ins (wp.quat_rotate, wp.transform, ...) read a wp.quat as (x, y, z, w), and so does SciPy's as_quat() by default. MJWarp stores its quats in wp.quat arrays but keeps the w-first meaning, so never pass one to a Warp built-in: use MJWarp's math helpers or reorder with wp.quat(q[1], q[2], q[3], q[0]) (Ch 4 §6, Ch 2 §3).

    Integrating an orientation

    For a constant body-frame angular velocity \omega over a step h, the exact update is the exponential map, multiplied on the right because \omega is in the body frame:

    q_{n+1} = q_n \otimes \Big(\cos\tfrac{h\lVert\omega\rVert}{2},\; \tfrac{\omega}{\lVert\omega\rVert} \sin\tfrac{h\lVert\omega\rVert}{2}\Big)

    This is math.quat_integrate, used by forward.py::_next_position for free and ball joints (Ch 3 §6). The tempting shortcut q \leftarrow \mathrm{normalize}(q + \tfrac h2\, q\otimes(0,\omega)) leaves the unit sphere each step and, after renormalizing, has turned by 2\arctan(h\lVert\omega\rVert/2) instead of h\lVert\omega\rVert: a loss of about (h\lVert\omega\rVert)^3/12 per step, forever.

    Quaternion product, rotation and integration in NumPy (w, x, y, z)
    import numpy as np

def quat_mul(u, v):                      # (w, x, y, z), as math.mul_quat
    w1, x1, y1, z1 = u
    w2, x2, y2, z2 = v
    return np.array([w1*w2 - x1*x2 - y1*y2 - z1*z2,
                     w1*x2 + x1*w2 + y1*z2 - z1*y2,
                     w1*y2 - x1*z2 + y1*w2 + z1*x2,
                     w1*z2 + x1*y2 - y1*x2 + z1*w2])

def axis_angle(axis, angle):             # unit quaternion for a turn about axis
    axis = np.asarray(axis, float) / np.linalg.norm(axis)
    return np.concatenate([[np.cos(angle / 2)], np.sin(angle / 2) * axis])

def rotate(q, v):                        # q v q*, with q* = (w, -x, -y, -z)
    qc = q * np.array([1, -1, -1, -1])
    return quat_mul(quat_mul(q, np.concatenate([[0.0], v])), qc)[1:]

def quat_integrate(q, omega, dt):        # body-frame omega, as math.quat_integrate
    n = np.linalg.norm(omega)
    if n == 0:
        return q / np.linalg.norm(q)
    q = quat_mul(q / np.linalg.norm(q), axis_angle(omega, n * dt))
    return q / np.linalg.norm(q)

q = axis_angle([0, 0, 1], np.pi / 2)     # 90 degrees about z
print("q (w,x,y,z):", q.round(4), "| Warp order (x,y,z,w):", np.roll(q, -1).round(4))
print("rotate x-axis:", rotate(q, np.array([1.0, 0, 0])).round(4))

omega, dt = np.array([0.0, 0.0, 10.0]), 0.01
exact = naive = np.array([1.0, 0, 0, 0])
for _ in range(1000):
    exact = quat_integrate(exact, omega, dt)
    naive = naive + 0.5 * dt * quat_mul(naive, np.concatenate([[0.0], omega]))
    naive = naive / np.linalg.norm(naive)
angle = lambda q: 2 * np.arctan2(q[3], q[0])
print(f"after 1000 steps: exact error {abs(np.angle(np.exp(1j*(angle(exact) - 100.0)))):.1e} rad,"
      f" naive error {abs(np.angle(np.exp(1j*(angle(naive) - 100.0)))):.4f} rad")

    8Spatial vectors and the com frame

    Tree algorithms move velocities and forces between bodies all the time, so MuJoCo and MJWarp pack them into 6-D spatial vectors (wp.spatial_vector). The order is always rotation first, translation second: wp.spatial_top(v) is the rotational 3-vector, wp.spatial_bottom(v) the translational one.

    • Motion vectors (twists) (\omega, v): cvel, cdof, cdof_dot, cacc.
    • Force vectors (wrenches) (\tau, f): cfrc_int, cfrc_ext. A joint force is a dot product of the two kinds: qfrc_bias[dof] = dot(cdof[dof], cfrc_int[body]).

    The translational part needs a reference point. MuJoCo picks one per kinematic tree, c = subtree_com[body_rootid[b]], with world axes: the com frame. Every quantity whose name starts with c uses it, so a child's contribution adds to its parent's without any shifting. The price is one rule: the linear part of cvel is the velocity of the body point currently at c, not of the body origin. For any point p on the body:

    v_p = v + \omega \times (p - c), \qquad \texttt{cvel}[b] = (\omega_b,\ v_b)

    Pitfall: xfrc_applied uses the other order

    d.xfrc_applied is also a wp.spatial_vector per body, but it is (force, torque), applied at the body's centre of mass xipos, as in MuJoCo C. Do not reuse the cfrc_* convention for it (Ch 11 §3).

    The body's mass properties in the com frame form a 6x6 spatial inertia, stored as 10 numbers in cinert (6 rotational inertia entries about c, the first moment m(x_i - c), the mass). crb sums them over each subtree, and the composite rigid body algorithm turns those sums into the rows of M (Ch 11 §7, Ch 11 §8).

    9Summary

    Takeaways

    • SPD means positive eigenvalues: a bowl with one minimum. M and Newton's H are SPD, so they have Cholesky factors.
    • MJWarp stores M as a CSR lower triangle and factors it once per new qpos; never mix up the factorize pairing.
    • Gradient descent zigzags; Newton is exact on one quadratic piece; CG is exact in n steps and needs only products (MJWarp preconditions it with M).
    • The linesearch minimizes a convex piecewise quadratic exactly, with Newton steps from piece to piece.
    • Quaternions are (w, x, y, z) in MuJoCo; integrate them with the exponential map, never by adding.
    • Spatial vectors are (rotation, translation) about the subtree com: v_p = v + \omega \times (p - c).
