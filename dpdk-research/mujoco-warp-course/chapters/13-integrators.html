    Part III · Chapter 13

    Time Integration: Euler, RK4, Implicit and Discrete

    After forward has computed an acceleration, one more piece of code decides what the world looks like h seconds later. That piece is the integrator. This chapter opens all five of MJWarp's integrators, measures their energy behaviour, accuracy and cost on real runs, and ends with a guide for choosing one.

    Advanced About 90 min· Prerequisites: Ch 12 (also uses Ch 3 and Ch 11)

    You will learn

    • What _advance does, kernel by kernel, and why MuJoCo's Euler is semi-implicit.
    • The math and cost of EULER (implicit damping), RK4 (4 forwards + RKMK), IMPLICITFAST, IMPLICIT (LU) and DISCRETE.
    • How to measure energy drift and accuracy, and run a convergence study across timesteps in one batched launch.
    • Why a new integrator needs a new CUDA graph, and why step2 with RK4 runs Euler.

    Before you start: recap

    • A rigid body has 6 DOF: position + orientation (quat), linear + angular velocity; Ch 3. Explicit and semi-implicit Euler differ only in the order of the two updates (Ch 3 §2).
    • forward ends with qacc, the solution of M\,\ddot q = \tau - c + J^\top f (Ch 11); the forces in it, including joint damping and actuator gains, come from Ch 12.
    • factor_m writes qLD; solving with an old qLD gives wrong qacc; Ch 11 §9.
    • Batched model fields are read as x[worldid % x.shape[0]], so assigning a length-nworld array gives each world its own value (Ch 10).
    • step1 / step2 split a step so a policy can read sensors and write ctrl in between (Ch 8).

    1One step = forward + integrate

    Every mjw.step(m, d) has two halves. First forward(m, d) runs the whole pipeline you met in Ch 7 and fills d.qacc. Then step looks at one Python integer, m.opt.integrator, and calls the matching function. This is the complete dispatch in forward.py:

    def step(m: Model, d: Data):
  """Advance simulation."""
  forward(m, d)

  if m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.EULER:
    euler(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.RK4:
    rungekutta4(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator in (IntegratorType.IMPLICITFAST, IntegratorType.IMPLICIT):
    implicit(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.DISCRETE:
    discrete(m, d)
  else:
    raise NotImplementedError(f"integrator {m.opt.integrator} not implemented.")

    The four functions differ in which acceleration they use. They all finish in the same place: the private helper _advance(m, d, qacc, qvel=None), which writes the next state. So we start there.

    What _advance does, in order

    _advance is a fixed sequence of kernel launches. Each one is a grid over worlds and model elements (layout 1 of the launch-shape atlas, Ch 7), and each one reads the timestep of its own world as opt_timestep[worldid % opt_timestep.shape[0]]. Hover, tap or tab through the stations:

    Three details matter for everything that follows:

    • The velocity is updated first. _next_velocity writes d.qvel, and _next_position then reads qvel or d.qvel. Unless a caller passes its own qvel, the position moves with the velocity of the end of the step.
    • The timestep is per world. Both kernels index opt_timestep[worldid % shape[0]]. That is how Section 8 runs a whole convergence study in one launch.
    • _advance computes no forces for the step's acceleration. (In the 4.0.1 wheel with sleep enabled it re-runs fwd_velocity after the update for the sleep test, Ch 21; the dev tree does not.) Everything that makes the integrators different happens before it, when each one decides which qacc (and, for RK4, which qvel) to hand over.

    Version note: dev tree vs 4.0.1 wheel

    In the dev tree (commit 8f5b9f1) the signature is _advance(m, d, qacc, qvel=None, act_dot=None), the activation kernel is skipped when m.na == 0, and _next_position/_next_velocity are cache_kernel builders with an enable_sleep switch. The 4.0.1 wheel shown here takes no act_dot argument: RK4 copies its weighted act_dot into d.act_dot before calling _advance. The physics is the same.

    Choosing the integrator

    There are two ways to select one. In MJCF, <option integrator="RK4"/> (spellings: Euler, RK4, implicit, implicitfast, discrete) is compiled by MuJoCo and copied by put_model. On a device model you can also set the field directly: m.opt.integrator = mjw.IntegratorType.RK4. The field is a plain Python int, read by step each time it dispatches.

    Both columns agree to the last bit, and the table already tells a story. implicitfast gives exactly the Euler answer on this undamped pendulum (there is no velocity-dependent force to treat implicitly), discrete differs from Euler only in round-off, and RK4 and implicit land somewhere else. The rest of the chapter explains each row.

    Pitfall: the integrator is baked into a CUDA graph

    A CUDA graph records launches once and replays them; conditional nodes loop on device; Ch 7 §10. Because step picks the integrator in Python, a graph captured with Euler contains Euler's kernels forever. Changing m.opt.integrator afterwards changes nothing in the replay: recapture the graph after switching. The first call with a new integrator also compiles (or loads) its kernels, so it is slow. Ch 24 §4 lists every option that behaves like this.

    2Positions on a sphere: integrating orientation

    For hinge and slide joints, q += dt * v is all there is. Free and ball joints are different: their orientation is a unit quaternion, which lives on a sphere in 4-D, while their angular velocity is a 3-vector. You cannot add the two.

    Recall from Ch 2

    MuJoCo quats are (w,x,y,z); Ch 2 §3. A free joint stores 7 numbers in qpos and 6 in qvel; Ch 3 derived the exponential update (Ch 3 §6).

    Here is the whole update, from mujoco_warp/_src/math.py:

    @wp.func
def quat_integrate(q: wp.quat, v: wp.vec3, dt: float) -> wp.quat:
  """Integrates a quaternion given angular velocity and dt."""
  norm_ = wp.length(v)
  v = wp.normalize(v)  # does that need proper zero gradient handling?
  angle = dt * norm_

  q_res = axis_angle_to_quat(v, angle)
  q = wp.normalize(q)
  q_res = mul_quat(q, q_res)

  return wp.normalize(q_res)

    In math, with \omega the body-frame angular velocity:

    q_{n+1} = q_n \otimes \Big(\cos\tfrac{h\lVert\omega\rVert}{2},\; \sin\tfrac{h\lVert\omega\rVert}{2}\,\tfrac{\omega}{\lVert\omega\rVert}\Big)

    The step is the exact rotation for a constant \omega over h. The tempting shortcut treats the quaternion as four ordinary numbers, q \leftarrow \mathrm{normalize}\big(q + \tfrac h2\, q \otimes (0, \omega)\big). It leaves the sphere every step (its norm grows to \sqrt{1 + (h\lVert\omega\rVert/2)^2}), and pulling it back loses a little angle: the per-step rotation becomes 2\arctan(h\lVert\omega\rVert/2) instead of h\lVert\omega\rVert, an error of about (h\lVert\omega\rVert)^3/12 per step that accumulates.

    The cube is an ideal test: with three equal principal inertias, Euler's rotation equation has no gyroscopic term, so \omega stays constant and the exact answer is a closed form. MJWarp's error stays at the float32 level (about 1e-4 degrees) for every timestep, because each step is an exact rotation. The naive error grows with the cube of h: 15.6 degrees after 500 steps at dt = 0.05. The widget below replays these exact numbers.

    3EULER: semi-implicit, with implicit damping

    MuJoCo's default integrator is called Euler, but it is not the textbook explicit Euler. It is semi-implicit Euler:

    \dot q_{n+1} = \dot q_n + h\,\ddot q_n, \qquad q_{n+1} = q_n \oplus h\,\dot q_{n+1}

    (\oplus is the quaternion update of Section 2 for free and ball joints, plain addition for the rest). Using the new velocity in the position update is what makes the method nearly energy-preserving for oscillations. Explicit Euler, which uses the old velocity, pumps energy into every oscillator; Ch 3 showed both on a spring.

    Recall from Ch 3

    The phase-portrait figure in Ch 3 showed explicit Euler spiralling out while semi-implicit Euler stays on a closed loop. The widget below extends it with the methods of this chapter, on the same spring.

    Play with both sliders. Three lessons carry over to the real integrators:

    1. Semi-implicit Euler keeps a closed loop for an undamped spring as long as h\omega < 2; its energy wobbles but does not drift. Explicit Euler always spirals out.
    2. Treating a force implicitly adds numerical damping. Backward Euler spirals in even with c = 0. MuJoCo's IMPLICIT integrator shows a milder version of this on the pendulum in Section 8, because it treats the velocity-dependent Coriolis forces implicitly.
    3. Stiff damping breaks explicit damping. Raise c to 3 at a large h: the curve that takes the damping force explicitly flies off, the one that takes it implicitly ("MuJoCo Euler") calmly decays.

    Implicit damping, the one implicit part of EULER

    Joint damping produces a force -b\,\dot q. Taken explicitly, a pure damper gives \dot q_{n+1} = \dot q_n (1 - h b / m): once h b/m > 2 the factor is below -1 and the velocity flips sign and grows every step. MuJoCo instead evaluates the damping force at the end of the step. Write D = \partial f / \partial \dot q for the damping derivative (here D = -B, a diagonal of damping coefficients). Then

    M \ddot q_{\text{new}} = f + h D\,\ddot q_{\text{new}} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; (M + hB)\,\ddot q_{\text{new}} = f = M\ddot q,

    where M \ddot q is the total force that forward already balanced (smooth forces plus constraint forces). For a single damper this multiplies the velocity by 1/(1 + hb/m) per step: always between 0 and 1, stable for every b. This is the code, from forward.py:

    @event_scope
def euler(m: Model, d: Data):
  """Euler integrator, semi-implicit in velocity."""
  # integrate damping implicitly
  if not (m.opt.disableflags & (DisableBit.EULERDAMP | DisableBit.DAMPER)):
    qacc = wp.empty((d.nworld, m.nv), dtype=float)

    # Compute damping derivative
    damp_deriv = wp.empty((d.nworld, m.nv), dtype=float)
    wp.launch(_compute_damping_deriv, dim=(d.nworld, m.nv), ...)   # inputs elided

    # Clone M, add the damping to the diagonal, and factor-solve. factor_solve_i factors each block
    # per-block (packed dense and/or sparse LDL); the scratch qLD matches d.qLD.
    M = wp.clone(d.M)
    qLD = wp.empty_like(d.qLD)
    qLDiagInv = wp.empty((d.nworld, m.nv), dtype=float)
    wp.launch(_euler_damp_qfrc, dim=(d.nworld, m.nv), ...)        # M[diag] += dt * damp_deriv
    smooth.factor_solve_i(m, d, M, qLD, qLDiagInv, qacc, d.efc.Ma)
    _advance(m, d, qacc)
  else:
    _advance(m, d, d.qacc)

    Tip: what the flags control

    The solve runs unless DisableBit.EULERDAMP or DisableBit.DAMPER is set (MJCF <flag eulerdamp="disable"/>). It does not check whether the model has any damping: an undamped model still pays for the clone and the factorization every step. If your model has no joint or actuator damping, disabling EULERDAMP gives the identical result for less work.

    The widget below shows the per-step velocity of the pure damper for any hb/m, and overlays the real MJWarp values from Example 13.4 when the slider sits on one of its eight ratios.

    The MJWarp numbers match the formulas to every printed digit. With EULERDAMP off, the four worlds above hb/m = 2 blow up, exactly as predicted. The world at hb/m = 1 stops dead in one step (factor 0), which is also the explicit method being "too clever": it is the edge of overshooting.

    Pitfall (4.0.1 wheel): njmax=0 freezes damped velocities

    The last line of Example 13.4 is a real trap. When d.njmax == 0, the 4.0.1 solver.solve takes a shortcut: it copies qacc_smooth into qacc and returns without filling d.efc.Ma. Euler's damping solve uses d.efc.Ma as its right-hand side, so it solves against zeros: qacc = 0 and nothing moves. The same right-hand side feeds IMPLICITFAST and IMPLICIT. Never pass njmax=0 to make_data with these integrators in 4.0.1 (the default njmax is never 0).

    Version note

    The dev tree (8f5b9f1) fixes this: its solver.solve handles njmax == 0 by also computing support.mul_m(m, d, d.efc.Ma, d.qacc) and zeroing qfrc_constraint.

    4RK4: four forwards per step

    The classic fourth-order Runge-Kutta method evaluates the dynamics at four points inside the step and averages the slopes. For a state y = (q, \dot q) with \dot y = F(y):

    k_1 = F(y_n),\quad k_2 = F(y_n + \tfrac h2 k_1),
    k_3 = F(y_n + \tfrac h2 k_2),\quad k_4 = F(y_n + h k_3),
    y_{n+1} = y_n + h\big(\tfrac16 k_1 + \tfrac13 k_2 + \tfrac13 k_3 + \tfrac16 k_4\big)

    The error per unit time shrinks with h^4: halve the step and the error drops sixteen times. MJWarp stores the method as a Butcher tableau, A = [0.5, 0.5, 1.0] # diagonal only and B = [1/6, 1/3, 1/3, 1/6]. Each F is a full _forward: kinematics, collision, constraints, forces and the constraint solve. Step through rungekutta4:

    The four RK4 stages

    forward.py::rungekutta4Illustrative curve
    A = [0.5, 0.5, 1.0]  # diagonal only
B = [1/6, 1/3, 1/3, 1/6]
qpos_t0, qvel_t0 = clone(d.qpos), clone(d.qvel)
qvel_stage = clone(d.qvel)
_rk_accumulate(m, d, B[0], ...)  # k1
for i in range(3):
  a, b = A[i], B[i + 1]
  _rk_perturb_state(m, d, a, ...)
  _forward(m, d, skip_sensor=True)
  _rk_correct_velocity(...)  # RKMK
  _rk_accumulate(m, d, b, ...)

qacc_last = clone(d.qacc)
_advance(m, d, qacc_rk, qvel_rk)
d.qacc = qacc_rk; warmstart = qacc_last

    RKMK: Runge-Kutta on rotations

    The stages add slopes, which is fine for hinge angles but not for quaternions. MJWarp follows MuJoCo C and uses the Runge-Kutta-Munthe-Kaas idea: each stage rotates the start orientation by a rotation vector r = a\,h\,\omega_{\text{prev}}, and the stage's angular velocity is converted into the rate of change of that vector before it is averaged. _rk_correct_velocity does this per joint with math.deriv_expmap(rot, vel), which computes \omega + \tfrac12 r\times\omega + c\, r\times(r\times\omega) with the Bortz coefficient c = (1 - \tfrac x2\cot\tfrac x2)/x^2, x = \lVert r\rVert (a port of mju_derivExpmap). Hinge and slide joints are copied unchanged; the first stage needs no correction because its rotation vector is zero.

    What RK4 leaves in Data

    Four forwards write many fields four times. Which values survive matters when you read Data after a step:

    Field after mjw.step (RK4)HoldsWhy

    sensordatastart of stepstages 2-4 call _forward(skip_sensor=True)

    qacc_warmstartlast stage's qaccthe most recent solve: best warm start
    energylast stage (state \approx t+h)_energy_pos/_energy_vel run in every _forward

    The jointpos sensor reads 1.07492, the angle before the step, in MJWarp and in C alike. The energy after the step is the last stage's (it matches the post-step state to float32 noise), and it differs from the start-of-step energy. C MuJoCo behaves the same way, so this is a convention, not a bug, but it explains why Example 13.3 calls mjw.forward before reading d.energy.

    Version note: RK4 in the dev tree

    The dev tree (8f5b9f1) also perturbs d.time in each stage (_rk_stage_time: time-dependent forces see t + a h) and leaves d.act_dot at the last stage's value "as in mj_RungeKutta", passing the weighted act_dot_rk to _advance instead. In the 4.0.1 wheel the stages run at the start time and d.act_dot ends up holding the weighted value.

    step2 with RK4 runs Euler

    RK4 needs to call _forward at perturbed states, so it cannot be split around a user callback. The last lines of step2 say so:

      # integrate with Euler, implicitfast, or discrete
  if m.opt.integrator in (IntegratorType.IMPLICITFAST, IntegratorType.IMPLICIT):
    implicit(m, d)
  elif m.opt.integrator == IntegratorType.DISCRETE:
    discrete(m, d)
  else:
    # note: RK4 defaults to Euler
    euler(m, d)

    Pitfall: under RK4 the control callback runs four times

    _forward calls m.callback.control before fwd_actuation, and rungekutta4 calls _forward(m, d, skip_sensor=True) for stages 2-4. So with RK4 the callback runs once per stage, at each of the 4 stage states, and in stages 2-4 it sees perturbed qpos/qvel while d.sensordata still holds the start-of-step values. Keep the callback a cheap, side-effect-free function of the state: a policy that reads sensordata, keeps internal state or samples noise behaves differently than in a step1/step2 loop.

    5IMPLICITFAST: implicit in velocity, symmetric

    EULER treats one force implicitly: joint damping. IMPLICITFAST generalizes the idea to every velocity-dependent smooth force it can differentiate cheaply: joint, tendon and actuator damping, velocity servos and other actuators whose force depends on velocity, and fluid drag. Let D = \partial(\text{qfrc\_passive} + \text{qfrc\_actuator}) / \partial \dot q. A step that evaluates these forces at the end-of-step velocity, linearized, is

    (M - hD)\,\ddot q_{\text{new}} = M\ddot q \quad(\text{right-hand side } \texttt{d.efc.Ma}),\qquad \text{then } \texttt{\_advance}(\ddot q_{\text{new}}).

    The word "fast" means that D leaves out the derivative of the Coriolis and centrifugal forces (that is IMPLICIT, next section). Without them D is symmetric for the usual damping and actuator terms, and it has the sparsity of M, so M - hD can be stored in M's own sparse layout and factored with the same Cholesky/LDL machinery (smooth.factor_solve_i). The positions are still updated semi-implicitly by _advance: only velocities are implicit.

    Recall from Ch 11

    factor_m writes qLD; solving with an old qLD gives wrong qacc; Ch 11 §9. IMPLICITFAST never touches d.qLD: it factors M - hD into a scratch qLD allocated in the step.

      elif (m.opt.disableflags & (DisableBit.ACTUATION | DisableBit.SPRING | DisableBit.DAMPER)) != (
    DisableBit.ACTUATION | DisableBit.SPRING | DisableBit.DAMPER
  ):
    # qDeriv is in M-structure; the scratch qLD matches d.qLD (per-block).
    qDeriv = wp.empty((d.nworld, m.nC), dtype=float)
    qLD = wp.empty_like(d.qLD)
    qLDiagInv = wp.empty((d.nworld, m.nv), dtype=float)

    smooth.factor_solve_i(m, d, qDeriv, qLD, qLDiagInv, qacc, d.efc.Ma)
    implicit_free_body(m, d, d.efc.Ma, qacc, solve=True)
    _advance(m, d, qacc)
  else:
    _advance(m, d, d.qacc)

    Read it from the top:

    • Shortcut. If ACTUATION, SPRING and DAMPER are all disabled there is nothing to differentiate, and the step is plain semi-implicit Euler.
    • deriv_smooth_vel is public (mjw.deriv_smooth_vel(m, d, out)). Its docstring says out receives M - dt * qDeriv. It starts from d.M and subtracts h times the derivatives of actuator forces (per actuator, through the sparse actuator moment), DOF damping, tendon damping and the ellipsoid and box fluid models.
    • Free bodies get their own solve. _implicit_free_body_reset_m copies the plain M rows back for every standalone free body (m.body_freeadr), and implicit_free_body(..., solve=True) then overwrites those six rows of qacc with A^{-1}\,\texttt{Ma}, where A is the body's 6×6 block of M - hD including the gyroscopic bias derivative. So a spinning free body gets the rotational term that the rest of the tree only gets under IMPLICIT.

    Not supported: the implicitfast midpoint

    The MJWarp README lists "IMPLICITFAST midpoint integrator feature is not supported" among its differences from MuJoCo C. MJWarp's IMPLICITFAST is the plain backward-in-velocity step shown here.

    Pitfall: flex elasticity needs DISCRETE

    check_discrete, called at the top of every _forward, raises ValueError("flex elasticity is no longer integrated implicitly under integrator='implicit' and 'implicitfast': set integrator='discrete', or 'Euler' with a small timestep") for models with flex stiffness under IMPLICIT or IMPLICITFAST. Section 7 has the full rule list.

    6IMPLICIT: adding the RNE derivative, solved by LU

    IMPLICIT keeps everything IMPLICITFAST does and adds the velocity derivative of the bias force c(q, \dot q): the Coriolis and centrifugal terms that the RNE pass of Ch 12 computes. Those forces are quadratic in velocity, so their derivative depends on the current velocity and is not symmetric. The comment in implicit_qlu gets the sign right: "inverse dynamics Coriolis enters equations of motion as -qfrc_bias, so M - dt * df/dv = M + dt * d(qfrc_bias)/dv".

    \Big(M - hD + h\,\tfrac{\partial c}{\partial \dot q}\Big)\,\ddot q_{\text{new}} = M\ddot q
    def implicit_qlu(m: Model, d: Data, qLU: Optional[wp.array2d[float]] = None):
  """Sets qLU (d.qLU if None) = M - dt * qDeriv in D-structure, including RNE derivatives."""
  qLU = d.qLU if qLU is None else qLU

  # Smooth velocity derivatives into M-structure
  qH_M = wp.empty((d.nworld, m.nC), dtype=float)
  derivative.deriv_smooth_vel(m, d, qH_M)

  # Map qH_M (M-structure) to qLU (D-structure) via mapM2D.
  wp.launch(_map_m2d, dim=(d.nworld, m.nD), inputs=[m.mapM2D, qH_M], outputs=[qLU])

  # Compute RNE derivatives, scale by timestep, and add in-place to qLU.
  derivative.deriv_rne_vel(m, d, qLU)

    A non-symmetric matrix needs a full sparsity pattern (upper and lower), the "D-structure" with m.nD entries, and an LU factorization instead of Cholesky. Then implicit calls smooth.factor_solve_lu(m, d, d.qLU, qacc, d.efc.Ma), applies the same free-body solve as IMPLICITFAST, and calls _advance.

    The memory bill of deriv_rne_vel

    derivative.deriv_rne_vel differentiates the two RNE sweeps of Ch 12: a forward pass for the velocity and acceleration derivatives, a backward pass that accumulates body-force derivatives from leaves to root, and a projection onto the joints. To do this it allocates, on every call (there is a TODO(team): consider caching these allocations):

      Dcvel = wp.zeros((d.nworld, m.nbody, m.nv), dtype=wp.spatial_vector)
  Dcdof_dot = wp.zeros((d.nworld, m.nv, m.nv), dtype=wp.spatial_vector)
  Dcacc = wp.zeros((d.nworld, m.nbody, m.nv), dtype=wp.spatial_vector)
  Dcfrcbody = wp.zeros((d.nworld, m.nbody, m.nv), dtype=wp.spatial_vector)

    A wp.spatial_vector is 6 float32 = 24 bytes, so the scratch is 24\cdot n_\text{world}\,(3\,n_\text{body} n_v + n_v^2) bytes per step. Example 13.5 prints it for the humanoid: about 197 MiB per step at 4096 worlds and over 3 GiB at 65536. Under CUDA graph capture these allocations come from the graph's memory pool, but they still have to fit.

    7DISCRETE: an overview

    Forward link: Ch 23 owns this topic

    DISCRETE solves with an effective metric built from passive stiffness (eff_build/eff_solve); Ch 23 §7.

    The integrator function itself is one line:

    @event_scope
def discrete(m: Model, d: Data):
  """Advance simulation using discrete integrator."""
  _advance(m, d, d.qacc)

    That is because DISCRETE moves its work into forward. The IntegratorType docstring calls it a "discrete step map: constraint solve in the effective metric". Each forward stage does an extra piece, and each piece returns immediately for the other integrators:

    Stage (forward.py)Extra call under DISCRETERole

    fwd_velocityderivative.eff_shiftvelocity-dependent shifts of the metric
    fwd_actuationderivative.eff_actuationactuator contributions

    Without flex, as on our pendulum, the extra terms vanish and DISCRETE gives the Euler answer up to round-off (Examples 13.1 and 13.2). The rules that decide which integrator a flex model may use live in check_discrete:

    8Measuring: parity, energy and convergence

    Before comparing integrators with each other, check each one against the reference implementation. Every integrator should follow mujoco.mj_step with the same integrator to float32 accuracy.

    All five stay within about 1.5e-6 over 3 seconds, with and without damping. That is float32 round-off: MJWarp is the same algorithm as C MuJoCo, which is what makes the differences below properties of the methods.

    The energy race

    Take the undamped double pendulum, give it a big swing (q = (1.2, 0)) and a coarse step (dt = 0.02), and watch the total energy, which physics says is constant.

    The race has three outcomes, and each one is a property you can now explain:

    • Euler gains 19%. Semi-implicit Euler preserves a nearby energy for linear oscillators, but this pendulum is strongly nonlinear at a coarse step, and the error grows.
    • IMPLICITFAST is identical to Euler. The model has no velocity-dependent smooth force, so D = 0 and M - hD = M.
    • IMPLICIT loses 6.6%. It treats the Coriolis forces implicitly, and implicit treatment adds numerical dissipation, like backward Euler in Figure 5.
    • RK4 holds 25.875 J to all printed digits, at four forwards per step.

    A convergence study in one launch

    Energy is one yardstick; accuracy is another. How fast does the error fall as the step shrinks? Because m.opt.timestep is a batched field, each world can have its own timestep. Assign an array of length nworld, and all worlds step together, each with its own dt.

    Read the chart from right to left. The first-order methods fall with slope 1: halve dt, halve the error. RK4 falls much faster, then hits a floor near 1e-6 and even rises again: at small steps it takes more steps, and each step adds float32 round-off. The fitted RK4 slope over the points above the floor is above 4 because the largest steps (0.1 s) are not yet in the asymptotic range. The lesson for GPU simulation is practical: in float32, RK4's accuracy is used up at moderate timesteps; a smaller dt buys nothing below the floor.

    Tip: per-world fields make sweeps free

    The same trick works for every batched option: gravity, wind, density, solver tolerance. One launch, one model, one compile. Integer options (integrator, solver, iterations) are one value for all worlds. To compare values, run one model per value or set the int on the model between runs (Example 13.1, way B); a new integrator compiles its kernels on first use, and any captured CUDA graph must be recaptured.

    Do not forget the timestep

    An integrator is only half of the decision. A cheaper integrator at a smaller dt can beat a costlier one at a larger dt, and contact-rich scenes are usually limited by contact stiffness (solref, Ch 2 and Ch 19), not by the integrator's order. Measure: Example 13.9's per-world sweep and Exercise 13.2 do exactly that.

    12Summary

    Key takeaways

    • step = forward + one of euler, rungekutta4, implicit, discrete, chosen by the Python int m.opt.integrator. All end in _advance: activation, then velocity, then position with the new velocity (semi-implicit), plastic updates, history, time and overflow, warm start.
    • Free and ball joints rotate with math.quat_integrate, an exact rotation per step; "add and renormalize" drifts with h^3 per step.
    • EULER treats joint and actuator damping implicitly by solving (M + hB)\ddot q = Ma; that is why stiff damping is stable. EULERDAMP disables it.
    • RK4 runs three extra forwards, uses RKMK for rotations, keeps sensors from the start of the step and leaves energy from the last stage. It is about 4x Euler's cost; step2 runs Euler instead.
    • IMPLICITFAST factors the symmetric M - hD from deriv_smooth_vel; IMPLICIT adds the non-symmetric RNE derivative and solves with LU, one thread per world, with per-step scratch that grows as nworld·nbody·nv.
    • DISCRETE does its work inside forward (effective metric, Ch 23); check_discrete decides what flex models may use.
    • m.opt.timestep is per world: convergence and stability sweeps are one launch. Changing the integrator is a plain int assignment on m.opt, but it needs a CUDA graph recapture.
    • 4.0.1 pitfall: njmax=0 leaves d.efc.Ma unset, freezing Euler/implicit damping solves.
