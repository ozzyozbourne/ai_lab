Chapter 12's tree could not store a value larger than a quarter of a page, could not delete, and its file only grew. This page shows the three additions: a large value spills into a chain of overflow pages, a page that empties goes to a free list, and the next allocation takes a page from that list before the file grows. Text of the chapter: README.md.

1. One file, sixteen operations

The tree on top is the logical view; the row of cards under it is the same file, page by page, in file order. Solid arrows are overflow chains (leaf cell → first overflow page → next). Dashed arrows are the free list (metapage → trunk page → the free pages it lists). In the tree, 25 +2p means "key 25, and its value continues in 2 overflow pages". The sixteen operations are exactly part 1 of the chapter's Zig and Rust programs; the checks at the bottom of this page compare the result with their output.

2. How much of a value stays in the leaf?

A leaf cell may keep at most X = 1088 value bytes, so that any three cells fit in a page (the split argument of chapter 12 needs that). A larger value keeps a prefix and a 4-byte page id in the leaf. The size of the prefix follows SQLite's rule: if the bytes left over after filling whole overflow pages are few (K ≤ X), they stay in the leaf and every overflow page is full; otherwise the leaf keeps only the minimum M = 544. Type a size.

3. Your own file

Starts empty. Keys are 0 to 99 (two digits, so at most 100 keys and a tree of at most two levels: a limit of this page, not of the format); a value may have up to 65536 bytes. Each cell of the page map is one page of the file, labelled with its page id. Try a large value, overwrite it with a small one, then put another large value and watch the page ids come back. Delete all keys shows the file not shrinking: every page ends up on the free list.
