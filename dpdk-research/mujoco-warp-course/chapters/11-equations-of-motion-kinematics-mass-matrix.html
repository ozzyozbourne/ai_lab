    Equations of Motion, Kinematics and the Mass Matrix

    Every step of every world starts with the same question: where is everything, and how heavy does it feel to each joint? This chapter derives the equation MJWarp solves, then follows the data through the first two pipeline stations: forward kinematics over tree branches, the com-based spatial quantities, the sparse mass matrix and the factorization that turns forces into accelerations.

    Advanced About 90 min· Prerequisites: Ch 3, Ch 7

    You will learn

    • State the equation of motion M(q)\,\ddot q + c(q,\dot q) = \tau + J^\top f and name the MJWarp field behind every term.
    • Read 6-D spatial vectors in MJWarp's (rotation, translation) order and the com frame used by cdof, cinert and cvel.
    • Derive M and c of a 2-link arm by hand and see M as kinetic energy.
    • Explain branch-parallel kinematics and the contiguous-subtree trick that replace level-by-level sweeps.
    • Read the CSR mass matrix, convert it to dense, and use factor_m, solve_m, mul_m and jac.
    • Avoid the stale-factor trap, and (going deeper) predict which of four factorization paths each block of M takes.

    Before you start: recap

    • A rigid body has 6 DOF: position + orientation (quat), linear + angular velocity (Ch 3 §4). Its mass properties are a mass, a centre of mass and a 3x3 inertia tensor, diagonal in the principal axes (Ch 3 §5).
    • Joints remove freedom: a robot is described by a few joint coordinates instead of 6 numbers per body (Ch 3 §7). nq counts position numbers, nv counts DOFs (Ch 2 §3).
    • MuJoCo quats are (w,x,y,z); Ch 2 §3.
    • Every Data array is (nworld, ...) and one kernel thread usually handles one (world, element) pair (Ch 6 §1, Ch 4 §3).
    • forward runs position, velocity, actuation and acceleration stages, then the solver; the force budget qfrc_smooth = qfrc_passive - qfrc_bias + qfrc_actuator + qfrc_applied (Ch 7 §2, Ch 7 §5).
    • Calling stages by hand needs exactly one factorization per new qpos (Ch 7 §3); this chapter owns the full story.

    1The equation of motion in generalized coordinates

    Recall from Ch 3

    A rigid body has 6 DOF: position + orientation (quat), linear + angular velocity; Ch 3. For one free body, Newton and Euler give m\,\dot v = F and I\dot\omega + \omega\times I\omega = \tau.

    Ch 3 wrote Newton's law body by body in Cartesian coordinates. A robot is a tree of bodies tied together by joints, so most of those 6-per-body numbers are not free. MuJoCo instead writes one equation for the whole tree in generalized coordinates q (qpos) with velocities \dot q (qvel, one number per DOF). This is the equation of motion, often called the manipulator equation:

    M(q)\,\ddot q \;+\; c(q,\dot q) \;=\; \tau \;+\; J^\top f

    Read it as Newton's m a = F, one row per DOF:

    • M(q), the mass matrix, is an n_v\times n_v symmetric positive definite matrix. Row i says how much "inertia" joint i feels, and the off-diagonal entries say how accelerating one joint pushes on another. It depends on the pose q only.
    • c(q,\dot q), the bias force, holds everything that acts even when \ddot q = 0: gravity, plus the Coriolis and centrifugal terms that appear because the bodies move on curved paths.
    • \tau are the applied joint-space forces: springs, dampers and other passive forces, actuator forces and anything you put in qfrc_applied (or xfrc_applied, mapped to joints).
    • J^\top f are the constraint forces: contacts, joint limits and equalities. The solver (Ch 19-20) finds f; this chapter only needs to know that it arrives as one more joint-space force vector.

    Every term has a home in mjw.Data. Hover, tap or tab to a card to see which field holds it and which pipeline stage writes it.

    Recall from Ch 7

    In Ch 7 §5 the force budget was an identity you checked numerically. It is the equation above, rearranged: M\,\ddot q_\text{smooth} = \tau - c is qfrc_smooth, and the solver adds J^\top f.

    Written in MJWarp field names, the forward dynamics that mjw.forward computes is

    \underbrace{M}_{\texttt{d.M}}\,\underbrace{\ddot q}_{\texttt{d.qacc}} \,=\, \underbrace{\texttt{qfrc\_passive} - \texttt{qfrc\_bias} + \texttt{qfrc\_actuator} + \texttt{qfrc\_applied}}_{\texttt{d.qfrc\_smooth}} \,+\, \underbrace{J^\top f}_{\texttt{qfrc\_constraint}}

    Two numbers make this cheap. First, M and c are never built from symbolic formulas: recursive tree algorithms compute them in time roughly linear in the number of bodies. Second, MJWarp computes them for thousands of worlds at once, one GPU thread per (world, element). The rest of this chapter follows exactly those computations, in pipeline order.

    2Generalized coordinates, and M as kinetic energy

    Take the shared double pendulum PENDULUM (model M2): two capsules of mass 1 and length 0.5, hinged about the world y axis. In Cartesian coordinates it has two bodies, 12 pose DOFs (6 per body), cut down to 2 by 10 hinge constraint equations holding it together. In joint coordinates it has q = (q_1, q_2), two numbers, and no constraints left to enforce: every q is a valid pose. That is the point of reduced coordinates: the joints can never come apart.

    Cartesian (maximal)Generalized (reduced, MuJoCo)
    state of PENDULUM2 bodies x (pos, quat, lin vel, ang vel) = 26 numbersqpos (nq = 2), qvel (nv = 2)
    jointsconstraint equations the solver must keep satisfiedbuilt into the coordinates
    inertiablock diagonal: m_b I_3 and I_b per bodydense coupled M(q), 2\times 2
    count that mattersbodiesDOFs: hinge/slide 1, ball 3, free 6

    Where does M(q) come from? From kinetic energy. Every body's velocity is linear in \dot q: v_b = J_b(q)\,\dot q. Plug that into the sum of the bodies' kinetic energies and you get a quadratic form in \dot q:

    T = \sum_b \tfrac12 m_b \lVert v_{b}\rVert^2 + \tfrac12 \omega_b^\top I_b\, \omega_b \;=\; \tfrac12\, \dot q^\top \Big(\sum_b m_b J_{v,b}^\top J_{v,b} + J_{\omega,b}^\top I_b J_{\omega,b}\Big)\dot q \;=\; \tfrac12\,\dot q^\top M(q)\,\dot q

    So M is symmetric, and positive definite because a moving system always has positive kinetic energy. The Lagrange equations \tfrac{d}{dt}\tfrac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} - \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial q} = \tau with L = T - V then give exactly M\ddot q + c = \tau, where c collects the velocity-squared terms (Christoffel symbols of M) and the gravity gradient \partial V/\partial q.

    For the planar 2-link arm with link masses m_i, com distances l_{c,i} from each hinge, first-link length l_1 and inertias I_i about the com:

    M(q) = \begin{pmatrix} I_1 + I_2 + m_1 l_{c1}^2 + m_2\,(l_1^2 + l_{c2}^2 + 2 l_1 l_{c2}\cos q_2) & I_2 + m_2\,(l_{c2}^2 + l_1 l_{c2}\cos q_2) \\ \cdot & I_2 + m_2 l_{c2}^2 \end{pmatrix}

    Only q_2 appears: rotating the whole arm about the shoulder does not change how heavy it is. Bending the elbow does. Drag the sliders and watch the mass matrix and the kinetic-energy ellipse \dot q^\top M \dot q = 1 (the set of joint velocities with the same energy) change.

    Example 11.1 does this derivation in NumPy and checks it against MuJoCo C at three random states. It reads the link parameters from the compiled MjModel (body_mass, body_ipos, body_inertia, body_iquat) so nothing is typed in by hand. It uses MuJoCo C only: the errors are at float64 round-off.

    Note

    M_{22} = 0.0885 in every state: the elbow only ever moves link 2, whose inertia about the elbow does not depend on any angle. M_{11} is largest with the arm straight (q_2 = 0) and smallest folded (q_2 = \pm\pi). mj_fullM takes (m, d, dst) in MuJoCo 4.

    The kinetic-energy identity is worth checking in MJWarp itself, in every world at once. With the energy flag enabled, d.energy[:, 1] holds the kinetic energy (written by sensor.energy_vel). Example 11.2 compares it with \tfrac12 \dot q^\top M \dot q from the CSR d.M (densified with a helper you will meet properly in §8) and with a body-by-body sum in MuJoCo C using mj_objectVelocity.

    Why not just write the formulas for every robot? Because the cost explodes. A 27-DOF humanoid has 27^2 mass-matrix entries, each a long trigonometric expression, and c has 27^3 Christoffel terms. Tree algorithms reuse partial results along the kinematic tree instead: positions flow root to leaf, inertias and forces flow leaf to root, and the cost is about linear in the number of bodies (plus the nonzeros of M). Those two directions, outward and inward, are the shape of everything that follows.

    3Spatial algebra: 6-vectors and the com frame

    Tree algorithms move velocities and forces between bodies all the time. Doing that with separate 3-vectors (angular velocity here, linear velocity of some point there) is error-prone, so MuJoCo and MJWarp pack them into 6-D spatial vectors. In MJWarp the type is Warp's wp.spatial_vector (6 floats). The order is always rotation first, translation second: wp.spatial_top(v) is the rotational 3-vector, wp.spatial_bottom(v) the translational one.

    Two kinds of spatial vector share this storage:

    • Motion vectors (twists): (\omega, v). Examples: cvel (body velocity), cdof (the motion one DOF produces at unit speed), cdof_dot, cacc.
    • Force vectors (wrenches): (\tau, f), a torque and a force. Examples: cfrc_int, cfrc_ext. A joint force is the dot product of a motion and a force: qfrc_bias[dof] = dot(cdof[dof], cfrc_int[body]) in smooth.py::_qfrc_bias.

    Pitfall

    d.xfrc_applied is also a wp.spatial_vector per body, but it is (force, torque), applied at the body's centre of mass xipos, as in MuJoCo C. support.py::_apply_ft_gather dots the linear part of cdof with its first three numbers. Do not reuse the cfrc_* convention for it.

    The com frame

    A spatial vector needs a reference point for its translational part. MuJoCo picks one per kinematic tree: the com frame, a frame with world axes centred at c = subtree_com[body_rootid[b]], the centre of mass of the whole tree. Every quantity whose name starts with c uses it: cdof, cinert, crb, cvel, cacc, cfrc_int, cfrc_ext. Because all bodies of a tree share the same point, adding a child's velocity contribution to its parent's is a plain 6-vector addition: no cross products to shift the point. That is the whole reason for the convention.

    The price is one rule you must remember when you read cvel: its linear part is the velocity of the point of the body that happens to sit at c right now, not of the body origin. To get the velocity of any point p on body b:

    v_p = v_b + \omega_b \times (p - c), \qquad \texttt{cvel}[b] = (\omega_b,\; v_b)

    cinert: a 6x6 inertia in 10 numbers

    A body's mass properties in the com frame form a 6x6 spatial inertia. It is symmetric and has structure, so MJWarp stores it as a vec10 (types.py): smooth.py::_cinert rotates the principal inertia body_inertia by ximat, shifts it to c with the parallel-axis theorem and writes

    Example 11.3 reads d.cvel for the pendulum, splits it into (angular, linear) and checks the reference-point rule twice: for link 1, which spins about a fixed hinge anchor a, the linear part must be \omega\times(c - a); for both links, v_b + \omega_b\times(x_{i,b} - c) must equal a finite difference of the com position xipos.

    Link 2's angular velocity is \dot q_1 + \dot q_2 = 1.5 + 2.0 = 3.5 rad/s: in a chain, twists add. That addition is literally the code of smooth.py::_comvel_branch: cvel += cdof[dofid] * qvel[dofid], walking from root to leaf. We will see the same walk for poses in §5.

    4Joint math in kinematics, and what kinematics writes

    Forward kinematics turns qpos into poses. For each body, MJWarp starts from the parent's pose and the body's fixed offset (body_pos, body_quat), then applies the body's joints in order. Condensed from smooth.py::_kinematics_branch (4.0.1 wheel; per-world indexing of batched Model fields omitted):

    # fixed offset from the parent
xpos  = rot_vec_quat(body_pos[b], xquat[parent]) + xpos[parent]
xquat = mul_quat(xquat[parent], body_quat[b])
for each joint j of body b:
    xanchor[j] = rot_vec_quat(jnt_pos[j], xquat) + xpos      # joint anchor in world
    xaxis[j]   = rot_vec_quat(jnt_axis[j], xquat)            # joint axis in world
    HINGE: xquat = mul_quat(xquat, axis_angle_to_quat(jnt_axis[j], q - qpos0))
           xpos  = xanchor[j] - rot_vec_quat(jnt_pos[j], xquat)   # off-centre correction
    SLIDE: xpos += xaxis[j] * (q - qpos0)
    BALL:  xquat = mul_quat(xquat, normalize(qpos[adr:adr+4]))
           xpos  = xanchor[j] - rot_vec_quat(jnt_pos[j], xquat)   # off-centre correction
FREE (only joint of its body): xpos = qpos[adr:adr+3]; xquat = normalize(qpos[adr+3:adr+7])

    Three details matter in practice:

    • Off-centre anchors. A hinge whose pos is not the body origin rotates the body about the anchor, not about its origin. Rotating the frame alone would swing the body about the wrong point, so the kernel re-derives the origin from the anchor: xpos = xanchor - R(xquat) jnt_pos. Drag the slider below and compare with the ghost that skips the correction.
    • Relative to qpos0. Hinge and slide use q - qpos0 (the joint's ref), so a model whose joints have ref set is posed by the offset, exactly as in C.
    • Normalization. Free and ball quaternions in qpos are normalized when read, and the final xquat is normalized again. A slightly denormalized quaternion in qpos is harmless here.

    Pitfall

    d.cacc and d.cfrc_int after mjw.forward are not the post-solve values you get from MuJoCo C's mj_rnePostConstraint. forward.py calls smooth.rne_postconstraint only when m.opt.run_rne_postconstraint is set (default False, io.py) or a sensor needs it (m.sensor_rne_postconstraint). Set m.opt.run_rne_postconstraint = True before you read them.

    5Parallel over branches: how kinematics launches

    The outward sweep has a dependency: a body's pose needs its parent's pose. On a CPU, MuJoCo simply loops over bodies in order (parents always come first). On a GPU we want thousands of threads. Two designs are possible:

    1. Level by level. Group bodies by depth, launch one kernel per level, and let the launch boundary act as a barrier. The humanoid has 7 levels, so 6 launches (5 barriers between them; the world body never moves), most of them with very few threads.
    2. Branch by branch. Split the tree into root-to-leaf paths, one per leaf body, and give each path its own thread that walks the whole path in one launch. Bodies shared by several paths (the torso) are simply computed several times.

    MJWarp 4.0.1 does the second. io.py::put_model builds one branch per leaf body (a body with no children) and stores them flattened in m.body_branches, with m.body_branch_start marking where each one begins; m.nbranch is their count. Then smooth.py::kinematics launches _kinematics_branch with dim=(d.nworld, m.nbranch), followed by four per-element launches for xmat, the inertial frames, geoms and sites. Step through both designs on the humanoid:

    Five threads walk five branches

    smooth.py::_kinematics_branchTree from ex4

    Why is redundant work the better deal? Count it. The branch walk visits 22 bodies for 16 moving bodies (6 extra, the torso 5 times), but it is one launch in which each thread does at most 6 body updates in a row with no synchronization. The level design does exactly 16 updates, but needs 6 launches (5 barriers between them), and each launch has only 1 to 4 bodies, so 1 to 4 threads per world. Kernel launches and barriers cost microseconds each; one extra quaternion product costs nanoseconds. Trees in robotics are shallow and have few leaves, so the redundancy stays small.

    Note: a benign race

    All five threads of a world write xpos_out[worldid, torso]. That is safe because every thread computes the torso pose from the same inputs with the same instructions, so they all write bit-identical values, and each thread reads only poses it has written itself earlier in its own walk (or the world body, set once by make_data).

    Example 11.4 prints the branch arrays for the humanoid and the level grouping (m.body_tree) that the alternative would use.

    mjw.kinematics(m, d) runs only this stage. Example 11.5 gives every humanoid world its own random pose (free-joint position and orientation plus 21 hinge angles) and compares seven output fields with mujoco.mj_kinematics, world by world.

    Errors of a few 10^{-7} are float32 round-off (MJWarp computes in float32, C in float64). njnt = 22 (1 free + 21 hinges) is why xanchor has 22 rows while nv = 27: the free joint has one anchor but six DOFs.

    The velocity stage reuses the same branch arrays: smooth.py::com_vel launches _comvel_branch with dim=(d.nworld, m.nbranch) to accumulate cvel root to leaf (and cdof_dot on the way), and Ch 12's RNE forward pass _cacc_branch does the same for accelerations. Learn the pattern once; it appears three times per step.

    Version note

    In the dev tree (commit 8f5b9f1) kinematics is split into kinematics1 (the branch walk) and kinematics2 (inertial frames, geoms, sites), and the orientation outputs are quaternions (xiquat, geom_xquat, site_xquat) instead of matrices. Neither exists in the 4.0.1 wheel: there you call mjw.kinematics and read ximat, geom_xmat, site_xmat.

    Write your own kinematics kernel

    The general kernel handles any tree, any joint type, mocap bodies and batched model fields. When you know the model, a closed form is much less work. For the planar 2-link arm, the tip is

    p_\text{tip} = p_\text{base} + R_y(q_1)\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\-l_1\end{pmatrix} + R_y(q_1+q_2)\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\-l_2\end{pmatrix},\qquad R_y(a)\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\-l\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-l\sin a\\0\\-l\cos a\end{pmatrix}

    Example 11.11 writes that as a one-thread-per-world Warp kernel, checks it against d.site_xpos from mjw.kinematics and times both. It is the smallest useful custom kernel over MJWarp data (Ch 26 goes much further).

    On the CPU dev box the five launches of mjw.kinematics cost about six times one launch of the custom kernel at nworld = 8; at this size launch overhead dominates both. Do not read a GPU ratio into it: run the notebook on Colab to see yours.

    6Subtrees as contiguous ranges

    The inward sweeps (sum over everything below a body) have the opposite dependency: a body needs all its descendants. MJWarp avoids a level-by-level loop here with a numbering trick. MuJoCo's compiler numbers bodies depth first, so a parent always precedes its children and every subtree is a contiguous index range:

    \text{subtree}(b) = \{\, b,\; b+1,\; \dots,\; b + \texttt{body\_subtreenum}[b] - 1 \,\}

    io.py::put_model computes m.body_subtreenum (subtree size including the body) in one backward pass over the bodies. Now "sum over the subtree" is a plain loop over a range, and every body can do it independently: one thread per (world, body), one launch, no barriers. The work is \sum_b \texttt{subtreenum}[b], which for shallow trees is a small multiple of nbody (for the humanoid 73 terms for 17 bodies, 56 without the world body).

    Three kernels use the range, all with the same for j in range(b, b + body_subtreenum[b]) loop:

    Kernel (smooth.py)SumsOutputStage
    _subtree_combody_mass * xipos, divided by body_subtreemassd.subtree_comcom_pos (kinematics station)
    _crb_subtreecinert (10 numbers)d.crbcrb
    _cfrc_subtreeper-body RNE forcesd.cfrc_intrne in fwd_velocity (every forward, bias forces only); rne_postconstraint only when opt.run_rne_postconstraint or a sensor needs it (Ch 12)

    The world body is special. _subtree_com and _cfrc_subtree skip bodyid == 0, and small per-world kernels (_subtree_com_world, _cfrc_world) add up the tree roots instead, hopping from root to root with rootid += body_subtreenum[rootid]. That is the same range trick one level up: the roots tile the index range [1, nbody). _crb_subtree does not accumulate into the world body at all.

    7com_pos: subtree_com, cinert and cdof

    smooth.py::com_pos is the second half of the kinematics station. It makes four launches: _subtree_com and _subtree_com_world (§6), _cinert (§3, one thread per (world, body)) and _cdof (one thread per (world, joint)). The last one produces the most important array of this chapter.

    cdof[w, j] is the motion vector (in the com frame) that DOF j produces when it moves at unit speed and all other DOFs are still. _cdof writes, with c the tree's com and a the joint anchor:

    Jointcdof (rotation | translation)Why
    hinge(xaxis | xaxis × (c − xanchor))rotation about the axis through the anchor; the point at c moves on a circle
    slide(0 | xaxis)pure translation along the axis
    ball (3 DOF)(R ek | R ek × (c − xanchor)), k = 0,1,2rotations about the body's own axes (rows of xmatT)
    free (6 DOF)(0 | ek) then (R ek | R ek × (c − xanchor))world-axis translation, then body-axis rotation

    Why it matters: by linearity, a body's twist is the sum of the twists of all DOFs between it and the root,

    \texttt{cvel}[b] = \sum_{j \,\in\, \text{ancestors}(b)} \texttt{cdof}[j]\;\dot q_j

    so the cdof rows are the columns of the body's spatial Jacobian in the com frame. To get the ordinary Jacobian of a point p on body b, shift the reference point from c to p, for every DOF that is an ancestor of b:

    J_r[:, j] = \texttt{cdof}_\text{rot}[j], \qquad J_p[:, j] = \texttt{cdof}_\text{lin}[j] + \texttt{cdof}_\text{rot}[j] \times (p - c)

    This is support.py::jac_dof line for line (it returns zeros when body_isdofancestor[b, j] == 0). Hover a DOF in the figure to see its column on a 3-link arm.

    Look at the last column of jacp for body l3: all zeros. mj_jacBody uses the body origin xpos[l3], which sits exactly on joint 3's axis, so turning joint 3 does not move it. The hand site would move (Figure 8, dof 2).

    The station's last call is smooth.py::camlight, which places cameras and lights (two launches, _cam_local_to_global and _light_local_to_global). It needs subtree_com because the trackcom and targetbodycom modes aim at a subtree's centre of mass. Then the kinematics station is done: fwd_kinematics = kinematics, com_pos, camlight, flex, tendon (forward.py).

    8crb and the CSR mass matrix

    The crb station builds M with the composite rigid body algorithm. Two launches in smooth.py::crb:

    1. _crb_subtree, one thread per (world, body): crb[b] = sum of cinert over the subtree of b (§6). Because all cinert of a tree are in the same com frame, the sum is a plain 10-number addition. crb[b] is the inertia of "body b with everything below it welded rigid".
    2. _M, one thread per (world, DOF). For DOF i on body b_i it computes buf = inert_vec(crb[b_i], cdof[i]), the momentum of that composite body when DOF i moves at unit speed, and then projects it on every ancestor DOF:
      M_{ij} = \texttt{cdof}[j]^\top\, \texttt{crb}[b_i]\, \texttt{cdof}[i] \quad (j \text{ an ancestor of } i \text{ or } j = i), \qquad M_{ii} \mathrel{+}= \text{armature}_i

    Why only ancestors? If j is neither an ancestor nor a descendant of i (two different legs), moving one does not move any body the other moves, so M_{ij} = 0. M is sparse with a pattern fixed by the tree, and MJWarp stores only its lower triangle in CSR form:

    • d.M, shape (nworld, nC): the stored values, row after row;
    • M_rowadr[i]: where row i starts; M_rownnz[i]: how many entries it has;
    • M_colind[adr]: the column of each entry, ancestors in increasing order, the diagonal last.

    All three index arrays are computed by MuJoCo at compile time and copied into mjw.Model (m.M_rownnz, m.M_rowadr, m.M_colind); the examples read them from the MjModel. The diagonal term adds the joint's armature (dof_armature plus any actuator armature, util_misc.dof_armature_total); the same station then runs tendon_armature to add tendon armature.

    Filling CSR rows: one thread per DOF

    smooth.py::_M3-hinge arm of Example 11.7

    The pattern is easiest to see on a real robot. Each humanoid row stores the DOF's ancestors: the 6 free-joint DOFs first, then the chain to that limb. Hover or tap a row of the matrix, or use the slider (keyboard), to focus a row.

    Pitfall

    d.M is not an (nv, nv) array. Indexing d.M.numpy()[w].reshape(nv, nv) fails (or, worse, succeeds when nC happens to be a square) and gives garbage. Always go through M_rowadr / M_rownnz / M_colind, or use mjw.mul_m to apply M without reading it.

    Notice qLD shape: (8, 0): a diagonal block needs no factor at all, only the reciprocal diagonal in qLDiagInv. That is the first of four factorization paths; the block router below lists them, and §10 opens them up.

    Jacobians: mjw.jac

    jac is §7's formula as a kernel (support.py::_make_jac_kernel, launched with dim=(nworld, nv): one thread per (world, column)). You pass one world-space point and one body id per world, so every world can ask about a different point. It needs subtree_com and cdof, so run kinematics + com_pos (or fwd_position, forward) first. Pass None for jacp or jacr to skip one; the kernel is specialized at compile time (wp.static(has_jacp)).

    Dense or sparse: what is_sparse really switches

    io.py::is_sparse(mjm) returns True when opt.jacobian is auto and nv > 32 (otherwise it follows mj_isSparse). In the 4.0.1 wheel this flag selects the layout of the constraint Jacobian d.efc.J (dense (nworld, njmax_pad, nv_pad), or CSR (nworld, 1, njmax_nnz)); it does not affect M, which is always CSR and whose factorization is decided per block (types.py::Model docstring of is_sparse). Example 11.12 prints both layouts.

    The factorize subtlety (owner of the Ch 7 pitfall)

    The factor lives in d.qLD / d.qLDiagInv and is rewritten only when someone factors. Two stage functions can do it, and their defaults point in opposite directions:

    • fwd_position(m, d, factorize=True) (public default): after crb it calls smooth.factor_m.
    • fwd_acceleration(m, d, factorize=False) (public default): it calls smooth.solve_m, which only reads the stored factor. With factorize=True it calls the fused smooth.factor_solve_i(m, d, d.M, d.qLD, d.qLDiagInv, d.qacc_smooth, d.qfrc_smooth) instead.
    • forward.py::_forward (inside mjw.forward and mjw.step) uses the other pairing: fwd_position(m, d, factorize=False) then fwd_acceleration(m, d, factorize=True). Factor and solve fused in one pass is cheaper than two.

    So when you call stages by hand you need exactly one factorization per new qpos. Mix the non-factoring halves and solve_m uses the factor of the previous pose: no exception, no NaN, just a wrong qacc_smooth.

    Version note

    In the dev tree (commit 8f5b9f1) forward.py::fwd_acceleration(factorize=True), the half _forward uses, passes store_factor = (m.opt.solver == SolverType.CG) or (m.opt.noslip_iterations > 0) to smooth.factor_solve_i. With store_factor=False the dense-tile blocks are factored and solved in registers but their factor is not written to d.qLD; scalar, compact and sparse-LDL blocks are always written (factor_solve_i docstring; types.py: "forward stores dense blocks only for the CG solver"). So with the default Newton solver, after mjw.forward or mjw.step, d.qLD does not hold the tile factor of the current M: call mjw.factor_m(m, d) before solve_m on any model with a dense-tile block (7 to 64 DOFs, e.g. HUMANOID). The 4.0.1 wheel always stores the factor, which is what Figure 10 and Example 11.13 measure.

    Pitfall

    Calling stages by hand needs exactly one factorization per new qpos. Use fwd_position() + fwd_acceleration() or fwd_position(factorize=False) + fwd_acceleration(factorize=True). Never fwd_position(factorize=False) + fwd_acceleration(). The same rule applies to your own code: after anything that changes d.M (new qpos, a crb call, randomized masses), call factor_m before solve_m. In the dev tree, also call it after mjw.forward before your own solve_m when the model has a dense-tile block and the solver is not CG (version note after Figure 10).

    Two more facts from forward.py (4.0.1): with sleeping enabled, fwd_acceleration solves in a compacted dense space (solver.smooth_solve_compact, Ch 21), and with the DISCRETE integrator it uses derivative.eff_solve (Ch 23); step1 factors in fwd_position and step2 solves with the public default. The experimental opt.run_qacc_smooth = False can skip qacc_smooth altogether (smooth.skip_qacc_smooth, Ch 7).

    The block router, in one table

    M is block diagonal with one block per kinematic tree (DOFs of different trees never couple): io.py::_m_blocks reads them from tree_dofadr / tree_dofnum. At put_model time io.py::m_block_layout sends each block down one of four paths, and factor_m's docstring says exactly that: "Compact blocks use reciprocal diagonals, full small blocks use scalar Cholesky, larger dense blocks use tile Cholesky, and oversized blocks use sparse LDL."

    10Going deeper: the four factorization families

    Going deeper: m.M_tiles, qLD and the block router

    This section is for readers who want to know what factor_m actually launches. You can skip to the quiz and lose nothing you need for Ch 12-14.

    put_model stores the router's decision in four Model fields:

    • m.M_tiles: a tuple of types.TileSet(adr, size, elemid), one per distinct block size and path. adr lists the start DOF of every block of that size; an empty elemid selects the scalar kernel, a non-empty one holds the gather indices for the tile kernel. Grouping by size matters because tile shapes are compile-time constants: one kernel per size (@cache_kernel).
    • m.qLD_block_adr (nv,): for each DOF, the offset of its block's factor in the packed dense region of qLD, or the sentinels Q_LD_BLOCK_COMPACT = -2 / Q_LD_BLOCK_SPARSE = -1 (types.py).
    • m.qLD_block_total: the length of the packed dense region, \sum \text{size}^2 over scalar and tile blocks. The sparse LDL region, if any, starts right after it.
    • m.qLD_updates: the sparse level schedule, a tuple of wp.vec3i arrays (i, k, Madr_ki), one array per tree depth.

    Pick a model and click (or tab to and press Enter on) a block to see its path and open the matching stepper.

    Path 1 and 2: compact and scalar Cholesky (_small_cholesky_factorize_block)

    Blocks of at most 6 DOFs (M_BLOCK_SCALAR_MAX) are factored by one thread per (world, block) with plain loops, specialized on block_size at compile time. If qLD_block_adr[start] is the compact sentinel, the block is diagonal and the thread only writes qLDiagInv = 1 / M_ii. Otherwise it runs a textbook Cholesky M = U^\top U and stores U row-major. Because a full lower triangle in CSR is a packed triangle, the kernel reads M_{ji} at M_rowadr[start] + j(j+1)/2 + i with no index array. Step through the pendulum, with the real numbers of Example 11.8:

    CSR → packed upper factor, pendulum (2x2)

    smooth.py::_small_cholesky_factorize_blockValues from ex8
    for i in range(size):
    value = M[adr + i*(i+1)//2 + i]
    for k in range(i):
        value -= L[k*size + i] ** 2
    L[i*size + i] = sqrt(value)
    for j in range(i + 1, size):
        value = M[adr + j*(j+1)//2 + i]
        for k in range(i):
            value -= L[k*size + i] * L[k*size + j]
        L[i*size + j] = value / L[i*size + i]

    Path 3: tile Cholesky (_tile_cholesky_factorize_block)

    A tile is a small matrix that a whole thread block owns together, in fast shared memory. (Ch 4 previewed tiles and Ch 26 owns them: a tiled launch hands each logical index a whole block of threads that cooperate through shared memory.) Here the logical index is (world, block): _factor_blocks calls wp.launch_tiled(..., dim=(d.nworld, tile.adr.size), block_dim=m.block_dim.cholesky_factorize), which is 32 threads by default (types.BlockDim). On the CPU device Warp resolves block_dim to 1 unless wp.config.enable_cpu_blocks is set, so the same kernel runs in this course's CPU verification, just serially.

    The kernel body is five tile calls. The precomputed index tile block_elemid maps each of the 27 \times 27 = 729 dense slots of the humanoid block to its CSR address in d.M (mirrored for the upper half); structurally absent pairs carry an out-of-bounds index that reads as 0.

    Gather, factor, store: HUMANOID 27x27

    smooth.py::_tile_cholesky_factorize_blockPattern from ex8
    worldid, blk = wp.tid()
start = block_dof[blk]
idx = wp.tile_load(block_elemid, shape=(729,),
                   offset=(blk * 729,), storage="shared")
block = wp.tile_load_indexed(M_in[worldid], idx,
                   shape=(729,), storage="shared")
L = wp.tile_reshape(block, (27, 27))

    The factor is nearly dense: factoring the free-joint rows first fills in most zeros between limbs (at qpos0, 337 of the 378 upper-triangle slots have |U_{ij}| > 10^{-6} and 359 are nonzero, where M stores 243 entries; Example 11.12 prints these counts), which is why a dense container costs little here: the factor would be nearly dense anyway. (The 64-DOF cut-off itself, M_BLOCK_DENSE_MAX, is the benchmarked crossover described in Path 4.) solve_m later loads the same 729 numbers back as a tile and back-substitutes. In fwd_acceleration(factorize=True) the fused _tile_cholesky_factorize_solve_block does gather, factor and solve in one launch, without writing and re-reading the factor in between.

    Path 4: sparse LDL with a level schedule (_factor_i_sparse)

    Beyond 64 DOFs (M_BLOCK_DENSE_MAX) MJWarp stops using dense tiles. The reason is a measured crossover, not a hard limit: the comment on M_BLOCK_DENSE_MAX in types.py says dense tile Cholesky beats sparse LDL up to about 64 DOFs and degrades past about 80 (likewise M_BLOCK_SCALAR_MAX = 6 is where scalar Cholesky stops beating the tile kernel). Past it MJWarp falls back to MuJoCo's own sparse L^\top D L factorization, which keeps the tree's sparsity (no fill-in outside ancestor columns). The C version is a serial loop over rows from the leaves up. MJWarp parallelizes it with a schedule built in put_model: every elimination step "row k updates ancestor row i" becomes a triple (i, k, Madr_ki), grouped by the depth of i. Updates into rows of the same depth never read each other's output, so one launch per level, deepest first, is enough:

    # smooth.py::_factor_i_sparse (4.0.1), simplified
wp.copy(L, M)
for i in reversed(range(len(m.qLD_updates))):           # deepest level first
    wp.launch(_qLD_acc(...), dim=(d.nworld, m.qLD_updates[i].size), ...)
wp.launch(_qLDiag_div, dim=(d.nworld, m.nv), ...)       # qLDiagInv = 1 / diag

    Version note

    The loop above and the stepper below are the 4.0.1 wheel. In the dev tree (commit 8f5b9f1) _factor_i_sparse makes a single launch of _factor_LD_sparse_fused with dim=(nworld, number of sparse trees, block_dim), where block_dim = m.block_dim.factor_LD_sparse_fused (BlockDim default 64; put_model sets it from the trees' mean level width, 64 to 512; 1 on CPU). Each block factors one tree: it runs that tree's levels deepest first inside the kernel, separated by a block-wide sync (WP_TILE_SYNC), and gathers descendant contributions instead of scattering them with atomics. The schedule comes from m.qLD_fused_level_adr, m.qLD_fused_pair_adr and m.qLD_fused_pairs. _qLD_acc, _qLD_scale and _qLDiag_div exist only in the 4.0.1 wheel; m.qLD_updates is still used by the sparse solve (_solve_LD_sparse).

    One launch per level, many rows in parallel (4.0.1 wheel)

    smooth.py::_qLD_acc (wheel only)Illustrative 5-DOF tree

    Quiz

    What does d.qfrc_bias, the term c(q,\dot q), contain?

    smooth.rne computes it with qacc = 0 (gravity enters through the base acceleration). It is subtracted in qfrc_smooth.

    d.cvel[w, b] is a wp.spatial_vector. What are its last three numbers?

    All c-quantities share the com frame of their tree, so twists add without shifting. For any point p: v_p = v + w x (p - c).

    Why does kinematics launch one thread per (world, branch) instead of one launch per tree level?

    The humanoid needs 6 level launches (5 barriers between them) with 1-4 bodies each, or 1 branch launch that recomputes the torso 5 times.

    A free base carries one 4-link hinge chain and one single-link stub. What is m.nbranch?

    One branch per leaf body (io.py::put_model): the chain tip and the stub. The branches visit 5 and 2 bodies, so body_branches has 7 entries.

    For which pair of humanoid DOFs is M_{ij} = 0?

    Neither is an ancestor of the other, so moving one moves no body the other moves. Figure 9 shows these zero blocks.

    After changing d.qpos you call fwd_position(m, d, factorize=False), fwd_velocity, fwd_actuation and fwd_acceleration(m, d). What happens?

    Example 11.13 measures an error of about 69 rad/s² on the pendulum. Use exactly one factorization per new qpos.

    Which factorization path does the HUMANOID's single 27-DOF block take?

    7 to 64 DOFs go to the tile path: M_tiles = [(27, [0], 729)], qLD_block_total = 729 (Example 11.12).

    What does io.py::is_sparse (true for nv > 32 with the auto setting) change in the 4.0.1 wheel?

    Dense (nworld, njmax_pad, nv_pad) vs CSR (nworld, 1, njmax_nnz); the is_sparse docstring in types.py says it does not affect M.

    Body b has body_subtreenum[b] = 8. Which bodies does _crb_subtree sum for crb[b]?

    Depth-first numbering makes every subtree the contiguous range [b, b + body_subtreenum[b]).

def my_rne(q, qd, xanchor, xipos):
    """c(q, qdot): RNE with qacc = 0 and the gravity trick (base accelerates up by g)."""
    e = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])                     # both hinge axes (world y)
    g = -mjm.opt.gravity[2]
    mass = mjm.body_mass[1:3]
    p1, p2 = xanchor[0], xanchor[1]
    c1, c2 = xipos[1], xipos[2]
    # forward sweep (root -> leaf): angular velocity and linear acceleration
    w1 = e * qd[0]
    w2 = w1 + e * qd[1]
    a_p1 = np.array([0.0, 0.0, g])                    # gravity trick
    a_c1 = a_p1 + np.cross(w1, np.cross(w1, c1 - p1))
    a_p2 = a_p1 + np.cross(w1, np.cross(w1, p2 - p1))
    a_c2 = a_p2 + np.cross(w2, np.cross(w2, c2 - p2))
    # backward sweep (leaf -> root): force and moment carried to each joint
    F2 = mass[1] * a_c2                               # alpha = 0 and w x I w = 0 (planar)
    F1 = mass[0] * a_c1
    tau2 = e @ np.cross(c2 - p2, F2)
    tau1 = e @ (np.cross(c1 - p1, F1) + np.cross(c2 - p1, F2))
    return np.array([tau1, tau2])

    13Summary

    Key takeaways

    • MJWarp solves M(q)\ddot q + c(q,\dot q) = \tau + J^\top f per world: d.M, d.qfrc_bias, qfrc_passive + qfrc_actuator + qfrc_applied, d.qfrc_constraint. \tfrac12\dot q^\top M\dot q is the kinetic energy.
    • Spatial vectors are (rotation, translation) for both twists and wrenches, in the com frame of each tree (subtree_com[body_rootid]). xfrc_applied is the exception: (force, torque) at xipos.
    • kinematics runs one thread per (world, branch) walking root to leaf; shared bodies are recomputed instead of synchronized. com_vel and RNE reuse the branches.
    • Depth-first numbering makes every subtree the range [b, b + body_subtreenum[b]), so subtree com, crb and cfrc_int are one launch each.
    • cdof rows are the Jacobian columns in the com frame; mjw.jac shifts them to a point.
    • d.M is the CSR lower triangle (ancestors only, diagonal last, armature added). Read it with M_rowadr / M_rownnz / M_colind; apply it with mul_m.
    • factor_m writes qLD / qLDiagInv; solve_m only reads them. One factorization per new qpos, or qacc_smooth is silently wrong.
    • Each block of M goes compact, scalar Cholesky (≤ 6), tile Cholesky (≤ 64) or sparse LDL with a level schedule.

    Next, Ch 12 fills in the right-hand side: passive forces, the RNE that produces qfrc_bias (its three phases reuse this chapter's branches and subtrees), actuators and tendons. Ch 13 reuses factor_solve_i for the implicit integrators.

    Stuck? See Troubleshooting or the course map. The CSR and Cholesky background is collected in the math toolkit.
