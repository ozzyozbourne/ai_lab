    Part IV · Chapter 18

    Collision IV: Heightfields, SDFs and User SDF Plugins

    Terrain and curved, non-convex shapes do not fit the closed-form colliders of Ch 16 or the convex GJK/EPA path of Ch 17 directly. MJWarp handles them with two special narrowphases: a heightfield is cut into small triangular prisms that GJK can handle, and a signed distance field is searched with many parallel gradient descents. This chapter takes both apart, shows how to plug your own SDF into the Warp kernels, and ends Part IV with a checkpoint project.

    Expert About 100 min· Prerequisites: Ch 17, Ch 9, Ch 4

    You will learn

    • How ccd_hfield_kernel selects a sub-grid, builds two prisms per cell, runs GJK/EPA on each and keeps at most 4 contacts.
    • How the SDF narrowphase places Halton starting points and runs a two-phase gradient descent, and what sdf_initpoints and sdf_iterations change.
    • How to use MuJoCo SDF plugins in MJWarp (register_sdf_plugins) and write your own analytic SDF as a @wp.func pair.
    • The process-level rule: patch user_sdf before the first collision kernel compiles.
    • How to build terrain with MjSpec, size buffers for it, and debug HFIELD overflow.
    • How to profile all rigid collision paths of one scene and fix its budgets (Part IV checkpoint).

    Before you start: recap

    • All worlds share one contact pool of naconmax slots; nconmax is per world, and a full pool silently drops contacts and sets bits in d.overflow (Ch 9 §2, §7).
    • The broadphase writes candidate pairs; MJ_COLLISION_TABLE sends each geom type pair to the PRIMITIVE or CONVEX path, and geom[0] is always the lower type (Ch 16 §1, §2).
    • GJK finds the distance between two convex shapes from their support functions; EPA finds the penetration depth; every CONVEX candidate needs a slot of CCD scratch, sized by naccdmax (Ch 17).
    • wp.static(...) is evaluated when Warp builds a module, and a module is built once per process and then reused (Ch 4 §7, §8).
    • mujoco.MjSpec builds a model from Python objects instead of an XML string (Ch 2 §11).

    1A heightfield is a grid of heights

    A heightfield (type="hfield") describes terrain as an nrow × ncol grid of elevation samples. The samples are numbers in [0, 1]; MuJoCo scales them by size[2]. The four size values are

    sizemeaningTERRAIN (M7)
    size[0], size[1]half-extent in x and y: the grid spans [-size[0], size[0]] × [-size[1], size[1]]1, 1
    size[2]height of an elevation sample of 1.00.2
    size[3]depth of the base below z = 0 (the solid "slab" under the terrain)0.1

    Row r runs along y and column c along x, so sample (r, c) sits at x = c\,\Delta x - \text{size}_0,\; y = r\,\Delta y - \text{size}_1 with \Delta x = 2\,\text{size}_0/(\text{ncol}-1). Hover the parts of the figure.

    The shared model M7 TERRAIN is a 5 × 5 field of bumps with four free bodies on it. Every geom type that can touch a heightfield (sphere, capsule, ellipsoid, cylinder, box, mesh) is routed to the CONVEX path in MJ_COLLISION_TABLE: there is no closed-form collider for terrain, not even for a sphere. Plane-hfield and hfield-hfield have no table entry, so those pairs never collide.

    Ten contacts in two worlds, all from the HFIELD kernel; the tetrahedron mesh rests on an edge and gets two. MJ_COLLISION_TABLE is imported only to name the path (private API: the name may change).

    2From grid to prisms: inside ccd_hfield_kernel

    convex_narrowphase launches one heightfield kernel per (HFIELD, other type) pair that exists in the model, built by ccd_hfield_kernel_builder and cached. The launch shape is the flat pool-slot strip (layout 2 of the Ch 7 atlas): dim = d.naconmax, one thread per broadphase candidate, and threads whose candidate is not an (HFIELD, that type) pair return at once. One thread then does all of this for its pair:

    1. _hfield_filter: move the other geom into the heightfield frame and run a cheap box-sphere test with its bounding radius rbound against the field's box (size[0..1] horizontally, size[2] up, -size[3] down). If that passes, compute a tight axis-aligned box from six support-function queries (±x, ±y, ±z, the same support as GJK) and test it again.
    2. Reserve CCD scratch: ccdid = atomic_add(nccd, geomgeomid, 1). geomgeomid is the index of this kernel's geom-type pair (here HFIELD and the other type) in the upper triangle of the type-by-type table (upper_trid_index), fixed when the kernel is built, so each type pair has its own counter in nccd. Past naccdmax the pair is dropped and OverflowType.CCD is set, exactly like the convex kernels of Ch 17.
    3. Sub-grid: turn the tight box into cell indices c_{\min} = \lfloor (x_{\min} + s_0)\,\tfrac{\text{ncol}-1}{2 s_0} \rfloor,\; c_{\max} = \lceil (x_{\max} + s_0)\,\tfrac{\text{ncol}-1}{2 s_0} \rceil (and the same for rows), clamped to the grid.
    4. Prisms: walk rows r in [rmin, rmax) and columns c in (cmin, cmax]. A 6-row matrix prism slides along the row: each new grid vertex pushes out the oldest one, so each step yields the next triangle (bottom vertices at -size[3], top vertices at the elevation plus the margin). Two triangles per cell.
    5. Height test: skip a prism whose three top vertices are all below the other geom's lowest point zmin.
    6. GJK/EPA per prism: ccd(...) against the other geom. A prism in contact (ncontact > 0 and dist < margin + gap) is stored in a local buffer of MJ_MAXCONPAIR = 50 candidates; the deepest is remembered.
    7. Select at most 4 and call write_contact for each (§3).

    Why prisms?

    A heightfield as a whole is not convex, so GJK cannot run on it. Each prism is convex (6 vertices, passed as geom1.polyvert), so the same GJK/EPA code from Ch 17 works unchanged. The price: one CCD call per nearby prism instead of one per pair.

    The sub-grid formula is easy to explore by hand. In the widget below, drag the ball (or focus the canvas and use the arrow keys), and change the grid resolution and the ball radius. The highlighted block is [rmin, rmax) × (cmin, cmax] computed with the kernel's own formulas; the darker triangles are the prisms whose top triangle overlaps the ball's contact footprint on flat ground (a circle of radius \sqrt{2Rh - h^2} for a ball of radius R sunk h deep). Those are the prisms that fill the 50-slot candidate buffer.

    Going deeper: the margin, the base and the zmin test

    The top vertices of every prism get + margin (the pair's combined margin) added to their height, and the other geom's own margin is set to the same value before CCD (geom2.margin = margin, "geom1 margin added to z-axis of hfield prism top points"). Bottom vertices sit at -size[3], so a geom that has sunk below the surface still overlaps a prism and is pushed back up. The zmin height test uses the lowest support point of the other geom in the field frame; it skips prisms that are entirely under the geom, which is the common case on gentle terrain. Only the six support queries in _hfield_filter know the geom's shape; everything after that is generic CCD.

    3Four contacts, not six: the selection rule and MuJoCo C

    After the prism loop the thread holds up to 50 candidate contacts (position, normal, distance). It writes at most four of them. The rule is greedy and geometric: it tries to spread the contacts over the footprint, so that the constraint solver gets a stable support polygon from very few rows. With p_0 the deepest candidate and n its normal:

    contactpicked asscore maximised over the remaining candidates p
    0minimum distance (deepest)-\text{dist}(p)
    1farthest from contact 0\lVert p - p_0 \rVert
    2farthest from the line p_0 p_1\lvert (p - p_0)\cdot (n \times (p_0 - p_1)) \rvert
    3farthest from the triangle edges\lvert (p - p_0)\cdot(n\times(p_0-p_2))\rvert + \lvert (p_1 - p)\cdot(n\times(p_1-p_2))\rvert

    Each step stops the selection early when it finds nothing (id == -1) or when its best score is in (0, 1e-3), the constant MIN_DIST_TO_NEXT_CONTACT = 1.0e-3. For contact 1 the score is a distance, so contact 1 is skipped if it lies within 1 mm of contact 0. For contacts 2 and 3 the same 1e-3 is compared with an unnormalised score, not a distance (see the deep-dive below). Step through frames 6 to 9 of the stepper above (contacts 0 to 3) to watch the rule pick its points.

    Two details you can only see in the source

    The scores of contacts 2 and 3 are not normalised. n × (p0 - p1) has the length of p0 - p1, so the 1 mm cut-off is really "1e-3 divided by the length of that edge". With candidates 3 cm apart, a point must be about 3.3 cm off the line to be kept. Exact duplicates are kept. The cut-off test is 0.0 < d and d < 1e-3; a score of exactly 0 (several prisms reporting the very same point) passes, so the same position can be written more than once. Example 18.3 hits both cases.

    MuJoCo C runs the same prism decomposition (a comment in ccd_hfield_kernel says "see MuJoCo mjc_ConvexHField") but keeps its own set of contacts, often more of them. Example 18.3 puts one sphere on a flat 11 × 11 field in two poses and prints both engines' contacts, then lets both settle.

    • Pose A (on a vertex): six prisms meet at the vertex and all report the same point. C keeps all 6; MJWarp writes 4 identical contacts (exact duplicates survive, see above).
    • Pose B (off the vertex): C keeps 4 different points. MJWarp writes 2. Contact 2 needs a score of at least 1e-3, but a candidate 2 cm off the line through the first two, multiplied by the 3 cm length of that edge, scores only 6e-4, so selection stops after two contacts.
    • Same physics. After 1 s the ball rests at z = 0.09969 (C) and 0.09963 (MJWarp). The contact sets differ, the support is the same.

    Pitfall: tests that compare contact counts

    Contact counts differ from MuJoCo C on heightfields (4 vs up to 6 or more here, and SDF pairs differ too, §7). Tests and sizing scripts should compare physical outcomes (rest heights, forces, trajectories within a tolerance), and size buffers from MJWarp's own counts, not from mjd.ncon.

    4HFIELD overflow: when the grid is too fine

    The candidate buffer has MJ_MAXCONPAIR entries (mujoco.mjMAXCONPAIR = 50, re-exported in types.py). The check if count >= MJ_MAXCONPAIR runs before each prism, so overflow means "50 touching prisms collected and at least one more prism still to visit". The kernel then stops the loop, prints one warning per pair and sets OverflowType.HFIELD for that world with atomic_or. It still writes its 4 contacts, chosen from the 50 it collected, and because rows are visited from rmin upward, those 50 come from the low-y part of the footprint.

    Fixes, from best to worst

    • Coarser grid where large geoms land: the number of touching prisms grows roughly with (footprint / cell size)2. Only roughly: the footprint's AABB is rounded down and up to whole cells, so the alignment counts too, and a coarser grid can overflow where a finer one does not (in Exercise 18.1, n = 15 overflows and n = 16 does not). Check the grid you use.
    • Smaller colliding geoms (a robot foot, not a big box), or a capsule/sphere instead of a flat face, which touches fewer prisms.
    • Silence only the print with m.opt.warn_overflow &= ~mjw.OverflowType.HFIELD (the bit is still set, and the change recompiles the kernel because the flag is read with wp.static).

    5Building terrain with MjSpec, and sizing buffers for it

    Real terrains come from noise, scans or procedural generators, not from hand-typed XML. spec.add_hfield(name=, nrow=, ncol=, size=) creates the asset and hf.userdata takes the flattened, row-major elevation list (row = y). Example 18.2 builds 32 × 32 smoothed noise and drops 16 balls in each world from per-world random positions.

    Look at the first two lines of the output. When you do not pass sizes, make_data uses io.py::_default_nconmax and _default_njmax, which have a term that only switches on with a heightfield:

    For 16 balls that is generous: the example keeps running maxima, and the run peaks at 156 rows per world and 249 contacts in the whole pool of 8 worlds (during the landing); at the end, with the balls at rest, it needs 136 rows and 243 contacts. The heuristic scales with nv, not with what touches the terrain, so for big robots on terrain it can be far too large (memory, and the solver loops over njmax), and for many small objects it can be too small. Use the Ch 9 recipe: run a representative rollout, record the peak nacon per world and nefc, add headroom, pass them explicitly.

    Never combine a heightfield with a flex

    put_model raises NotImplementedError("Flex-HField collision is not implemented.") for any model with a flex and an hfield geom, even if they could never touch (the same holds for SDF geoms: "Flex-SDF collision is not implemented."). Soft bodies on terrain are not possible in 4.0.1 (Ch 23).

    6Signed distance fields

    A signed distance field (SDF) describes a shape by a function \phi(p): the distance from point p to the surface, negative inside, zero on the surface, positive outside. Its gradient \nabla\phi points away from the surface and has length 1 (for an exact SDF). Two facts make SDFs attractive for collision: any shape works, convex or not (a nut, a gear, a cow), and the depth of a point inside a shape is just -\phi(p).

    In MuJoCo an SDF geom has type="sdf" and gets its shape either from a plugin (C code that evaluates φ, for example mujoco.sdf.nut) or from a mesh, which the compiler turns into an octree of SDF samples. The dispatcher collision_sdf.py::sdf(type, p, attr, sdf_type, volume_data, mesh_data) evaluates the right function for each side of a pair: the analytic primitives above for geom1, and for an SDF geom either sample_volume_sdf (octree, sdf_type == -1) or user_sdf (plugin, its type id in sdf_type). A HFIELD on the other side is not handled there (it would print "ERROR: SDF type not implemented").

    SDF pairs are not in MJ_COLLISION_TABLE. _narrowphase runs the CONVEX and PRIMITIVE kernels and then, only when m.has_sdf_geom, calls sdf_narrowphase(m, d, ctx). Because SDF has the highest value among the rigid geom types (8; FLEX and TRIANGLE are internal ids that never reach this kernel), an SDF geom is always geom[1] of its pair, and the kernel simply returns when geom_type[g2] != GeomType.SDF.

    7The SDF narrowphase: Halton starts and gradient descent

    Two SDFs give no support function, so GJK is out. Instead MJWarp looks for a point that is inside both shapes, by local optimisation, from many starting points at once. The kernel is _sdf_narrowphase, launched by sdf_narrowphase with dim=(m.opt.sdf_initpoints, d.naconmax): thread (i, contact_tid) is starting point i of candidate contact_tid. Each thread:

    1. Builds both geoms' local AABBs (geom_aabb), moves geom2's into geom1's frame (transform_aabb: 8 corners, min/max) and intersects them. An empty intersection returns ("a broadphase candidate gap can admit pairs with no geometric intersection").
    2. Places its start inside the intersection box with a Halton sequence: x = min + (max - min) * (halton(i, 2), halton(i, 3), halton(i, 5)).
    3. Runs gradient_descent in geom2's frame (two phases, below).
    4. Turns the result into a contact: normal n = \text{normalize}(\hat\nabla A - \hat\nabla B) (both gradients normalised, rotated to the world), position x - n\,\text{dist}/2, and calls write_contact. There is no de-duplication: every start that ends within the margin writes its own contact.

    Why Halton and not random?

    util_misc.py::halton(index, base) writes the index in base b and mirrors its digits behind the decimal point (1 → 1/2, 2 → 1/4, 3 → 3/4 in base 2). Successive points land in the largest gaps left by the earlier ones, so even 10 or 40 points cover the box evenly, and the same index always gives the same point (deterministic, no random state per thread). Press play and compare the empty cells.

    Two-phase gradient descent

    Let A(x) and B(x) be the two SDF values at x. gradient_descent runs gradient_step twice:

    • Phase 0, sdf_iterations steps on f(x) = A + B + \lvert\max(A,B)\rvert (clearance(..., sfd_intersection=False)). Piece by piece: outside both, f = A + B + max(A, B), which pulls the point toward both surfaces; inside one, f = (inside value) + 2 × (outside value), which pulls it hard into the other shape; inside both, f = min(A, B).
    • Phase 1, one step on \max(A,B) (sfd_intersection=True): the "intersection" objective, lowest at the deepest point of the overlap. Its value at the end is the reported dist.

    A consequence worth knowing: phase 0 does not aim at the deepest point of the overlap. Once a point is inside both shapes, minimising \min(A, B) pushes it toward the deepest point inside one shape (here, the middle of whichever shape it sits deeper in), not toward the middle of the overlap where \max(A, B) is lowest. Phase 0 is good at gathering starts into the overlap, which is what produces more contacts; only the single phase-1 step moves a point toward the \max(A, B) optimum. So more sdf_iterations can make the reported dist worse, as the widget and Example 18.7 below both show.

    Each step is a backtracking (Armijo) line search: start from alpha = linesearch_alpha0 (the launch passes 2.0, "passed at launch so NVRTC 13.1+ cannot unroll the line search"), halve it (rho = 0.5) until f(x - \alpha g) - f(x) \le -c\,\alpha\,\lVert g\rVert^2 with c = 0.1, or alpha ≤ 1e-4. A step that makes f worse is rejected and the descent stops early; so does a gradient with \lVert g\rVert^2 < 10^{-12}.

    One starting point, step by step

    collision_sdf.py::gradient_descentIllustrative
    x = halton start in AABB intersection

  niter = sdf_iterations if phase == 0 else 1

    What dist means on the SDF path

    The reported distance is \max(A, B) at the final point, not the penetration depth. For a flat overlap of depth h the best possible value is -h/2 (the point halfway between the two surfaces), and the descent does not always reach it. Primitive and convex colliders report the full depth. Example 18.7 measures it.

    Example 18.7 sweeps both options on one real SDF contact: a cube mesh with type="sdf" (no plugin, so no registration is needed) sitting 1 mm deep on a plane. Both options are plain kernel inputs, so changing m.opt.sdf_initpoints or m.opt.sdf_iterations between calls does not recompile.

    • Contacts = starts once the descent converges: 1, 5, 10, 40, 100. They all go into the shared pool, which is why _default_nconmax jumps to at least 64 (and njmax to 256) for any model with an SDF geom. Budget sdf_initpoints contacts per SDF pair.
    • Coverage grows with the starts: 1 start gives one point, 40 cover the whole face (the rotated square spans about 0.25 m along x).
    • Iterations change how many starts reach the overlap (27 with 0 or 1 iteration, 33 with 3, 40 with 10) and how deep they report, but not monotonically: the mean dist is -0.00042 with 0 iterations, -0.00025 with 1, -0.00042 with 3 and -0.00048 with 10 (the best possible is -0.00050). Phase 0 minimises \min(A, B) inside both shapes, so extra iterations gather starts rather than deepen them; here 10 iterations happen to land close to the optimum, in the widget above they do not.
    • MuJoCo C collides plane-SDF with a different collider here (4 contacts at the full 1 mm); for box-SDF or sphere-SDF pairs the two engines use the same method and agree closely.

    Version note: dev tree vs the 4.0.1 wheel

    The cloned source (commit 8f5b9f1) adds _sdf_candidates and prepare_sdf_context: a first kernel compacts the candidates whose geom[1] is an SDF into ctx.sdf_collisionid, and the main kernel then launches dim=(d.naconmax, m.opt.sdf_initpoints) so that "a candidate's initial points are adjacent threads, so a warp works on one geom pair". It also reads geom_xquat instead of geom_xmat. Dev tree only, not in 4.0.1: in the wheel every one of the sdf_initpoints × naconmax threads checks its candidate's type itself. Results are the same.

    8User SDF plugins: from MJCF to a Warp function

    MuJoCo's SDF plugins (mujoco.sdf.nut, bolt, gear, torus, ...) are C code. A Warp kernel cannot call them. So MJWarp keeps only the data and calls a user SDF hook:

    1. MJCF: <plugin plugin="mujoco.sdf.nut"><instance name="nut"><config key="radius" value="0.26"/>, a mesh generated from it, and <geom type="sdf" mesh="nut"><plugin instance="nut"/>.
    2. MjModel: mjm.geom_plugin[g] is the plugin instance of geom g, mjm.plugin[instance] its plugin type id (an index into MuJoCo's plugin registry), and the config values are stored as text in plugin_attr.
    3. put_model keeps only geom plugins (body, actuator and sensor plugins raise NotImplementedError): m.geom_plugin_index[g] points into m.plugin (type ids) and m.plugin_attr, where each config string has been parsed to a float and padded to _NPLUGINATTR = 128 slots (a vec_pluginattr).
    4. Kernel: get_sdf_params returns attr = plugin_attr[id] and sdf_type = plugin[id]; sdf() calls user_sdf(p, attr, sdf_type).
    5. user_sdf / user_sdf_grad in collision_sdf.py are placeholders: they wp.printf("ERROR: user_sdf function must be implemented by user code") and return 0 (and a zero gradient). Until you replace them, every SDF plugin pair finds nothing.

    Example 18.5 loads the shipped test_data/collision_sdf/nutbolt.xml without registration. Its work runs in a child process so that the device printf lines (on stdout) can be counted instead of flooding the page.

    C finds 6 contacts; MJWarp finds 0 and prints 400 error lines in one forward (160 from user_sdf, 240 from user_sdf_grad: a few calls per starting point before the zero gradient stops the descent). Nothing raises: the only signals are the printf flood and nacon.

    Example 18.6 does it right: it calls register_sdf_plugins(mjw) (from mujoco_warp.test_data.collision_sdf.utils, a shipped test helper; private API) at the top of a fresh process. The helper compiles a tiny model with all four plugins to learn their type ids in this MuJoCo build, defines user_sdf / user_sdf_grad as an if-chain over wp.static(id) constants calling nut, bolt, torus and gear (Warp ports in test_data/collision_sdf/*.py), and assigns them to mujoco_warp._src.collision_sdf.

    With registration: 4 nut-bolt contacts (C: 6), all a fraction of a millimetre deep. The parsed attributes are the config values (0.26 and 0.255 m radius) in slot 0 of 128. The --late child shows the trap of the next section: registering after one unregistered forward leaves nacon at 0 even with a fresh put_model, and both forwards print their 400 error lines (800 in total).

    9The fresh-process rule

    Why does a late patch do nothing? sdf() refers to user_sdf by name. Warp generates the code of a module's kernels when one of them first launches, and at that moment it resolves the name in the module's globals. The compiled module then stays loaded for the rest of the process; rebinding collision_sdf.user_sdf later does not make Warp rebuild it, and a new put_model or make_data launches the same compiled kernel. (The on-disk kernel cache is not the problem: a fresh process with the patch builds a different module, because the function source is part of the module hash.)

    Pitfall (verified): register at the top of a fresh process

    • Patch user_sdf / user_sdf_grad (or call register_sdf_plugins(mjw)) before the first mjw.put_model, forward, step or collision in the process. Later has no effect and gives silent nacon = 0.
    • In a notebook, run such scripts with %%writefile + !python file.py, or put the patch in the first cell after a Runtime > Restart session.
    • mjwarp-viewer and the other CLI tools load models through cli.py::load_model, which calls register_sdf_plugins automatically when an XML model's spec.plugins has a mujoco.sdf.* plugin (not for .mjb files).
    • No SDF or heightfield geom in a model with a flex (put_model raises).

    10Writing your own analytic SDF

    A user SDF is two @wp.funcs with the placeholders' exact signatures, user_sdf(p: wp.vec3, attr: vec_pluginattr, sdf_type: int) -> float and user_sdf_grad(...) -> wp.vec3, where p is in the SDF geom's local frame. The plan:

    1. Pick a carrier plugin. MuJoCo still needs a real plugin instance to compile the model: it builds the visual mesh and, importantly, the geom's bounding box (geom_aabb) from it, and MJWarp starts the descent inside that box. Choose a plugin whose attribute layout and bounding box fit your shape.
    2. Learn its type id by compiling a tiny model with it (int(m.plugin[0])), as register_sdf_plugins does.
    3. Write value and gradient, and a dispatcher that compares sdf_type with wp.static(TYPE_ID), so the branch is a compile-time constant.
    4. Patch both names on mujoco_warp._src.collision_sdf at the top of a fresh process.

    Example 18.8 builds a rounded tile: a flat square of half-size R (core box (R, R, 0)) inflated by a radius r. Its bounding box (R + r, R + r, r) is exactly the bounding box of a torus with radius1 = R, radius2 = r, so it reuses the mujoco.sdf.torus layout: attr[0] = R, attr[1] = r. MuJoCo C (and the viewer mesh) still see a torus; MJWarp sees the tile. A ball dropped on the centre proves which shape is used: on a torus it falls through the hole.

    • The ball rests at z = 0.0799 (r + radius = 0.08): MJWarp collides with the tile. C's ball falls through the torus hole to the floor at z = -0.25.
    • 17 contacts for one ball resting on a flat face: one per starting point that ends inside (§7).
    • The normal points down (-z): frame[0] points from geom[0] (the sphere, type 2) to geom[1] (the SDF, type 8), as for every pair (Ch 16 §2).

    Gradient rules for your own SDF

    • Return a gradient even where the formula is undefined (the tile returns (0, 0, 1) on its core square). A zero gradient makes gradient_step stop at once (dot(g, g) < 1e-12).
    • The gradient is normalised before it becomes a normal, but the descent uses it unnormalised: keep its length near 1 (an exact SDF) or the line search starts with the wrong step size.
    • Remember the bounding box: a shape that pokes out of the carrier plugin's geom_aabb is never sampled there.

    Play with the shape before you write it. The builder evaluates a 2-D rounded box \phi(p) = \lVert\max(q, 0)\rVert + \min(\max(q_x, q_y), 0) - r, q = \lvert p\rvert - b, the same formula as rounded_tile in the x-y plane. Set by = 0 for a capsule-like slot, b = 0 for a disc.

    11Mesh and volume SDFs: sampled, not analytic

    A mesh used by an SDF geom (<geom type="sdf" mesh="..."/> without a plugin, as in Example 18.7 and the shipped cow.xml) is compiled by MuJoCo into an octree: each node has an AABB (oct_aabb: centre, half-size), 8 children (oct_child, -1 for none) and 8 SDF values at its corners (oct_coeff). The 0.2 m cube of Example 18.7 already has 63,497 nodes. MJWarp samples it with three functions:

    • box_project(center, half_size, p): a point outside the root box is moved onto the box (plus 1e-4) and the distance it moved is returned as dist0.
    • find_oct(oct_child, oct_aabb, p, grad, root): walk down from the root. At each node compute the cell coordinate u = (p - v_{\min})/(v_{\max} - v_{\min}) \in [0,1]^3, pick child 4z + 2y + x with each bit u ≥ 0.5, until all 8 children are -1 (a leaf, at most 100 levels). At the leaf return the 8 trilinear weights w_j = \prod_k (u_k \text{ or } 1-u_k), or their derivatives when grad=True.
    • sample_volume_sdf = dist0 + dot(weights, oct_coeff[leaf]); sample_volume_grad uses the derivative weights inside the box and finite differences (h = 1e-4, forward differences) outside it: four full sample_volume_sdf calls (the base value plus one per axis), each with its own box_project and tree walk.

    The figure is a 2-D analogue: a quadtree refined near the surface of a shape, bilinear interpolation from a leaf's 4 corners. Drag the query point; switch to the line plot to see where sampling loses accuracy.

    What this means for accuracy and cost

    • Sampled SDFs are only as good as the leaf size near the surface: corners and thin features are rounded off, and the error is largest where the true SDF bends inside a leaf.
    • Each sample is a tree walk with data-dependent depth (divergent loads on a GPU) plus 8 corner reads, so a sampled SDF is expected to cost more per query than a cheap analytic one (this chapter does not time sdf_narrowphase); gradients outside the box cost four full samples (forward differences: the base value plus one per axis) instead of one tree walk. An analytic SDF is a closed-form evaluation: a few flops for primitives, while plugin ports such as gear and bolt are longer, and their gradients use finite differences.
    • A MESH geom on the other side of an SDF pair is not sampled: the kernel marks its MeshData valid, so sdf() takes the ray_mesh branch (a ray from the point toward the mesh origin) for it.

    1. starts with deliberately small budgets (8 contacts, 32 rows and 1 CCD slot per world), steps 1 s, decodes d.overflow with describe_overflow (Ch 9), and grows whatever overflowed (doubling nconmax/njmax, quadrupling nccdmax) until the run is clean. It clamps nccdmax to nconmax, because make_data raises ValueError when nccdmax > nconmax, and raises nconmax too when CCD still overflows at that cap;
    2. wraps wp.launch for one mjw.collision call to list every kernel, naming the builder and type pair from warp_util._KERNEL_CACHE (as in Ch 16, Example 16.8), and counts contacts per path and type pair.

    What the run teaches:

    • The budget ladder. Try 0 overflows everything: contacts (BROADPHASE and NARROWPHASE: 91 wanted, 64 slots), rows (NEFC) and CCD scratch (CCD: 1 slot per world is not enough for the convex and HFIELD pairs that pass their filters). CCD needs two 4× bumps (1, 4, 16); contacts and rows need four doublings. The final budgets (128 contacts, 512 rows, 16 CCD slots per world) are 6× and about 16× below the io.py defaults (768 and 8351), which scale with nv = 204 because of the heightfield term.
    • Overflow bits are sticky. Try 3 ends with 364 of 512 slots used and still reports BROADPHASE: the bits are ORed into d.overflow and nothing clears them, so they record the worst moment of the run (the landing). Zero d.overflow yourself if you want per-step information.
    • Kernels per type pair. One collision call launches 1 broadphase kernel, 5 HFIELD kernels (one per type that can touch the terrain), 8 CONVEX kernels and 1 primitive kernel. The HFIELD and primitive kernels launch one thread per pool slot (dim = naconmax) and threads past ncollision return early. The CONVEX (CCD) kernels are grid-stride loops over the first ncollision candidates; their launch width (_ccd_grid_size in collision_convex.py) is naconmax only on CPU, where Warp has no occupancy data, and a few device waves (capped at naconmax) on GPU, so the dim [1024] above is a CPU value. The broadphase has its own launch shape ([8, 629] here: worlds × filtered geom pairs). The 8 convex kernels run every step, one per type pair that the broadphase can hand them, whether or not that pair touches now. Only 3 of the 8 pairs (capsule-mesh, sphere-ellipsoid, sphere-mesh) hold a contact at the measured step. Each type pair also has its own CCD counter (nccd is indexed by geomgeomid), so naccdmax bounds the candidates of one pair type.
    • Per path after 1 s (the 8 worlds are identical, so every count is 8× one world's): 232 HFIELD contacts (29 per world: scatter spheres and capsules on the terrain plus the four TERRAIN bodies), 120 PRIMITIVE contacts (sphere-capsule pairs in the pile, and bodies that reached the floor plane near or past the terrain's edge) and 32 CONVEX contacts (scatter bodies touching the tetra mesh and the ellipsoid). The 30 bodies are dropped as a 5 × 5 (+5) block, 0.25 m apart, over the middle of the heightfield, so they land on the terrain and on the TERRAIN bodies; sphere-sphere, sphere-capsule and capsule-capsule are PRIMITIVE pairs, so CONVEX contacts need a scatter body to touch the ellipsoid, cylinder or mesh. The script asserts that CONVEX and HFIELD-CAPSULE are both non-zero.

    15Summary

    Key takeaways

    • Heightfield pairs are CONVEX: _hfield_filter (box-sphere, then a tight support AABB) → sub-grid → two prisms per cell → GJK/EPA per prism → at most 50 candidates → at most 4 contacts (deepest, farthest, farthest from the line, farthest from the edges).
    • HFIELD overflow means the 50-slot candidate buffer filled while prisms were still left to visit; contacts then come from the low-y part of the footprint. Use coarser grids under big geoms. Compare physics with MuJoCo C, not contact counts.
    • The default sizes grow with nv when the model has a heightfield; measure and pass explicit nconmax/njmax/nccdmax.
    • SDF pairs: sdf_initpoints Halton starts in the AABB intersection, sdf_iterations Armijo steps on A + B + |\max(A,B)|, one step on \max(A,B); one contact per converged start; dist is \max(A,B), at best -h/2.
    • Plugins: put_model keeps type ids and parsed attributes; the kernel calls user_sdf/user_sdf_grad, which are placeholders until you patch them. register_sdf_plugins(mjw) patches nut, bolt, torus and gear.
    • Patch at the top of a fresh process, before the first MJWarp call; a late patch is silently ignored.
    • Mesh SDFs are octree samples (find_oct, trilinear weights, box_project outside): less exact than analytic SDFs and expected to cost more per query (a data-dependent tree walk plus 8 corner reads).

    Stuck? See Troubleshooting (SDF plugins registered too late, flex + hfield, overflow) or the course map.
