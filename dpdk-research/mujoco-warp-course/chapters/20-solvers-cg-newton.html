    The Primal Solvers: CG, Newton and the Exact Linesearch

    Chapter 19 turned every contact, limit and equality into a row of one convex cost. This chapter opens the code that minimizes it, thousands of times in parallel, every step: solver.solve. We follow it kernel by kernel, from the warmstart to the last linesearch, and measure what each knob does on real runs.

    Expert About 120 min· Prerequisites: Ch 19 (also uses Ch 7, Ch 9 and Ch 11)

    You will learn

    • How solve runs: warmstart, init_context, iterations, termination and the recovered qfrc_constraint.
    • How CG (M-preconditioned, Polak-Ribière with \beta \ge 0) and Newton (H = M + J^\top D J, three Cholesky paths, incremental updates) build each search direction.
    • How the exact linesearch minimizes a piecewise-quadratic ray cost with three probes per round.
    • How to set iterations, per-world tolerance and compile-time ls_iterations, and read solver_niter and the ITERATIONS / LS_ITERATIONS bits.
    • Why each world stops at its own iteration on a GPU (capture_while), and how to use warmstart, run_qacc_smooth and noslip.

    Before you start: recap

    • The constraint solve minimizes one convex cost over the accelerations qacc: a Gauss term plus one term per efc row. Gradient descent, Newton, CG and linesearch on a piecewise quadratic are in the math toolkit of Ch 19 §2.
    • Each efc row has a Jacobian row efc.J, a softness efc.D and a reference acceleration efc.aref, built by make_constraint (Ch 19); there are at most njmax rows per world (Ch 9 §5).
    • factor_m writes qLD; solving with an old qLD gives wrong qacc; Ch 11 §9. CG uses that factor every iteration.
    • 32 lanes run in lockstep; branches serialize; contiguous reads coalesce; Ch 4 §4. A tiled launch gives each logical index a block of threads that share memory; Ch 26 §8 (preview in Ch 4 §11).
    • A CUDA graph records launches once and replays them; conditional nodes loop on device; Ch 7 §10. The solve's place in the pipeline and its launch shapes are in Ch 7 §6 and §8.

    1The problem every iteration attacks

    Recall from Ch 19

    The math toolkit (Ch 19 §2): gradient descent steps along -\nabla f; Newton steps along -H^{-1}\nabla f; CG mixes the new steepest direction with the previous one; an exact linesearch on a piecewise-quadratic function walks its kinks. This chapter shows where each piece lives in solver.py.

    For one world, write a for qacc (length nv), a_0 for qacc_smooth (the acceleration without constraints), M for the mass matrix, and J_i, D_i, r_i for row i of efc.J, efc.D and efc.aref. The primal solver minimizes

    f(a) = \tfrac12 (a-a_0)^\top M (a-a_0) + \sum_i s_i\big(J_i a - r_i\big)

    The first term is Gauss's principle: do not move away from the free motion more than needed, measured in the metric of M. Each s_i is a small convex function of the row's "jaref" x_i = J_i a - r_i (the code calls it Jaref). Which formula a row uses depends on its ConstraintState, and that state changes as a moves. So f is convex and piecewise quadratic (with linear pieces for frictionloss and a cone piece for elliptic contacts). Hover the parts of Figure 1.

    2solve and _solve: the flow

    forward calls solver.solve(m, d) right after fwd_acceleration has written qfrc_smooth and qacc_smooth (and factorized M into qLD). solve itself is short. It picks one of three routes:

    • Sleep enabled (EnableBit.SLEEP, not DISCRETE): island.update_active_dofs, then solve_compact, a dense Newton over the awake dofs only. That route is Chapter 21's.
    • No rows possible (d.njmax == 0 or m.nv == 0): copy qacc_smooth into qacc, set solver_niter to 0, done.
    • Otherwise: create a SolverContext (the scratch arrays below), run _solve, then noslip if opt.noslip_iterations > 0 (§13).

    _solve is where the work happens. Figure 2 shows its steps; hover each box.

    The SolverContext holds one row per world of everything the iterations need: Jaref and jv (nworld, njmax), grad (nworld, nv_pad), search and mv (nworld, nv), the scalars alpha, improvement, grad_dot, newton_decrement, inertia_scale, the flag done, and for Newton the Hessian h (nworld, nv_pad, nv_pad). _create_solver_context allocates it fresh on every solve call; the CG-only arrays (Mgrad, prev_grad, beta) and the Newton-only ones (h, hfactor, the tile masks) are empty for the other solver.

    Note

    solve is public (mjw.solve), so you can rerun only the constraint solve after forward. It needs qacc_smooth, qfrc_smooth, the M factor and the efc rows from the same state. The examples below use a simpler trick to look inside: they rerun forward from one saved state with different opt.iterations.

    3The gradient and the constraint states

    Differentiate f. The Gauss term gives M(a - a_0) = Ma - q_{smooth}, because M a_0 = q_{smooth} (qfrc_smooth) by definition of qacc_smooth. Each row term gives s_i'(x_i)\,J_i^\top, and -s_i'(x_i) is exactly that row's force efc.force. Summing, \sum_i J_i^\top f_i = J^\top f = qfrc_constraint. So

    \nabla f(a) = Ma - q_{smooth} - J^\top f(a) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \texttt{grad = Ma - qfrc\_smooth - qfrc\_constraint}

    which is the comment at the top of _update_gradient. At the minimum the gradient is zero: Ma = q_{smooth} + q_{constraint}, Newton's second law with the constraint forces included. The solver never builds the gradient from scratch: it keeps Ma and Jaref up to date incrementally (the linesearch adds \alpha\,Mv and \alpha\,Jv), and _update_constraint_efc turns each row's Jaref into a force and a state:

    Row kindCondition on x = J_i a - r_iStateForce f_iCost
    equalityanyQUADRATIC-D x\tfrac12 D x^2
    frictionlossx \le -f/DLINEARNEG+flinear
    |x| < f/DQUADRATIC-D x\tfrac12 D x^2
    x \ge f/DLINEARPOS-flinear
    limit, pyramidal contactx \ge 0SATISFIED00
    x < 0QUADRATIC-D x\tfrac12 D x^2
    elliptic contactzone of (N, T) vs the coneSATISFIED / QUADRATIC / CONEsee _eval_elliptic_middle

    The second derivative is just as simple: a QUADRATIC row contributes D_i J_i^\top J_i to the Hessian, a SATISFIED or LINEAR row contributes nothing. That is why Newton's Hessian H = M + J^\top \operatorname{diag}(D \cdot \text{active}) J changes only when a row crosses into or out of QUADRATIC (§7).

When you compare two solvers, compare their costs first, not their qacc. Exercise 20.2 checks a smaller stack where the two agree to 1e-4.

    5CG: M-preconditioned, Polak-Ribière, \beta \ge 0

    Plain gradient descent on f would zigzag, because the Gauss term's bowl is stretched by M (a heavy base and a light finger differ by orders of magnitude). MJWarp's conjugate gradient (SolverType.CG) fixes both problems at once:

    1. Precondition with M. Instead of -\nabla f it uses -M^{-1}\nabla f (Mgrad). For an unconstrained world this is already the exact answer in one step. Mgrad costs one triangular solve with the factor qLD that fwd_acceleration computed (smooth.solve_m): M is a preconditioner that is already factorized.
    2. Conjugate directions. The new direction mixes in the old one: s_{k+1} = -M^{-1}g_{k+1} + \beta_k s_k, with the Polak-Ribière coefficient clamped at zero.
    \beta_k = \max\!\left(0,\; \frac{g_{k+1}^\top\,(M^{-1}g_{k+1} - M^{-1}g_k)}{\max(\epsilon,\; g_k^\top M^{-1} g_k)}\right)

    When \beta = 0 the method restarts from the preconditioned steepest direction. The clamp matters on this cost: every time a row changes state the quadratic model changes, and the old direction can stop being useful.

    Version note

    The 4.0.1 wheel computes the Polak-Ribière β above (the code comment says # polak-ribiere). The dev tree at 8f5b9f1 replaces it with a Hager-Zhang β clamped at zero plus a restart test (solver_test.py::test_solve_cg_finalize_hager_zhang_beta, dev tree only, not in 4.0.1). Research notes written from the dev tree say "Hager-Zhang"; on the wheel you are running Polak-Ribière.

    CG has a second shortcut. Right after _solve_init_search_cg_tiled sets s_0 = -M^{-1}g_0, it checks \tfrac12\,\text{scale}\,g_0^\top M^{-1} g_0 < \text{tol}. That quantity is the predicted improvement of a full preconditioned step; if it is already below tolerance, the world is done with solver_niter = 0. Newton has no such pre-check: it always runs at least one iteration.

    CG vs Newton in numbers

    Example 20.1 steps eight kicked box_stack(5) worlds for 50 steps with each of the four (solver, cone) pairs and records solver_niter after every step.

    Newton needs about ten times fewer iterations on this scene, and every world has its own count. A Newton iteration costs more (a Hessian and a Cholesky instead of one triangular solve); at nv 30 the Hessian fits one tile. Whether that makes a step cheaper on your GPU depends on nv; run Example 20.10 on Colab. Chapter 21's sleeping requires Newton for a separate reason: its compacted path, solve_compact, is a dense Newton solve over the awake dofs (§2). CG is the choice when memory is tight or nv is large: it stores no nv_pad x nv_pad matrix per world. Time both on your GPU before choosing.

    6Newton: H = M + J^\top D J and its Cholesky

    Newton's method minimizes the local quadratic model of f exactly. On a piecewise quadratic the model is exact as long as no row changes state, so a Newton step with \alpha = 1 jumps straight to the minimum of the current piece. The direction is

    s = -H^{-1} g, \qquad H = M + \sum_{i\,\in\,\text{QUADRATIC}} D_i\, J_i^\top J_i \;(+\text{cone terms for elliptic})

    H is SPD (M is SPD and each added term is positive semidefinite), so it has a Cholesky factorization H = U^\top U. MJWarp builds and factors one H per world per iteration (or updates it, §7), in three steps.

    Building H

    • Dense J (m.is_sparse False, the default for nv ≤ 32): _update_gradient_JTDAJ_dense_tiled(nv_pad, 16, njmax, nC), one block of 128 threads per world. It scatters M's upper triangle from its CSR storage into a shared nv_pad x nv_pad tile (identity on the padded dofs), then loops over the rows 16 at a time: load a 16 x nv_pad slab of J, zero the rows that are not QUADRATIC (_state_check), and add tile_matmul(Jᵀ D, J).
    • Sparse J (nv > 32 with jacobian="auto"): _update_gradient_init_h_sparse scatters M into H, then _JTDACJ_sparse visits "constraint groups" (consecutive rows with identical sparse support, efc.jtdaj_adr/nrow/nblock), 32 threads per group, adding only the nonzero blocks with atomics.
    • Elliptic cones add the curvature of the cone pieces: _update_gradient_JTCJ_dense (dense) or the elliptic branch of _JTDACJ_sparse.

    Three Cholesky paths, chosen by nv

    Pitfall: H memory grows with nv² per world

    _create_solver_context allocates h as (nworld, nv_pad, nv_pad) float32 on every solve, plus a second hfactor of the same size when nv > 48 and the incremental path is on. At nv 120 that is 64 KB per world per buffer: 128 KB x 4096 worlds = 512 MB of scratch. If memory runs out at large nv and nworld, CG (no H at all) is the fallback.

    Going deeper: why the one-tile path pads to 4, not 16

    For nv ≤ 48 the whole matrix is one tile in shared memory, and the factorization works on rows of whole 16-byte vectors (4 floats), so nv_pad only needs to be a multiple of 4: the humanoid (nv 27) gets 28, not 32. For larger nv the blocked factorization reads 16x16 tiles, and Newton reserves one extra "augmented" column, so _get_padded_sizes rounds nv + 1 up to 16 (60 → 64, 120 → 128). The padded dofs get 1 on the diagonal of H so the factorization stays well conditioned.

    7Incremental Hessian updates and the stable fast path

    Rebuilding H every iteration is wasteful: between two iterations only the rows that cross into or out of QUADRATIC change it, by exactly \pm D_i J_i^\top J_i. For Newton with pyramidal cones (_use_incremental(m): solver == NEWTON and cone != ELLIPTIC) MJWarp exploits this in three layers:

    1. Track flips. _update_constraint_efc(track_changes=True) compares each row's old and new state. A QUADRATIC flip appends the row to quad_changed_ids (atomic counter quad_changed_count); any state change bumps state_changed_count (that also catches LINEARNEG ↔ LINEARPOS friction flips, which change the force but not H).
    2. Rank-1 updates. _update_gradient_h_incremental (dense J) or _update_gradient_h_incremental_sparse adds +D_i J_i^\top J_i for each row that became QUADRATIC and subtracts it for each row that left, on the upper triangle only. Then the Cholesky runs again. On the blocked and sparse-tile paths (nv > 48, where hfactor is allocated) it runs with skip_unchanged=True: worlds without QUADRATIC flips reuse their cached factor in hfactor. On the one-tile path (nv ≤ 48) there is no hfactor: _update_gradient_cholesky skips only worlds with no state change at all (state_changed_count == 0) and refactors the rest from h, so a world with only a friction LINEARNEG ↔ LINEARPOS flip is refactored. Every box_stack(5) example in this chapter (nv 30) takes the one-tile path; only Example 20.11's larger stacks (box_stack(10), nv 60; box_stack(20), nv 120) reach the blocked and sparse-tile paths.
    3. Stable fast path. A world with no state change at all was exactly quadratic over the last step. Its new gradient is the old one scaled: after a step t along a Newton direction, g \to (\sigma - t)\,g, where \sigma is ctx.grad_scale, the factor that turns the stored, stale g into the true gradient (1 after a rebuild). So it skips the qfrc_constraint, gradient, Cholesky and search updates (_update_gradient_zero_grad_dot(stable_fast=True) rescales grad_dot and newton_decrement by the ratio squared), and the next linesearch reuses its mv and jv (ctx.search_unchanged). The true qfrc_constraint is recovered once at the end of _solve.

    How many rows actually flip? MJWarp does not expose its per-iteration states, so Example 20.12 reruns one cold-started Newton solve with opt.iterations = 0, 1, 2, ... from the same state and reads d.efc.state after each. DeterminismType.CONTACTS | CONSTRAINT keeps the row order identical between reruns (Chapter 22 explains these bits).

    Most of the work happens in the first iteration: 15 of 76 rows flip (pyramidal), then 5, then 1, then none. With the incremental path, iteration 2 costs five rank-1 updates instead of a 76-row rebuild, and iteration 4 takes the fast path. The elliptic run shows why that path is off for elliptic cones: rows move between QUADRATIC and CONE, the cone terms depend on Jaref, and H is rebuilt in full every iteration (the "quad flips" column there is just a count; nothing consumes it). Both solves stop at niter 4 even though the tolerance was 1e-12: once the states have settled, one Newton step lands on the minimum of the now exact quadratic piece, and a stopping test of §9 fires on quantities that are down at float32 rounding level.

    8The exact linesearch

    Both solvers hand the same job to _linesearch: given the current a and a direction s, find the step \alpha that minimizes the ray cost \varphi(\alpha) = f(a + \alpha s) - f(a). Along a ray every row's jaref is linear, x_i(\alpha) = x_i + \alpha\, (Js)_i, so \varphi is a convex piecewise quadratic with a kink wherever some x_i(\alpha) crosses a state boundary. Its slope \varphi' is piecewise linear and increasing; the minimizer is where the slope crosses zero. This is an exact linesearch: it does not settle for "good enough decrease" (as Armijo backtracking would), it finds the minimizer to a tolerance.

    Everything the kernel needs is a sum over rows of tiny per-row polynomials. At each probe α the kernel needs the triple (\varphi, \varphi', \varphi''):

    • The Gauss part is one quadratic per world, quad_gauss = (c_0, c_1, c_2) = (0,\ s^\top(Ma - q_{smooth}),\ \tfrac12 s^\top M s) holds polynomial coefficients, \varphi_G = c_0 + c_1\alpha + c_2\alpha^2, turned into (\varphi, \varphi', \varphi'') with \varphi'' = 2c_2 at each probe (p0 = (q[0], q[1], 2·q[2]) + rows); it is built from mv = M s (plus the equality rows, which are always quadratic).
    • Every other row adds _eval_pt_direct(jaref, jv, D, alpha) = (\tfrac12 D x^2,\ D\,jv\,x,\ D\,jv^2) when it is quadratic at that α, the frictionloss or cone formula otherwise, and nothing when satisfied.

    The search (_linesearch_iterative_kernel, one block of 32 threads per world striding over the rows) goes like this:

    1. p0. Evaluate at α = 0. The slope must be negative (a descent direction).
    2. Newton probe from 0. \alpha_{in} = -\varphi'(0)/\varphi''(0): the exact minimizer if no row changes state on the way. Accept it if |\varphi'| < gtol and the cost dropped.
    3. Bracket rounds. Otherwise keep two points lo and hi (initially 0 and the probe, ordered by slope) and, for up to opt.ls_iterations rounds, evaluate three candidates in one pass over the rows: a Newton step from lo, a Newton step from hi, and the midpoint (_eval_pt_direct_3alphas, then one block reduction of a packed mat33). Accept the lowest-cost candidate with a small slope; otherwise move lo and hi to candidates that stay on their side of the minimum (_in_bracket). Stop when nothing moves.
    4. Apply. qacc += α·search, Ma += α·mv, Jaref += α·jv, and store alpha and improvement (the cost drop) for the stopping tests.

    The kernel evaluates the three α values in one pass over the rows, branching on constraint type and loading efc_D and the contact data once per row (the solver.py header comment, item 5), instead of three passes; three probes also narrow the bracket more per round.

    The acceptance tolerance on the slope is

    \texttt{gtol} = \texttt{tolerance}\cdot\texttt{ls\_tolerance}\cdot\lVert s\rVert \,/\, \texttt{inertia\_scale}

    raised, if needed, to _ALPHA_NOISE_EPS · |q1| (§9) so that float32 rounding cannot keep it from ever accepting. opt.ls_tolerance is per world (shape ("*",), default 0.01).

    Example 20.6 takes the first CG direction on a box stack two steps after a kick, when the bottom box is just meeting the floor (12 rows), evaluates \varphi along it in NumPy, finds the exact minimizer by walking the kinks, replays the kernel's bracketing in NumPy, and compares both with the α MJWarp actually took.

    Pitfall: ls_iterations recompiles

    _linesearch_iterative_kernel is a cache_kernel factory with ls_iterations as a Python argument: the round loop is for _ in range(LS_ITERATIONS) with a compile-time bound, so the compiler can unroll it. Every new value compiles a new kernel (seconds on GPU, much longer on CPU), and a CUDA graph captured with the old value keeps running the old kernel until you recapture (Chapter 24). tolerance and ls_tolerance are device arrays: change them any time, even under a graph. iterations is a runtime value that never recompiles, but it is a scalar kernel input and a host loop bound, so a captured graph keeps the old value until you recapture. Cone type, sparsity and warn_overflow are factory arguments too.

    If the round loop runs out before a probe is accepted, the kernel still applies the best α it found, sets OverflowType.LS_ITERATIONS (1 << 10) in d.overflow and, if that bit is in opt.warn_overflow, prints a warning. Example 20.3 provokes it in part 2.

    After each iteration every running world increments solver_niter and runs four tests (three for CG). All of them are made dimensionless with inertia_scale = 1/\operatorname{tr}(M) (_inertia_scale), so one tolerance works for a 1 kg ball and a 100 kg robot:

    TestFormulaMeaningSolver
    no stepalpha == 0the linesearch found nothing betterboth
    improvement0 < scale·improvement < tolthe cost barely droppedboth
    gradientscale·‖grad‖ < tolfirst-order optimalboth
    model improvementscale·½·newton_decrement < tolthe next Newton step would gain less than tolNewton

    If none passes but solver_niter == opt.iterations, the world stops anyway and sets OverflowType.ITERATIONS (1 << 9). Either way it sets ctx.done and does wp.atomic_add(nsolving, 0, -1). A done world returns at the top of every later kernel.

    Example 20.4 logs a cold-started solve iteration by iteration (rerunning it with iterations = 0, 1, 2, ... and a tolerance of 1e-12 so nothing stops early), rebuilds cost, gradient, improvement and the Newton decrement in NumPy after each run, and checks which test would fire at the default 1e-6. Then it collects the per-world solver_niter of 64 kicked worlds after 10 ordinary (warm-started) steps.

    The NumPy replay predicts the stopping iteration exactly: 40 for CG, 3 for Newton, and MJWarp's own solver_niter agrees. CG stopped on the improvement test while its gradient was still around 1e-3: improvement and gradient are different measures, and CG's long tail of small improvements trips the improvement test first. Newton stopped on the model-improvement test, which CG does not have. Drag the tolerance in Figure 7 to see which test fires where.

    Tolerance is per world, and has a floor

    m.opt.tolerance is a batched option of shape ("*",): the kernels read tolerance[worldid % tolerance.shape[0]], so an array with one entry per world gives each world its own tolerance (and a length-B array is shared round-robin). Two details. put_model raises the MJCF value to at least 1e-6 (opt.tolerance = max(opt.tolerance, 1e-6), "C MuJoCo tolerance was chosen for float64"): the default 1e-8 becomes 1e-6. Assigning an array afterwards bypasses that clamp.

    CG's iteration count climbs steadily with tighter tolerances; Newton's does not move. That is not quadratic convergence at work: once the constraint states settle, one Newton step lands exactly on the minimum of that quadratic piece (§6, §7), so the scaled gradient drops from order 10 to about 1e-6 in a single step and then stays there, at the float32 floor. It never reaches 1e-8; at that tolerance the stop comes from the improvement, α == 0 or decrement test firing at rounding-noise level, after the same three iterations. 1e-8 did not cause trouble here, but there is a floor. The linesearch works in float32 relative to the cost at its anchor, and its bracketing cannot resolve steps below about \varepsilon_{32}\,|q_1|/\varphi''. The kernel guards against that with _ALPHA_NOISE_EPS = 8 · 1.19e-7: the slope tolerance is never below _ALPHA_NOISE_EPS · |q1|, a start slope below that floor counts as "already at the minimum", and (for incremental Newton) a step shorter than the noise floor sets ls_exhausted so the stale-ray fast path rebuilds the world. Tolerances far below 1e-6 mostly buy iterations that move qacc by rounding noise.

    Capping iterations, and LS_ITERATIONS

    A capped CG returns whatever it has: after one iteration the bottom box's vertical acceleration is −3.47 instead of −0.089, and after five it even has the wrong sign. The ITERATIONS bit is your only warning, so check d.overflow in development. The kernel's printf lines come from the C side of the process: with stdout redirected to a file (as here) they appear after Python's own lines; in a terminal or a notebook they appear right after the replayed step, once per iteration whose linesearch ran out of rounds (four of the six here).

    Tip: silence the print, keep the bit

    The bit is always set; the print only happens when the bit is in m.opt.warn_overflow. m.opt.warn_overflow &= ~mjw.OverflowType.ITERATIONS silences it (it is read with wp.static, so that change recompiles the printing kernels, as Chapter 9 §10 explains). Bits are sticky: clear them with d.overflow.zero_() or a fresh make_data.

    11Per-world convergence on the GPU

    Recall from Ch 7

    A CUDA graph records launches once and replays them; conditional nodes loop on device; Ch 7 §10. Chapter 24 owns capture, replay and the CUDA ≥ 12.4 requirement for conditional nodes.

    Every world converges at its own iteration, but a GPU kernel launch covers all worlds. MJWarp reconciles the two with one integer, nsolving, and a conditional node:

    • nsolving starts at nworld; each world that finishes subtracts 1 with an atomic.
    • wp.capture_while(nsolving, while_body=_solver_iteration, ...) repeats the iteration while nsolving > 0. Inside a CUDA graph capture this becomes a conditional while-node: the GPU itself checks the counter and relaunches the body, with no trip to the host.
    • Outside a graph capture (eager stepping, as every example here runs), Warp runs capture_while as a host loop that copies nsolving back after every iteration. Same result, one small synchronization per iteration.
    • With m.opt.graph_conditional = False (needed with JAX, or CUDA < 12.4, where check_toolkit_driver only warns) _solve runs for _ in range(opt.iterations): done worlds return early inside each kernel, but all opt.iterations launches still happen. That is the "fixed loop" in the stepper below.

    12Warmstart, and the experimental run_qacc_smooth=False

    The start point matters: Newton converges in a few steps from anywhere, but each step costs a factorization, and CG's iteration count depends strongly on how far it starts from the minimum. MJWarp's warmstart is the previous step's acceleration:

    • step copies qacc into qacc_warmstart after integrating (forward.py::_advance; RK4 stores the last stage's acceleration). forward alone never writes it, so calling forward repeatedly reuses a stale warmstart: harmless, but it changes iteration counts. Snapshots that must replay bitwise include it (State.WARMSTART, Ch 8 §4).
    • _solve_init_dof starts a world from qacc_warmstart when warmstart is enabled and the world has rows (nefc > 0), and from qacc_smooth otherwise. m.opt.disableflags |= mjw.DisableBit.WARMSTART always starts from qacc_smooth.

    Warmstart helps CG on the kicked stacks (mean 44 → 33) and barely matters for Newton. On the resting stack Newton is even slightly faster cold: when the stack sits still, qacc_smooth plus one Newton step is already the answer, while last step's qacc carries a little of the settling motion. C MuJoCo handles this by evaluating the cost at both start points and keeping the cheaper one.

    Version note

    mujoco-warp 4.0.1 never picks the better of the two starts: with warmstart on and rows present it always uses qacc_warmstart. The dev tree adds solver.py::_warmstart_best (dev tree only, not in 4.0.1) for the DISCRETE integrator only, with a TODO to extend it. DISCRETE solves with an effective metric built from passive stiffness (eff_build/eff_solve); Ch 23 §7.

    Experimental: m.opt.run_qacc_smooth = False

    If Newton always starts from the warmstart, it never needs qacc_smooth: the gradient Ma − qfrc_smooth − qfrc_constraint uses qfrc_smooth, not qacc_smooth. And Newton never needs M^{-1} either, because it factors H. So run_qacc_smooth = False skips both the M factorization and the qacc_smooth solve (fwd_acceleration skips smooth.factor_solve_i) and _solve_init_dof copies the warmstart into every world, rows or not. A world without rows then reaches qacc_smooth in one Newton step (H = M). It only takes effect when smooth.skip_qacc_smooth(m, d) is true: Newton, warmstart on, noslip_iterations == 0, iterations > 0 and njmax > 0; otherwise the flag is ignored (types.Option docstring; forward_test.py::test_run_qacc_smooth).

    The two runs are bitwise identical here. That is expected: every world of a stack always has rows, so both modes start from the same warmstart and run the same Newton iterations; the only difference is the skipped work. On this CPU the factorization of a 30-dof M is a tiny part of the step, so there is no visible gain; the saving grows with nv and is a GPU question. d.qacc_smooth is left stale (zeros here, from make_data): never read it in this mode. smooth.skip_qacc_smooth is private and printed here only to show the decision.

    Pitfall

    The flag is experimental (types.Option says so). Anything downstream that reads qacc_smooth or a fresh qLD after the solve, such as your own kernels or a noslip pass, sees stale data; that is why noslip disables the mode.

    13noslip: removing the creep of soft friction

    MuJoCo's constraints are soft: a friction row only produces force through a small residual velocity, so an object that should stick creeps slowly. noslip (solver.py::noslip, enabled by opt.noslip_iterations > 0) is a second, small solve after the main one:

    • It keeps every non-frictional force fixed and re-solves only the friction rows: dof/tendon frictionloss and contact friction.
    • It uses the unregularized Delassus operator A = J M^{-1} J^\top (_noslip_factor_m, _noslip_minv_jt, _noslip_delassus), so there is no softness left in those rows.
    • It runs projected Gauss-Seidel sweeps (_noslip_solve, one thread per world): frictionloss rows are clamped to their interval; pyramidal contacts are processed by pairs of opposing pyramid edges; elliptic contacts by a small cone projection. It stops when the scaled improvement of a sweep falls below opt.noslip_tolerance (per world, default 1e-6) or after noslip_iterations sweeps, and adds its sweep count to d.solver_niter.
    • It then recomputes qacc, qfrc_constraint, efc.state and efc.Ma. It allocates noslip_Y of shape (nworld, njmax, nv) and noslip_L (nworld, nv, nv) on every call: memory to watch at large nv.
    • DISCRETE is not supported: put_model and noslip raise NotImplementedError("noslip solver not implemented for the discrete integrator.").

    MJWarp and C MuJoCo agree to the micrometre in all four cases. With elliptic cones, noslip turns 2.6 mm of creep into 1 µm. With pyramidal cones it changes nothing in this scene, in both engines: a pyramidal contact has no separate friction rows, every row mixes normal and tangential force, and noslip only redistributes force within each pair of opposing edges. So it is not a guaranteed fix: measure it on your scene. Notice also solver_niter going from 1 to 3 and 4: those are noslip sweeps added to the main solver's count.

    14Check yourself

    Quiz

    You change m.opt.ls_iterations from 50 to 20 between steps. What happens on the next step?

    _linesearch_iterative_kernel(ls_iterations, ...) is a cache_kernel factory; LS_ITERATIONS bounds for _ in range(LS_ITERATIONS) at compile time. A captured graph keeps the old kernel until you recapture.

    In Example 20.4, CG stopped at iteration 40 with a scaled gradient of about 1e-3, far above the tolerance 1e-6. Which test fired?

    The cost drop per iteration had fallen below 1e-6 (scaled), so _solve_beta_finalize_tiled marked the world done. Improvement and gradient measure different things.

    Which quantity does the gradient kernel compute for every world?

    It is the comment at the top of _update_gradient. At the minimum it is zero: Ma = qfrc_smooth + qfrc_constraint.

    A model has nv = 72 and uses Newton. Which Cholesky path runs, and what is nv_pad?

    49 ≤ 72 ≤ 96 → blocked; _get_padded_sizes rounds nv + 1 = 73 up to 80 for Newton.

    In the 64-world batch of Example 20.4 the median world needs 5 iterations; why does the solver loop run 9 times?

    The loop count is the max over worlds; each world's solver_niter is its own. That is why the max matters more than the mean.

    For which setting is Newton's incremental Hessian update (rank-1 updates for flipped rows) used?

    _use_incremental(m): solver == NEWTON and cone != ELLIPTIC. Elliptic rows skip the state tracking and their cone terms depend on Jaref.

    You turn on noslip_iterations = 10 and solver_niter goes from 1 to 4. Why?

    _noslip_solve ends with solver_niter_out[worldid] += niter.

    m.opt.run_qacc_smooth = False with opt.solver = CG. What does it do?

    The option docstring: "ignored with CG, disabled warmstart, noslip, or zero njmax or iterations".

    16Summary

    Key takeaways

    • solve starts from qacc_warmstart (or qacc_smooth), builds the context (inertia_scale, Jaref, Ma, states), and iterates linesearch → constraint update → gradient and direction → stopping tests until every world is done.
    • The gradient is Ma − qfrc_smooth − qfrc_constraint; QUADRATIC rows are the only ones that add to the Hessian.
    • CG preconditions with the already-factored M and mixes directions with a Polak-Ribière β clamped at zero (the dev tree switches to Hager-Zhang). Newton factors H = M + J^\top D J per world: one tile up to nv 48, blocked 16x16 tiles up to 96, sparse tiles beyond.
    • Pyramidal Newton updates H by rank-1 terms for rows that flipped, and worlds with no flips take a fast path.
    • The linesearch is exact: a Newton probe from 0, then rounds of three probes in one block reduction; LS_ITERATIONS when it runs out. ls_iterations recompiles; iterations, tolerance, ls_tolerance do not, and the last two are per world.
    • Four stopping tests, all scaled by 1/\operatorname{tr}M; ITERATIONS when the cap is hit. capture_while(nsolving) runs until the slowest world is done, so watch the max of solver_niter.
    • Warmstart helps CG most; 4.0.1 never picks the better start. run_qacc_smooth=False skips the M factorization for warmstarted Newton (experimental). noslip removes elliptic creep and adds its sweeps to solver_niter.

    Next, Chapter 21 shows how sleeping worlds skip most of this work, and Chapter 22 why the row order you saw in Example 20.12 needed a determinism flag.
