A B-tree grows by splitting. Insert keys, watch a full node split around its middle key, and see why the tree only ever gets taller at the root.

This page belongs to Chapter 04: Insertion and splitting. The second page of this chapter, How full do the nodes get?, compares ascending and random keys. The tree below has order M: a node holds at most M − 1 keys. It starts empty. Type a whole number from 0 to 999 and press Insert, or play the guide's twelve canonical keys. Play an interior split shows the one step those twelve keys do not reach bottom-up: a node that has children is split, and its children are divided between the two halves. Every frame comes from a JavaScript B-tree that follows the same rules as the chapter's Zig and Rust code, and is checked against their output at the bottom of the page.

What to look for

MomentBottom-up (what the databases in this guide do)Top-down (textbook, CLRS)
Walking downOnly looks; remembers the path.Splits every full node it is about to enter.
At the leafPuts the key in. If the leaf now holds M keys, it splits.Puts the key in. There is always room.
A splitM keys: the lower M/2 stay, the middle key goes up, the rest move to a new node.M − 1 keys: M/2 − 1 stay, the middle key goes up, M/2 − 1 move. Needs an even M.
The node that splits has childrenThe children are divided with the keys: the left half keeps M/2 keys and M/2 + 1 children (M/2 rounded down), the new node gets the rest.Each half gets M/2 − 1 keys and M/2 children.
The parent is full tooThe split repeats one level higher, along the remembered path.Cannot happen: the parent was made non-full on the way down.
Out of memoryEvery new node is taken before the leaf is touched. If one is missing, the tree stays exactly as it was.Each split takes its node when it happens. Splits already made stay; the tree is valid and holds the same keys, but the new key is not in it.
The root splitsA new root with one key is created above the two halves. This is the only way the tree gets taller.
