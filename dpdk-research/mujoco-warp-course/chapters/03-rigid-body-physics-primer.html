    Rigid-Body Physics from Scratch: Particles, Rigid Bodies, Joints and Contacts

    Before MJWarp steps 4096 worlds at once, it has to step one world correctly. This chapter builds the physics a simulator needs, one idea at a time: a point mass, a time step, a soft floor, a spinning box, a quaternion, a joint, a constraint. You write each idea in a few lines of NumPy, then find the same idea in MuJoCo and in the MJWarp source.

    Beginner About 90 min· Prerequisites: Ch 1, Ch 2

    • Mass, centre of mass, the inertia tensor, its principal axes (body_inertia, body_iquat) and the gyroscopic term \omega \times (I\omega).

    • Joints as reduced coordinates vs maximal coordinates plus constraints.
    • Contacts and limits as soft constraints, and a first look at M\ddot q + c = \tau + J^T f.

    Before you start: recap

    • A simulator maps state in to state out: (qpos, qvel, act, time) goes into step and the next state comes out (Ch 1 §1).
    • MjModel holds constants (masses, sizes, options); MjData holds the state and everything derived from it. MJWarp keeps the same field names (Ch 2 §1).
    • Joint sizes: free joint qpos 7 / qvel 6, ball 4 / 3, hinge and slide 1 / 1. MuJoCo quats are (w,x,y,z); Ch 2 §3.
    • solref = (timeconst, dampratio) of a spring-damper; solimp shapes impedance; Ch 2 §4. Contacts are soft: a little penetration is normal.
    • mujoco.mj_fullM(m, d, dst) copies the mass matrix into a dense array; in MuJoCo 4 the field is mjd.M (Ch 2 §10).

    1A point mass: state, force and F = m a

    Start with the simplest object physics knows: a point mass. It has a mass m, a position x and a velocity v. Nothing else. It cannot spin, because it has no size. Its state is the pair (x, v): if you know both now, you can predict the future.

    Forces change the velocity. Newton's second law says the net force equals mass times acceleration:

    F_\text{net} = m\,a, \qquad a = \dot v = \frac{F_\text{net}}{m}, \qquad v = \dot x

    "Net" means you add every force that acts on the body as vectors. Gravity alone gives F = (0, 0, -m g), so a = (0, 0, -g): every mass falls with the same acceleration. A table under the mass adds an upward normal force; when it exactly cancels gravity, the net force is zero and the mass stays put. Try it in the free-body diagram below: drag the tips of the force arrows and watch the acceleration.

    Time stepping: state in, state out

    A computer cannot follow a smooth curve x(t). It jumps in small time steps of length \Delta t (MuJoCo calls it opt.timestep). Each step reads the current state, computes the forces and the acceleration, and writes the next state. That loop is all a physics engine is, and it is exactly the "state in, state out" picture of Ch 1 §1.

    Example 3.1 drops a point mass from 20 m for 2 s with \Delta t = 0.01 s, two ways. The only difference between the two loops is the order of the two update lines.

    Both are wrong by the same 9.8 cm, in opposite directions. Under a constant force the velocity is exact in both methods (it is a straight line in time), but the position is not. Explicit Euler moves with the old velocity, which is always a little too slow on the way down, so the mass lags above the true curve and ends with too much potential energy. Semi-implicit Euler moves with the new velocity, which is a little too fast, so it ends low. The error is g\,T\,\Delta t / 2: halve the step and you halve the error. That is what first order means.

    So which one is better?

    For a falling mass, neither. The difference shows up as soon as the force depends on the position, like a spring or a pendulum. That is the next section, and it is the reason MuJoCo uses the second ordering.

    2Explicit vs semi-implicit Euler

    Write the two methods side by side. With a_n = F(x_n, v_n)/m:

    Explicit Euler

    \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= x_n + \Delta t\, v_n \\ v_{n+1} &= v_n + \Delta t\, a_n \end{aligned}

    Both lines read only the old state.

    Semi-implicit Euler

    \begin{aligned} v_{n+1} &= v_n + \Delta t\, a_n \\ x_{n+1} &= x_n + \Delta t\, v_{n+1} \end{aligned}

    The position line reads the new velocity.

    Test them on the most important system in physics, a mass on a spring: F = -k x, so a = -\omega^2 x with \omega = \sqrt{k/m}. The exact motion is a circle in the phase plane (x, v/\omega), because the energy E = \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2 never changes. Step through both methods below with a deliberately large step (\omega\,\Delta t = 0.63).

    Two integrators, one spring

    forward.py::_advanceIllustrative, dt = 0.1 s
    # textbook explicit Euler
a = -omega**2 * x     # old x
x = x + v * dt        # old v
v = v + a * dt
# MuJoCo _advance (semi-implicit)
qvel += qacc * dt     # _next_velocity
qpos += qvel * dt     # new qvel

    Explicit Euler steps along the tangent of the circle, and a tangent always points outward, so every step lands on a bigger circle. You can compute exactly how much bigger: one step multiplies the energy by 1 + \omega^2 \Delta t^2. That factor is always above 1, so explicit Euler gains energy on every undamped oscillator, at every step size. Semi-implicit Euler uses the updated velocity, which bends the step back toward the circle. Its update matrix has determinant exactly 1: it preserves area in the phase plane (it is symplectic). The energy wobbles a little, but it does not drift.

    Check the first row against the formula: (1 + 0.063^2)^{1000} = e^{3.94} \approx 51.4. Even at a tiny step, explicit Euler made the spring 51 times more energetic in 10 s. Semi-implicit Euler stays within 3%. At \Delta t = 0.2 it wobbles by up to 2.7× but still does not grow.

    Stability: the step size has a speed limit

    Semi-implicit Euler is stable only while \omega\,\Delta t < 2. Above that, the update matrix has an eigenvalue larger than 1 and the oscillation explodes. Every stiff spring in a scene (a contact, a joint limit, a stiff tendon) has its own \omega, and the stiffest one sets the limit for the whole step. You will see this limit hit in Example 3.8, where a pendulum rod made of a spring with \sqrt{k/m}\,\Delta t = 3.16 blows up.

    Going deeper: the two update matrices

    For a = -\omega^2 x, write the state as s = (x, v). One step is s_{n+1} = A\,s_n with

    A_\text{explicit} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \Delta t \\ -\omega^2\Delta t & 1 \end{pmatrix}
    A_\text{semi} = \begin{pmatrix} 1-\omega^2\Delta t^2 & \Delta t \\ -\omega^2\Delta t & 1 \end{pmatrix}

    \det A_\text{explicit} = 1 + \omega^2\Delta t^2 > 1: phase-space area, and the energy, grow by that factor per step. \det A_\text{semi} = 1, and its trace is 2 - \omega^2 \Delta t^2; the eigenvalues lie on the unit circle (no growth) exactly when |2 - \omega^2\Delta t^2| < 2, that is \omega\Delta t < 2. A unit-determinant map with eigenvalues on the unit circle conserves a nearby quadratic "shadow energy", which is why the real energy only wobbles. Ch 13 compares these with RK4 and the implicit integrators.

    Source: MuJoCo's Euler is semi-implicit

    mujoco_warp/_src/forward.py · euler has the docstring "Euler integrator, semi-implicit in velocity." It calls _advance, which first launches _next_velocity (qvel_out = qvel_in + qacc_scale_in * qacc_in * timestep, one thread per (world, dof)) and then _next_position with qvel_in = qvel or d.qvel, the comment saying "advance positions with qvel if given, d.qvel otherwise (semi-implicit)". Before that, euler also treats joint damping implicitly (it adds timestep * damping to a copy of the mass matrix), which you meet in Ch 13.

    3A bouncing ball on a spring-damper floor

    Now let a ball of radius R fall onto a floor. A real floor is very stiff, but not infinitely stiff: the ball squashes it a tiny bit. The simplest model of that is a penalty spring: whenever the ball dips below the floor by a penetration p = R - z > 0, the floor pushes up with a spring-damper force

    F = \max\bigl(0,\; k\,p - b\,v\bigr), \qquad \text{only while } p > 0

    The max(0, ·) makes the force one-sided: the floor can push, never pull. You could pick k and b by hand, but MuJoCo offers a nicer pair of knobs.

    Recall from Ch 2

    solref = (timeconst, dampratio) of a spring-damper; solimp shapes impedance; Ch 2 §4. The default solref is (0.02, 1).

    MJWarp turns solref into a stiffness and a damping in constraint.py::_contact_kbimp: k = 1 / (dmax² · timeconst² · dampratio²) and b = 2 / (dmax · timeconst), per unit of "constraint mass". Our NumPy floor uses the same formula with d_\text{max} = 1:

    \frac{k}{m} = \frac{1}{(\text{timeconst}\cdot\text{dampratio})^2}, \qquad \frac{b}{m} = \frac{2}{\text{timeconst}}

    This is a damped oscillator with natural frequency \omega_n = 1/(\text{timeconst}\cdot\text{dampratio}) and damping ratio b/(2 m \omega_n) = \text{dampratio}. So dampratio is literally the damping ratio: 1 is critically damped (the ball sinks in and stops, no bounce), smaller values bounce. timeconst sets how fast the contact acts: smaller is stiffer. Play with both:

    Example 3.3 runs the same model in NumPy for three solref values, then drops the shared BALL_DROP ball in C MuJoCo and in MJWarp with the default solref, and compares the deepest penetration.

    Two things stand out. First, the dynamic penetration of the critically damped NumPy floor has the same scale as MuJoCo's: 28.2 mm at \Delta t = 2 ms (about 32 mm as \Delta t \to 0; the coarse step itself shaves some off) against MuJoCo's 27.3 mm. The solref knobs describe the same spring-damper; the rest of the gap comes from MuJoCo's d_\text{max} = 0.95 inside k and from its impedance d < 1 (see the box below). Second, the resting penetration differs tenfold: 3.92 mm for NumPy, 0.37 mm for MuJoCo. A penalty spring must sink until k p = m g, so p = g/(k/m) = 9.81/2500. MuJoCo is not a penalty method: it treats the contact as a soft constraint and solves for the force (section 8). The spring only sets a reference acceleration that pulls the violation back to zero.

    Going deeper: predicting MuJoCo's 0.367 mm exactly

    For a single contact row, MuJoCo's soft-constraint solution mixes two accelerations with the impedance d \in (0, 1) from solimp: the acceleration the ball would have with no contact force, a_0 = -g, and the reference acceleration of the spring, a_\text{ref}:

    a_1 = (1-d)\,a_0 + d\,a_\text{ref}, \qquad a_\text{ref} = -b\,v - k\,d\,r

    The second formula is constraint.py::_efc_row's branch for every integrator except DISCRETE (Euler, RK4, implicit, implicitfast): aref = -k * imp * pos_aref - b * vel - aref_shift, with aref_shift = 0 here. (The DISCRETE integrator's branch adds a timestep * k * imp * vel term and divides by a factor f.) At rest (a_1 = 0, v = 0, signed distance r = -p): 0 = -(1-d) g + d^2 k p, so p = (1-d)\,g / (d^2 k). With the default solimp (0.9, 0.95, 0.001, 0.5, 2) the impedance at this depth is d = 0.9135 and k = 1/(0.95^2 \cdot 0.02^2) = 2770, which gives p = 0.0865 \cdot 9.81 / (0.8345 \cdot 2770) = 0.367 mm, the value MuJoCo and MJWarp print. Ch 19 derives the mixing rule from the constraint solver's optimization problem.

    Pitfall: timeconst below two time steps is clamped

    A spring that is too stiff for the step size explodes (section 2). MuJoCo protects you: _contact_kbimp does timeconst = wp.max(timeconst, 2.0 * timestep) unless the REFSAFE disable flag is set or integrator="discrete" is used. With the default timestep=0.002, any timeconst below 0.004 s behaves like 0.004 s.

    4A rigid body: six degrees of freedom

    A rigid body has size, so besides moving it can turn. "Rigid" means the distances between its points never change, so the whole body is described by where one reference point is and how the body is turned. That is 6 degrees of freedom (DOF): 3 for translation and 3 for rotation.

    The 6-DOF state has four parts:

    PartNumbersFree joint slotFrame
    position of the body frame3qpos[0:3]world
    orientation, a unit quaternion (w, x, y, z)4qpos[3:7]body relative to world
    linear velocity3qvel[0:3]world
    angular velocity \omega3qvel[3:6]body (local)

    Recall from Ch 2

    MuJoCo quats are (w,x,y,z); Ch 2 §3. A rotation by angle \theta about the unit axis u is q = (\cos\tfrac\theta2,\ u \sin\tfrac\theta2).

    Why 4 numbers for orientation but 3 for angular velocity?

    A rotation has only 3 degrees of freedom (an axis direction, 2 numbers, and an angle, 1 number). Every 3-number description, such as roll-pitch-yaw angles, has a singularity: an orientation where two of the angles do the same thing and a small turn needs a huge change of the numbers (gimbal lock). A unit quaternion uses 4 numbers tied by one rule, |q| = 1, so it still has 3 degrees of freedom but no singularity. The angular velocity is different: it is a rate, "turn about this axis at this speed", and a 3-vector always works. So:

    • qpos lives on a curved space (the unit sphere of quaternions): 7 numbers for 6 DOF.
    • qvel lives in a flat space of rates: 6 numbers for 6 DOF. This is why nq ≥ nv in MuJoCo, and why you can never just add qvel * dt to qpos for a free or ball joint (section 6).

    Pitfall: the angular velocity of a free joint is in the body frame

    For a free joint, qvel[0:3] is a world-frame linear velocity, but qvel[3:6] is the angular velocity in the body's own frame. You can see it in forward.py::_next_position: the position update is qpos_pos + timestep * qvel_lin (world), while the orientation update is math.quat_integrate(qpos_quat, qvel_ang, timestep), which multiplies the rotation on the right of the current quaternion (a body-frame rotation). smooth.py builds the free joint's rotational motion axes from the body's rotation matrix, with the comment "I_3 rotation in child frame". To spin a body about the world z axis, rotate the world vector into the body frame first.

    After 1 s at 2 rad/s the box has turned 2 rad about z, and the quaternion is (\cos 1, 0, 0, \sin 1) = (0.5403, 0, 0, 0.8415), still exactly unit length. The inertia matches the box formula: for side lengths a, b, c, I_{xx} = m(b^2 + c^2)/12 and so on. The last line shows that MJWarp's Model carries the same numbers on the device, with a leading batch axis of size 1 (Ch 6, Ch 10).

    5Mass properties: centre of mass, inertia tensor, principal axes

    Mass says how hard it is to push a body. For turning you need more: a long rod is easy to spin about its own axis and hard to spin end over end. Three quantities describe it.

    • Centre of mass (com) c = \frac1m\sum_i m_i r_i: the mass-weighted mean position. Gravity acts as if all the mass were there. MuJoCo stores it in the body frame as body_ipos.
    • The inertia tensor about the com, a symmetric 3×3 matrix I = \sum_i m_i\,(|r_i|^2 E - r_i r_i^T) with r_i measured from the com. It maps angular velocity to angular momentum: L = I\omega.
    • Its principal axes: the eigenvectors of I. In that frame the tensor is diagonal, and the three eigenvalues are the principal moments. MuJoCo stores the moments as body_inertia and the rotation from the body frame to the principal frame as the quaternion body_iquat.

    So MuJoCo never keeps the full 3×3 matrix: 3 moments plus a rotation say the same thing with no wasted numbers. The full tensor in the body frame is I = R\,\mathrm{diag}(\texttt{body\_inertia})\,R^T, where R is the rotation matrix of body_iquat. The widget lets you set the three moments and turn the principal frame:

    Not every three numbers are a body

    Because I_1 = \sum m (y^2 + z^2) etc., real moments satisfy the triangle inequality I_1 \le I_2 + I_3 (for every ordering). Push one slider far up in the widget and the ellipsoid becomes impossible. MuJoCo's compiler rejects such an <inertial diaginertia> unless you set balanceinertia.

    Building the tensor of a composite body

    When a body has several geoms, MuJoCo's compiler adds them up. The tool is the parallel-axis theorem: a part of mass m_i whose own centre sits at offset r from the com contributes its own inertia plus m_i(|r|^2 E - r r^T). Step through it for an L-shaped body made of two boxes:

    Inertia of an L-shape

    ex5_inertia_tensor.py (the compiler does the same)
    mass = sum(m_i)
com = sum(m_i * c_i) / mass
I = zeros((3, 3))
for m_i, c_i, h in parts:
    I_own = box_inertia(m_i, 2 * h)
    r = c_i - com
    I += I_own + m_i*(r@r*E - outer(r,r))
evals, evecs = eigh(I)   # moments, axes

    The off-diagonal I_{xz} = 0.0417 says the L's mass is not balanced around the body axes: spinning it about body x would wobble. NumPy's eigh returns the moments ascending; MuJoCo's compiler stored them descending, with a body_iquat to match. The order is a convention; the check that matters is that R\,\mathrm{diag}(I)\,R^T rebuilds the same tensor.

    Euler's rotation equation and the gyroscopic term

    For translation, F = m\dot v. For rotation, in the principal body frame, the analogue is Euler's rotation equation:

    I\,\dot\omega + \omega \times (I\,\omega) = \tau

    The extra piece \omega \times (I\omega) is the gyroscopic term. It is there because the body frame itself turns: even with zero torque, angular momentum L = I\omega is constant in the world, so seen from the turning body it must change. When \omega lies along a principal axis, I\omega is parallel to \omega and the term vanishes: the body spins steadily. Otherwise it couples the three axes, and a famous effect appears.

    You will watch this happen in MJWarp at the end of the next section, once you know how the orientation is stepped forward in time.

    6Integrating an orientation

    Positions are easy to step: x \mathrel{+}= v\,\Delta t. Orientations are not, because a quaternion must stay on the unit sphere. The exact rule for a constant body-frame angular velocity over one step is: "turn by the angle |\omega|\,\Delta t about the axis \omega/|\omega|", which as a quaternion is the exponential map

    q_{n+1} = q_n \otimes \exp\!\left(\tfrac12\,\omega\,\Delta t\right)
    \exp\!\left(\tfrac12\,\omega\Delta t\right) = \left(\cos\tfrac{|\omega|\Delta t}{2},\ \tfrac{\omega}{|\omega|}\sin\tfrac{|\omega|\Delta t}{2}\right)

    The product is on the right because \omega is in the body frame. This is what MJWarp does for every free and ball joint:

    Source: mujoco_warp/_src/math.py · quat_integrate

    @wp.func
def quat_integrate(q: wp.quat, v: wp.vec3, dt: float) -> wp.quat:
  """Integrates a quaternion given angular velocity and dt."""
  norm_ = wp.length(v)
  v = wp.normalize(v)  # does that need proper zero gradient handling?
  angle = dt * norm_

  q_res = axis_angle_to_quat(v, angle)
  q = wp.normalize(q)
  q_res = mul_quat(q, q_res)

  return wp.normalize(q_res)

    Called from forward.py::_next_position for JointType.FREE (with qvel[3:6]) and JointType.BALL (with its 3 dofs). Hinge and slide joints just do qpos + timestep * qvel, since their coordinate is a plain angle or distance. The owner of the interactive quaternion-integration widget is Ch 13.

    Version note

    In the 4.0.1 wheel _next_position is a plain @wp.kernel. In the dev tree (commit 8f5b9f1) it is a kernel factory _next_position(enable_sleep) that skips sleeping trees (Ch 21); the free/ball branch with math.quat_integrate is the same in both.

    The tempting shortcut is to differentiate instead: \dot q = \tfrac12\,q \otimes (0, \omega), so "q \mathrel{+}= \Delta t\,\dot q, then divide by |q|". Each step leaves the unit sphere along the tangent (norm \sqrt{1 + (|\omega|\Delta t/2)^2}) and the renormalization pulls it back, but to a point that has turned by 2\arctan(|\omega|\Delta t/2) instead of |\omega|\Delta t. The body falls behind by about (|\omega|\Delta t)^3/12 per step, forever.

    mujoco_warp._src.math is private API: the name may change between versions. It is used here only to show that the real function matches the formula.

    4.8° of error after 16 turns, from an update that looks perfectly reasonable. The exact update is limited only by round-off: 10^{-14} rad in float64, 10^{-5} rad in MJWarp's float32 (MJWarp has no float64 mode; Ch 1 lists it among the unsupported features). Without the renormalization the naive quaternion's norm reaches 3.5, and a non-unit quaternion is not a rotation at all.

    7Joints and reduced coordinates

    A free body has 6 DOF. Most bodies in a robot are not free: a forearm is attached to an upper arm by an elbow. There are two ways to describe that.

    • Maximal coordinates: give every body its full pose and add constraints ("these two points must coincide") that remove the forbidden DOFs. Simple to set up, but the constraints are only ever satisfied approximately, and they are stiff.
    • Reduced coordinates (joint or generalized coordinates): describe each body relative to its parent with only the DOFs its joint allows. A hinge leaves 1 of 6. The joint cannot come apart, because there is no number that could describe it coming apart.

    The table is the whole trade-off in five rows. A soft rod (k/m = 10^3) stretches by 2 cm and the period is 1.3% too long. Each factor 10 in stiffness buys a factor 10 in accuracy, until \sqrt{k/m}\,\Delta t passes 2 and semi-implicit Euler blows up (section 2). The hinge gets the large-amplitude period (1.4411 s vs the exact 1.4410 s, not the small-angle 1.4185 s) with one coordinate and no stiffness at all.

    Bodies in a tree

    MuJoCo builds every model as a kinematic tree: the world body is the root; each body hangs from one parent through zero or more joints, and its pose is computed from its parent's pose plus its joints' coordinates (Ch 11 walks the tree in MJWarp's kinematics). The joint types and their sizes are the ones you met in Ch 2:

    8Constraints: contacts, limits and equalities

    A constraint is a rule about the state that the joints alone do not guarantee. When the rule is broken, a constraint force pushes the state back. MuJoCo has three families, and they differ in which direction the force may act:

    • Contacts are one-sided: two shapes may separate freely but may not overlap. The normal force can only push. Tangential friction resists sliding, with a force limited to roughly \mu times the normal force (the friction cone, Ch 9).
    • Joint limits are one-sided too: a hinge with range="-90 90" moves freely inside the range; only past an end does the limit push back.
    • Equality constraints (connect, weld, joint, tendon) are two-sided: "these two frames coincide" can be violated in any direction, and the force may pull or push. They close loops the tree cannot express.

    Soft constraints

    A hard constraint ("never overlap, not by a nanometre") needs an infinite force over zero time: bad news for a simulator with a fixed step. MuJoCo's constraints are soft: a small violation is allowed, and the solver computes a force that drives it back to zero along the spring-damper path set by solref (section 3). That is why a ball resting on the floor sits 0.37 mm inside it, and why that is not a bug.

    In MJWarp the pipeline turns every active constraint into one or more constraint rows (the "efc" arrays; a pyramidal contact with friction gets 4) and a solver finds all the forces at once, every step. Example 3.9 lets a ball settle and reads the result.

    In every world the contact pushes up with exactly m g = 9.81 N, the bias force on the vertical dof (gravity, which appears in qfrc_bias because MuJoCo writes it on the left of the equation, section 9) is 9.81 N, and the vertical acceleration is zero. Each contact uses 4 constraint rows (nefc = 4), and all 4 worlds share one contact pool (nacon = 4). Sizing that pool with nconmax and njmax is the topic of Ch 9.

    The equation of motion, previewed

    For one point mass the law was m a = F. For a whole tree of bodies described in reduced coordinates q (that is qpos, with velocity qvel and acceleration qacc), the same law becomes one matrix equation:

    Example 3.10 checks it on the double pendulum from Ch 2. This scene has no contacts or limits, so J^T f = 0 and \ddot q = M^{-1}(\tau - c). You will derive M and c yourself in Ch 11; for now, see that they exist and that the equation balances.

    Read the bias line: with the elbow bent and both joints moving, c is gravity plus a small velocity part (Coriolis and centrifugal forces). M depends on the pose: bend the elbow and the off-diagonal coupling changes. MJWarp gets the same qacc in float32.

    Your own physics engine in 40 lines

    Everything in this chapter fits in one small class: a batched state (one ball per world), a "forward" pass (gravity, collision detection, a one-sided soft floor), and a semi-implicit integrator. The notebook has it as a cell and compares it with MJWarp in the last cell:

    # Your own physics engine in ~40 lines: many worlds, one ball each, a soft floor.
import numpy as np


class TinyEngine:
    """Batched point-mass engine: state (z, v) per world, semi-implicit Euler, penalty floor."""

    def __init__(self, nworld, radius=0.1, dt=0.002, timeconst=0.02, dampratio=1.0, g=9.81):
        self.nworld, self.radius, self.dt, self.g = nworld, radius, dt, g
        self.k = 1.0 / (timeconst * dampratio) ** 2     # floor stiffness per unit mass
        self.b = 2.0 / timeconst                         # floor damping per unit mass
        self.z = np.ones(nworld)                         # qpos: one number per world
        self.v = np.zeros(nworld)                        # qvel
        self.t = 0.0

    def forces(self):
        """Acceleration of every world at the current state (the 'forward' pass)."""
        a = np.full(self.nworld, -self.g)                # gravity
        pen = self.radius - self.z                       # > 0 inside the floor
        contact = pen > 0                                # 'collision detection'
        push = np.maximum(0.0, self.k * pen - self.b * self.v)
        a[contact] += push[contact]                      # one-sided 'constraint' force
        return a

    def step(self):
        """One time step: forward, then integrate (velocity first, like MuJoCo's Euler)."""
        a = self.forces()
        self.v += a * self.dt
        self.z += self.v * self.dt
        self.t += self.dt


eng = TinyEngine(nworld=4)
eng.z[:] = np.linspace(0.3, 0.8, 4)
for _ in range(500):
    eng.step()
print(f"t = {eng.t:.2f} s, z per world:", np.round(eng.z, 4))
print("rest penetration [mm]:", np.round(1000 * (eng.radius - eng.z), 3))

    10Check yourself

    Quiz

    A body with a free joint and no other joints. What are nq and nv?

    3 position + 4 quaternion = 7 coordinates; 3 linear + 3 angular velocity = 6 DOF. The quaternion's unit-norm rule removes the 7th DOF.

    Why does MuJoCo's Euler integrator update the velocity before the position?

    Semi-implicit Euler's update matrix has determinant 1. Explicit Euler multiplies a spring's energy by 1+\omega^2\Delta t^2 every step.

    Explicit Euler on an undamped spring with \omega\,\Delta t = 0.1. Each step multiplies the energy by...

    1 + 0.1^2 = 1.01: 1% per step, so about 1.01^{1000} \approx 21\,000 after 1000 steps.

    When is the gyroscopic term \omega \times (I\omega) zero?

    On a principal axis I\omega = I_k\,\omega is parallel to \omega, and the cross product of parallel vectors is zero.

    Which constraints are one-sided (they can only push)?

    A contact may separate and a joint may move inside its range freely; only the violation is resisted. Equalities resist error in every direction.

    What does mjm.body_iquat store?

    In that frame the inertia tensor is diagonal with entries body_inertia. The full tensor is R\,\mathrm{diag}(I)\,R^T.

    A ball resting on the floor in MJWarp sits 0.37 mm inside it. What should you do?

    The rest depth is (1-d)\,g/(d^2 k). A smaller timeconst or a higher impedance makes it smaller.

    Why can't you integrate a free joint's orientation with qpos[3:7] += qvel[3:6] * dt?

    That is why _next_position calls math.quat_integrate for free and ball joints and only adds for hinge and slide joints.

    In maximal coordinates the pendulum rod is a spring k. What does making it 10× stiffer do?

    Stability needs \sqrt{k/m}\,\Delta t < 2. Reduced coordinates avoid the trade-off: the hinge has no rod-length coordinate at all.

    import numpy as np

G = np.array([0.0, -9.81])
C_OVER_M = 0.01            # quadratic drag coefficient per unit mass [1/m]


def landing_distance(dt, v0=30.0, angle_deg=45.0):
    """Semi-implicit Euler for a projectile with drag a = g - (c/m)|v| v. Returns x at z = 0."""
    a0 = np.radians(angle_deg)
    p = np.zeros(2)
    v = v0 * np.array([np.cos(a0), np.sin(a0)])
    while True:
        a = G - C_OVER_M * np.linalg.norm(v) * v
        v = v + a * dt
        p_new = p + v * dt
        if p_new[1] < 0:                        # crossed the ground in this step
            s = p[1] / (p[1] - p_new[1])        # linear interpolation inside the step
            return p[0] + s * (p_new[0] - p[0])
        p = p_new


ref = landing_distance(1e-5)
mine = landing_distance(0.01)
print(f"reference (dt=1e-5): {ref:.3f} m, dt=0.01: {mine:.3f} m, "
      f"error {100 * abs(mine - ref) / ref:.3f} %")
print(f"no drag would give v0^2/g = {30.0**2 / 9.81:.3f} m")
assert abs(mine - ref) / ref < 0.02

    12Summary

    Key takeaways

    • A simulator repeats one loop: state in, forces, a = F/m, integrate, state out.
    • The order of the two Euler updates matters. Semi-implicit (velocity first, then position with the new velocity) is symplectic and does not pump energy into oscillators; it is what MuJoCo's euler does (_next_velocity then _next_position). It is stable only while \omega\,\Delta t < 2 for the stiffest spring in the scene.
    • solref (timeconst, dampratio) is a spring-damper: k = 1/(d_\text{max}\,\text{tc}\,\text{dr})^2, b = 2/(d_\text{max}\,\text{tc}); dampratio is the damping ratio.
    • A rigid body has 6 DOF: position + orientation (quat), linear + angular velocity. A free joint has qpos 7 and qvel 6, and its angular velocity is in the body frame.
    • MuJoCo stores the inertia tensor as principal moments body_inertia plus a rotation body_iquat (and the com body_ipos). Euler's equation I\dot\omega + \omega\times(I\omega) = \tau explains the tennis-racket flip; the gyroscopic term lives in qfrc_bias.
    • Orientations are integrated with the exponential map q \otimes \exp(\omega\Delta t/2) (math.quat_integrate); add-and-renormalize drifts by (|\omega|\Delta t)^3/12 per step.
    • Joints are reduced coordinates (exact, no stiffness); contacts, limits and equalities are soft constraints (one-sided, one-sided, two-sided) that the solver resolves each step. Together: M\ddot q + c = \tau + J^T f.
