    Part IV · Chapter 15

    Collision I: Geometry Toolkit, Pair Filtering and the Broadphase

    Before MJWarp computes a single contact, it has to decide which geom pairs are worth looking at. This chapter builds the geometric tools that decision rests on (bounding spheres, boxes and separating axes), then follows the decision through the code: the pair list that put_model prunes once, the bounding-volume filters that run every step, and the two broadphase algorithms, NXN and sweep-and-prune, that do the work for thousands of worlds at once.

    Advanced About 100 min· Prerequisites: Ch 9, Ch 11

    You will learn

    • Read the model-time pair pre-filter in io.py::put_model (nxn_pairid_contact: contype/conaffinity, weld, dof-less, parent-child, exclude, explicit pairs, collision sensors) and upper_tri_index.
    • Describe the four bounding-volume filters and why the default is PLANE | SPHERE | OBB.
    • Explain NXN (one logical thread per world and filtered pair) and SAP (project, sort, range, prefix sum, sweep with work packages).
    • Choose a broadphase (the 250k-pair / 1000-geom rule), switch it, and benchmark it.
    • Write a broadphase-like filter kernel yourself and check it against d.ncollision.

    Before you start: recap

    • Contacts of all worlds share one pool of naconmax = nconmax * nworld slots; threads reserve slots with an atomic counter (d.nacon) and anything past the end is dropped (Ch 9 §2).
    • At the end of a step, ncollision > naconmax sets the BROADPHASE overflow bit and nacon > naconmax the NARROWPHASE bit in d.overflow (Ch 9 §7).
    • Kinematics writes each geom's world position and rotation, d.geom_xpos and d.geom_xmat, for every world; collision reads them (Ch 11, Ch 7 §2).
    • A batched model field is read as field[worldid % field.shape[0], ...]: shape[0] is 1 unless you randomized it per world (Ch 6 §7).
    • wp.static bakes a Python value into a kernel, so changing it builds a new kernel (Ch 4 §7); integer wp.atomic_add hands out unique slot numbers (Ch 4 §9).

    1The geometry toolkit

    A scene with n geoms has n(n-1)/2 geom pairs. Ten geoms give 45 pairs, which is nothing. A thousand geoms give 499,500 pairs per world, and with 4,096 worlds that is about two billion pairs every step. The exact contact computation for one pair (the narrowphase, Ch 16 and Ch 17) can cost hundreds of floating-point operations, and for convex meshes far more. Nobody can afford to run it on every pair.

    The way out is the oldest idea in collision detection: first ask a cheap, conservative question for every pair ("could these two possibly touch?"), and only run the expensive question on the pairs that answer yes. That first stage is the broadphase. "Conservative" is the key word. A broadphase may say "maybe" for a pair that turns out not to touch (a false positive costs some narrowphase time), but it must never say "no" for a pair that does touch (a false negative is a missed contact, and objects pass through each other).

    All the cheap questions are built from a handful of geometric tools. This section builds them in 2-D, where you can see and drag them. Every tool has a 3-D version in MJWarp's source, which later sections point to.

    Bounding volumes: sphere, AABB, OBB

    A bounding volume is a simple shape that contains the real geom. If two bounding volumes do not overlap, the geoms inside cannot overlap either. MJWarp uses three kinds, all precomputed by MuJoCo's compiler and copied by put_model:

    • Bounding sphere, radius m.geom_rbound, centred on the geom's frame origin (d.geom_xpos). Rotation does not change it, so it is the cheapest test of all: two spheres overlap when the distance between centres is at most the sum of the radii. A plane is infinite and has no bounding sphere: MuJoCo stores rbound = 0 for planes, and MJWarp uses exactly that zero as its "this is a plane" marker.
    • AABB (axis-aligned bounding box): a box aligned with the world axes. Overlap is a per-axis interval test. The catch is that it must be rebuilt whenever the geom rotates, and a rotated thin shape gets a fat AABB.
    • OBB (oriented bounding box): a box in the geom's own frame. It rotates with the geom and stays tight. MuJoCo stores it as m.geom_aabb, shape (*, ngeom, 2) of vec3: row 0 is the box centre and row 1 the half-sizes, both in the geom's local frame (the name says "aabb" because the box is axis-aligned in the local frame). Testing two OBBs needs the separating-axis idea below.

    Drag the orange shape and turn the rotation slider. The verdict chips compare the three bounding-volume tests with the truth for the two shapes. No volume is always looser than another (the sphere and the AABB can disagree in either direction), but one rule always holds: if any volume says "apart", the shapes are apart. That is why MJWarp can chain the tests (sphere, then AABB, then OBB) and stop at the first "apart".

    The separating-axis test

    How do you decide whether two rotated boxes overlap? The separating axis theorem says: two convex shapes are disjoint exactly when there is some direction (an axis) such that their shadows (projections) on that axis do not overlap. Projecting is easy. A box with centre c, unit axes a_k and half-sizes h_k covers, on a unit axis n, the interval c\cdot n \pm r with

    r = \sum_k \left| h_k \,(a_k \cdot n) \right|.

    Two intervals are separated when the distance between their centres exceeds the sum of their radii: |(c_2 - c_1)\cdot n| > r_1 + r_2. The theorem would let you test infinitely many axes; for boxes, a finite list is enough. In 2-D it is the 2 edge normals of each box (4 axes). In 3-D it is the 3 face normals of each box plus the 9 cross products of an edge of one box with an edge of the other: 15 axes.

    Pick an axis below to see the two shadows. A red gap on any one axis proves the boxes are apart; the boxes overlap only if all four axes show overlap.

    Source: the 3-D OBB filter tests 6 axes, not 15

    mujoco_warp/_src/collision_driver.py::_obb_filter ("see mj_collideOBB") builds the 6 face normals of the two boxes from the columns of xmat1 and xmat2, projects both boxes on each one with exactly the radius formula above, and returns False as soon as radius[0] + radius[1] + margin < |proj[1] - proj[0]|. It skips the 9 edge-edge axes. That makes it weaker than an exact box-box test: some separated configurations, where only an edge-edge axis separates the boxes, still pass. For a broadphase that is fine: the answer stays conservative, the exact narrowphase rejects the pair later, and 6 axes are cheaper than 15. Exercise 15.2 checks your NumPy version against it.

    Closest points between two segments

    A capsule is a segment with a radius. Two capsules touch when the distance between their segments is at most the sum of their radii, so the core tool is "closest points between two segments". Write the segments as P(s) = p_1 + s\,d_1 and Q(t) = p_2 + t\,d_2 with s, t \in [0, 1], and minimize |P(s) - Q(t)|^2. Without the bounds the minimum solves a 2×2 linear system; with them you clamp s, recompute t, and if t leaves [0, 1] clamp it and recompute s (Ericson, Real-Time Collision Detection, §5.1.9). When the segments are parallel the system is singular and there is a whole family of closest pairs: the function picks s = 0. Drag the four endpoints.

    Going deeper: MJWarp's two segment routines

    _src/math.py::closest_segment_to_segment_points parametrizes each segment by its midpoint and half-length, solves the unclamped problem with a small + 1e-6 in the denominator (so the parallel case never divides by zero), clamps both parameters, and then refines by projecting each candidate point back onto the other segment and keeping the better pair. The capsule-capsule collider, collision_primitive_core.py::capsule_capsule, instead calls _segment_weights, which returns one or two closest-point pairs: for (near-)parallel axes it returns two, so a capsule lying on another capsule gets two contacts, not one. Ch 16 owns that collider.

    Convex and non-convex shapes

    A shape is convex if the segment between any two of its points stays inside it. Spheres, capsules, ellipsoids, cylinders and boxes are convex. The separating-axis theorem, and the support-function algorithms of Ch 17 (GJK and EPA), are only exact for convex shapes. A non-convex shape (an L-bracket, a mug) can have two parts that both overlap a third shape's shadow on every axis while the shapes themselves do not touch. The convex hull is the smallest convex shape containing a set of points (a rubber band snapped around it). MuJoCo collides a mesh geom through its convex hull; a genuinely non-convex object has to be split into several convex geoms. Minkowski sums, which turn "do A and B overlap" into "does one shape contain the origin", come in Ch 17.

    Example 15.1 packages the three 2-D tools in plain NumPy (no MJWarp yet) and runs them on four box poses and four segment pairs. The third box pose is the interesting one: the two boxes' AABBs overlap, but an OBB axis (B.y) separates them, which is exactly the case where an OBB filter saves a narrowphase call.

    Read the parallel case carefully: s=0.500 t=0.000. The unclamped system is singular, so the code starts at s=0, gets t<0, clamps t=0 and recomputes s = 0.5. Any pair with the same distance 0.5 would be equally correct.

    2The two-stage funnel

    mjw.collision(m, d) (and therefore every mjw.step) runs the funnel in collision_driver.py::collision:

    1. It creates a small CollisionContext (collision_core.py::create_collision_context) with three arrays of length d.naconmax: collision_pair (vec2i, the two geom ids), collision_pairid (vec2i, the pair's row of m.nxn_pairid, see §3) and collision_worldid. It zeroes d.ncollision and d.nacon.
    2. The broadphase (nxn_broadphase or sap_broadphase, chosen by m.opt.broadphase) writes one entry per candidate pair, for all worlds, reserving entries with wp.atomic_add(d.ncollision, 0, 1).
    3. The narrowphase (_narrowphase: convex, primitive, then SDF colliders) reads the candidates and writes real contacts into d.contact, reserving slots with d.nacon.

    So there are two counters and two buffers, and both buffers have the same capacity, naconmax. The animation shows three worlds. Every dot is one geom pair. Grey dots were removed when the model was built (§3) and never get a thread. The rest fall onto the broadphase sieve (§4-§6); survivors queue in collision_pair, mixed across worlds in whatever order the atomics hand out; the narrowphase turns some of them into contacts.

    Version note: where the candidate arrays live

    In the 4.0.1 wheel the candidate arrays are not fields of Data: collision() allocates them in a fresh CollisionContext on every call (the source carries # TODO(team): create context outside collision?). Only the counter, d.ncollision, is in Data. The dev tree builds the context through a prepare_collision_context helper, which is not in 4.0.1. Either way, you read d.ncollision, not the pair list.

    Pitfall: overflow shows up at the end of the step

    collision() itself never reports overflow. The check is in the end-of-step kernel built by forward.py::_next_time_builder: it compares ncollision_in[0] > naconmax_in, sets OverflowType.BROADPHASE in every world's d.overflow and prints a warning from world 0. If you call mjw.kinematics + mjw.collision by hand (as several examples here do), compare d.ncollision.numpy()[0] with d.naconmax yourself. With sleeping on in 4.0.1, collision() zeroes d.ncollision again before the incremental pass (§8), so after a step it counts only the incremental pass's candidates, and the BROADPHASE check sees only those.

    3Static pre-filtering in put_model

    Some pairs can never collide, whatever the state: two geoms on the same rigid body, a floor and a wall that are both part of the world, a pair you excluded in MJCF. Testing them every step would waste work, so io.py::put_model removes them once, in NumPy, when it builds the model. The result is the static work list every later stage starts from.

    From all pairs to the work list

    The code starts from every unordered pair in np.triu_indices(ngeom, k=1) order and stores it as m.nxn_geom_pair. It then computes five boolean masks over that list and marks a pair -2 ("never test") if any mask rejects it. Here is the core, verbatim from the 4.0.1 wheel:

    geom1, geom2 = np.triu_indices(mjm.ngeom, k=1)
m.nxn_geom_pair = np.stack((geom1, geom2), axis=1)
...
self_collision = weldid1 == weldid2
# both weld bodies dof-less: no forces can act, skip (matches filterBodyPair)
no_dofs = (mjm.body_dofnum[weldid1] == 0) & (mjm.body_dofnum[weldid2] == 0)
parent_child_collision = (
  filterparent & (weldid1 != 0) & (weldid2 != 0) & ((weldid1 == weld_parentid2) | (weldid2 == weld_parentid1))
)
mask = np.array((contype1 & conaffinity2) | (contype2 & conaffinity1), dtype=bool)
exclude = np.isin((bodyid1 << 16) + bodyid2, mjm.exclude_signature)

nxn_pairid_contact = -1 * np.ones(len(geom1), dtype=int)
nxn_pairid_contact[~(mask & ~self_collision & ~no_dofs & ~parent_child_collision & ~exclude)] = -2

    Note that nxn_pairid_contact is a local variable inside put_model, not a function or a model field. What ends up on the model is m.nxn_pairid, an (nnxn_geom_pair,) array of vec2i (as NumPy, (nnxn_geom_pair, 2) ints; the size name types.py uses, not to be confused with npair, the number of explicit <pair> elements) whose two components are:

    ColumnValueMeaning
    0: contact pair id-2never collide (a static filter rejected it)
    -1default pair: collide with materials mixed from the two geoms
    ≥ 0explicit <contact><pair> number i: collide with the pair's own margin, gap, friction, condim, solref, solimp
    1: collision-sensor id-1no distance sensor needs this pair
    ≥ 0a distance / normal / fromto sensor (GEOMDIST, GEOMNORMAL, GEOMFROMTO) needs it (Ch 14)

    Finally a pair is included if nxn_include = (nxn_pairid_contact > -2) | (nxn_pairid_collision >= 0), and the included rows become m.nxn_geom_pair_filtered and m.nxn_pairid_filtered. The diagram follows the pipeline; hover a box.

    contype and conaffinity

    Every geom has two bitmasks, contype ("what I am") and conaffinity ("what I touch"), both 1 by default. A pair is compatible when one geom's type shares a bit with the other's affinity, in either direction:

    (\text{contype}_1 \,\&\, \text{conaffinity}_2) \;|\; (\text{contype}_2 \,\&\, \text{conaffinity}_1) \ne 0 .

    The "either direction" part surprises people: geom c below (contype 2, conaffinity 1) touches a (1, 1) because a's type bit 1 is in c's affinity, even though c's type bit 2 is not in a's affinity. A geom with both masks 0 (d) never collides with anything, except through an explicit pair.

    Example 15.2 defines scatter(n, seed), the shared model M9 of the course: a plane plus n free spheres and capsules on a 10×10 lattice per layer. Every body has its own free joint, nothing is excluded, so all 210 pairs survive the static filter and both columns of nxn_pairid are -1. Note rbound = 0 for the floor. Because the bodies start apart, the broadphase finds no candidates at t = 0; the packed variant (spacing 0.22 m) gives 6 candidates and 1 contact. Later examples use the packed variant for one-shot collision calls.

    Pitfall: editing m.geom_contype after put_model changes nothing (for rigid geoms)

    mjw.Model does carry geom_contype and geom_conaffinity, but no rigid broadphase or narrowphase kernel reads them: the bitmask test was folded into nxn_pairid once. (Only collision_flex.py reads them at run time.) To change which rigid pairs collide, edit the mujoco.MjModel (or the MJCF) and call mjw.put_model again.

    Weld bodies, dof-less pairs, parents and excludes

    The other four masks work on bodies, and three of them on weld bodies. MuJoCo's body_weldid[b] is the outermost ancestor of b reachable without crossing a joint, so bodies with no joint between them share a weld id and move as one rigid object. In the arm scene below:

    • same weld body: floor and wall are both world geoms (weld 0); link3 and gripper are two geoms on one body.
    • both dof-less: target is a mocap body. Mocap bodies have no joints, so target against the world geoms is a pair where neither side can ever be pushed.
    • parent-child: consecutive links (link1-link2, link2-link3, link2-gripper) touch at their joints by construction, so MuJoCo filters them by default. The rule requires both weld ids to be non-zero, so a link that hangs from the world still collides with the floor. Set mjDSBL_FILTERPARENT to keep these pairs.
    • exclude: <exclude body1="link1" body2="link3"/> becomes the signature (1 << 16) + 3 = 65539, which removes both geom pairs between those bodies (link1-link3 and link1-gripper).

    The example's reasons() function re-derives every -2 with the same masks in the same order and asserts it agrees with m.nxn_pairid; that assertion is what makes the reason labels trustworthy. With parent filtering off, the three parent-child pairs come back (19 → 22 kept).

    The NXN matrix

    Put all of this on one picture: the strict upper triangle of an ngeom × ngeom matrix, one cell per pair. Every coloured cell that is not green is a pair no kernel will ever test. The green cells are the NXN work list, and each one is one logical thread per world. The data is the JSON written by Example 15.4; the toggles recompute the masks in your browser with the same rules.

    Packing a pair into one index: upper_tri_index

    m.nxn_pairid has one row per unordered pair, in triu_indices order. Kernels that start from two geom ids (SAP does, §6) need the row number without a search. Row i of the triangle holds the pairs (i, i+1), \dots, (i, n-1), so it has n-1-i entries, and the rows before it hold \sum_{r<i}(n-1-r) = i(2n-i-1)/2 entries. Pair (i, j) sits j-i-1 entries into its row:

    \text{upper\_tri\_index}(n, i, j) = \frac{i(2n-i-1)}{2} + j - i - 1 = \frac{i(2n-i-3)}{2} + j - 1 .

    That last form is exactly the code in _src/math.py::upper_tri_index (a wp.func, (i * (2 * n - i - 3)) // 2 + j - 1); put_model has a NumPy twin that first swaps i and j so the order does not matter. i(2n-i-3) is always even, so the integer division is exact.

    The work list is not contiguous in packed space: rows 0, 6, 12-15, 18, 19 and 22 are missing. NXN does not care (it runs over the filtered list directly). SAP does: it computes a packed index from two geom ids, reads m.nxn_pairid[idx] (the unfiltered array), and skips the pair if column 0 is -2 and column 1 is negative.

    Explicit pairs and collision sensors

    An explicit <pair geom1 geom2 .../> overrides every static rule (even contype 0) and makes the filters use the pair's margin + gap; Example 15.11 in the lab section shows the row [0, -1] it creates. A collision sensor's pair is included even when column 0 is -2, and it skips the bounding-volume filter (... or pairid[1] >= 0 in both broadphases), because a distance sensor wants a reading even when the geoms are far apart. In NXN that override sees every listed pair. In SAP it fires only for pairs the sweep visits: a pair is visited only when, in sorted order, j is at most the first geom whose lower bound is greater than i's upper bound (one extra neighbour, capped at n - 1). A far-apart sensor pair outside that reach is never tested (pitfall in §6).

    4The bounding-volume filters

    The static list tells the broadphase which pairs may collide. The filter decides, every step and for every world, whether a pair from that list is close enough to be worth a narrowphase call. It is a wp.func built by collision_driver.py::_broadphase_filter(opt_broadphase_filter, ...) and shared by NXN and SAP. Its first argument is the bitmask m.opt.broadphase_filter of mjw.BroadphaseFilter:

    BitValueFunctionTestNeeds rotation?
    PLANE1_plane_filtersigned distance of the other geom's centre along the plane normal ≤ rbound + marginsplane's z-axis only
    SPHERE2_sphere_filter|x2 - x1|² ≤ (r1 + r2 + m1 + m2)²no
    AABB4_aabb_filterworld AABB of each local box (8 rotated corners), per-axis overlap (Ericson §4.2)yes (both)
    OBB8_obb_filter6 face-normal separating axes (Gottschalk et al., mj_collideOBB)yes (both)

    Here is the control flow of the generated function in the 4.0.1 wheel (abridged; the batched-field indexing worldid % shape[0] is folded into plain reads):

    if pairid >= 0:   # explicit <pair>: its margin + gap (the filters use the sum of the margins)
  effective_margin1 = pair_margin[pairid] + pair_gap[pairid]
else:             # default pair: each geom's margin + gap
  effective_margin1 = geom_margin[geom1] + geom_gap[geom1]
  effective_margin2 = geom_margin[geom2] + geom_gap[geom2]
xpos1, xpos2 = geom_xpos_in[worldid, geom1], geom_xpos_in[worldid, geom2]
xmat1, xmat2 = geom_xmat_in[worldid, geom1], geom_xmat_in[worldid, geom2]

if rbound1 == 0.0 or rbound2 == 0.0:          # one geom is a plane
  if wp.static(opt_broadphase_filter & BroadphaseFilter.PLANE):
    return _plane_filter(rbound1, rbound2, effective_margin1, effective_margin2, xpos1, xpos2, xmat1, xmat2)
else:
  if wp.static(opt_broadphase_filter & BroadphaseFilter.SPHERE):
    if not _sphere_filter(...): return False
  if wp.static(opt_broadphase_filter & BroadphaseFilter.AABB):
    if not _aabb_filter(...): return False
  if wp.static(opt_broadphase_filter & BroadphaseFilter.OBB):
    if not _obb_filter(...): return False
return True

    Four details matter:

    • Planes bypass the volume tests. A pair with a plane gets the plane band test or nothing. Without the PLANE bit every plane pair passes. Without SPHERE, AABB and OBB, every non-plane pair passes.
    • Each test is an early exit. The tests run cheapest first; the first one that says "apart" ends the function. Adding AABB to the default can only remove candidates, at the price of 16 rotated corners per pair test (8 per box, recomputed for every pair; the source has a TODO to precompute them).
    • The bits are compile-time. wp.static(opt_broadphase_filter & ...) deletes the disabled tests from the kernel. Changing m.opt.broadphase_filter therefore builds a new kernel (cached per value by @cache_kernel) the next time collision runs.
    • Margins inflate everything. The effective margin is margin + gap, per geom or per explicit pair. A contact can be detected up to that distance (Ch 9, Ch 16), so the filter must not reject it earlier.

    Four panels, one per filter, on the same pair: a fixed box A and a long, thin box B that you can rotate and slide. The plane panel shows B's bounding circle above a floor. Watch what happens near 45 degrees: the AABB of B grows fat and passes, the OBB test keeps rejecting, and the sphere test never changed at all.

    Example 15.6 runs all useful combinations on the packed 100-body scatter scene (5,050 filtered pairs per world) and prints how many candidates reach the narrowphase, and how many contacts come out.

    Read the table top to bottom. PLANE alone rejects all 100 plane pairs (the bodies float 0.3 m up) but lets every body-body pair through: 4,950 per world. SPHERE alone does the opposite: 181 = 100 unfiltered plane pairs + 81 overlapping bounding spheres. Together they leave 81. On its own the AABB test is looser than the bounding sphere here (PLANE|AABB leaves 96 vs 81), the OBB test is tighter (PLANE|OBB leaves 66), the default PLANE|SPHERE|OBB reaches 51, and adding AABB removes one more pair per world. The contact count never moves, because every filter is conservative. The default is a balance: the sphere test is almost free and rejects most far pairs, the OBB test cleans up the near misses of elongated geoms, and AABB rarely removes anything OBB has not already removed.

    Tip: what a looser filter costs

    Fewer filter bits mean a cheaper broadphase but more candidates. Every extra candidate costs a narrowphase thread, a slot in the candidate buffer (BROADPHASE overflow risk) and, for convex pairs, CCD scratch (Ch 17). With PLANE alone the example above needs a candidate buffer of 4,950 per world; the default needs 51.

    5NXN: every listed pair, every world

    The NXN broadphase is the brute-force one, and on a GPU brute force is often the right answer. collision_driver.py::nxn_broadphase launches the kernel built by _nxn_broadphase(...) over a 2-D index space:

    wp.launch(
  kernel,
  dim=(d.nworld, m.nxn_geom_pair_filtered.shape[0]),
  block_dim=m.block_dim.nxn_broadphase,                       # 128 by default
  max_blocks=8 * block_size * min_grid_size // m.block_dim.nxn_broadphase,
  inputs=[..., m.nxn_geom_pair_filtered, m.nxn_pairid_filtered, d.geom_xpos, d.geom_xmat, ...],
  outputs=[d.ncollision, ctx.collision_pair, ctx.collision_pairid, ctx.collision_worldid],
)

    Each logical thread worldid, elementid = wp.tid() owns one pair of the static list in one world:

    1. load geom1, geom2 = nxn_geom_pair[elementid] and their types;
    2. skip if a per-world mesh is missing (§8) or if sleeping says so (§8);
    3. run the filter of §4 with pairid = nxn_pairid[elementid];
    4. if it passes, or if pairid[1] >= 0 (a sensor pair), call _add_geom_pair: cid = wp.atomic_add(ncollision_out, 0, 1), return if cid >= naconmax_in, otherwise write the pair (swapped so that type1 <= type2, the order the narrowphase dispatch table expects, Ch 16), its pairid and its world into slot cid.

    The cost is exactly nworld × nnxn_geom_pair_filtered filter evaluations per call, whether the scene is sparse or crowded. That sounds wasteful, but each evaluation is a handful of loads and a dot product, the threads never wait for each other, and neighbouring threads read neighbouring rows of the pair list. For the scenes most people simulate (a robot, a floor, a few objects: tens to a few hundred geoms) the static list is a few thousand pairs and NXN is the fastest option.

    Note: logical threads vs launched threads

    The kernel is declared with grid_stride=True and launched with a max_blocks cap computed from wp.get_suggested_block_size. When nworld × nnxn_geom_pair_filtered is larger than the cap, each hardware thread loops over several (world, element) cells. You still write and reason about the kernel as "one thread per cell"; Ch 17 shows the same grid-stride pattern for CCD.

    Recall from Ch 4

    Integer atomics hand out unique values, but in an unspecified order; float atomics additionally change rounding (Ch 4 §9). Here only integers are involved, so as long as ncollision ≤ naconmax the set of candidates is deterministic and only their order in the buffer varies. Once the buffer overflows, which candidates are dropped depends on the atomic order (Figure 6): _add_geom_pair returns when cid >= naconmax.

    6Sweep and prune

    NXN's cost grows with the number of listed pairs, which grows with n^2. For a few thousand geoms that is millions of pairs per world, almost all of them far apart. Sweep and prune (SAP) avoids looking at far pairs at all: it turns each geom's bounding sphere into an interval on one line, sorts the intervals, and for each interval only looks at the ones that start before it ends. In 1-D, sorting puts overlapping intervals next to each other, so the work is close to n \log n plus the number of overlaps.

    MJWarp's collision_driver.py::sap_broadphase does this for all worlds at once in six launches (pair margins, project, sort, range, scan, sweep). Here they are, then a stepper that runs them on real data.

    Step 1: project onto a fixed oblique axis (_sap_project)

    The axis is a constant in the code:

    # TODO(team): direction

# random fixed direction
direction = wp.vec3(0.5935, 0.7790, 0.1235)
direction = wp.normalize(direction)      # = (0.6013, 0.7892, 0.1251)

    Why oblique? Real scenes are full of structure aligned with the world axes: rows of objects along x, stacks along z, everything resting at the same height. Projected onto a world axis, a whole row or a whole floor layer lands on top of itself and every interval overlaps every other. A "random" direction makes such coincidences unlikely. A thread per (world, geom) computes the centre c = dot(direction, xpos) and the radius

    r = \underbrace{\text{rbound}}_{\text{plane: } \texttt{MJ\_MAXVAL}} + \max(\text{margin} + \text{gap},\; \text{geom\_pair\_margin})

    where geom_pair_margin, computed first by _sap_pair_margin with wp.atomic_max, is the largest margin + gap of any explicit pair the geom is in (an explicit pair can have a bigger margin than either geom). A plane gets rbound = MJ_MAXVAL = 1e10, an interval that covers the whole line. It writes lower = c - r, upper = c + r and sort_index = geomid.

    Step 2: sort each world's lower bounds

    Each world sorts its own ngeom lower bounds, carrying sort_index along as the value. The worlds must never mix, and there are two ways to guarantee that:

    • SAP_TILE: wp.launch_tiled(_segmented_sort(m.ngeom), dim=d.nworld, block_dim=m.block_dim.segmented_sort). One block of threads per world loads that world's keys and values into a shared-memory tile (wp.tile_load(..., shape=tile_size, storage="shared")), sorts them in place with wp.tile_sort(keys, values) and stores them back. The tile size is ngeom, fixed at kernel build time.
    • SAP_SEGMENTED: wp.utils.segmented_sort_pairs(keys, values, nworld * ngeom, segmented_index) over the flat array, where _sap_project filled segmented_index[w] = w * ngeom (and the end marker nworld * ngeom), so each world is its own segment.

    Recall from Ch 4

    A tiled launch gives each logical index a block of threads that share memory; Ch 26 §8 owns tiles, and Ch 4 §11 previews them.

    Step 3: how far does each interval reach? (collision_core.py::sap_range)

    For the element at sorted position i, every later element whose lower bound is at most my upper bound might overlap me. Because the lower bounds are sorted, those elements form a contiguous run starting at i + 1, and a binary search finds where it ends:

    @wp.func
def sap_binary_search(values: wp.array[Any], value: Any, lower: int, upper: int) -> int:
  """Binary search for the first element > value in sorted array."""
  while lower < upper:
    mid = (lower + upper) >> 1
    if values[mid] > value:
      upper = mid
    else:
      lower = mid + 1
  return upper

# in sap_range, one thread per (world, sorted position):
limit = sap_binary_search(lower_in[worldid], upper, sortedid + 1, n)
limit = wp.min(n - 1, limit)
range_out[worldid, sortedid] = limit - sortedid

    Look closely at the bookkeeping: limit is the first element that does not overlap, and the sweep later visits j = i + 1, ..., i + range, so it visits that first non-overlapping element too (unless the clamp to n - 1 kicked in). With the default filter that extra candidate is harmless: its bounding spheres are apart on this axis, so they are apart in 3-D too and the SPHERE test (on by default) rejects it. With a looser filter, such as PLANE alone, it would pass to the narrowphase.

    Step 4: prefix sum for load balancing (wp.utils.array_scan)

    Now range[w, i] says how many pairs element i of world w must test. Ranges are wildly uneven: the floor's range is ngeom - 1 (it overlaps everything), a lonely sphere's is 1. Giving each element its own thread would leave most threads idle while the floor's thread loops. Instead, wp.utils.array_scan(range_.reshape(-1), cumulative_sum.reshape(-1), True) computes the inclusive prefix sum over all worlds flattened. Its last entry is the total number of work packages (one work package = one pair to test), and any work id can be mapped back to its owner by a binary search.

    Step 5: the sweep (_sap_broadphase)

    The sweep kernel is launched with dim = nsweep = 5 * nworld * ngeom (the comment: "assumes each geom has 5 other geoms"). Thread t takes work ids t, t + nsweep, t + 2 nsweep, ... while they are below the total. For each one:

    i = sap_binary_search(cumulative_sum_in, worldgeomid, 0, nworldgeom)   # owner element

... missing-mesh and sleep skips, then the same filter and _add_geom_pair as NXN

    Two consequences are worth stating. First, SAP looks pairs up in the full m.nxn_pairid, so the static rules of §3 apply exactly as in NXN. Second, the 1-D overlap is a filter in its own right: SAP never visits a pair whose intervals are apart. SAP and NXN therefore find the same candidates (except for collision-sensor pairs, see the pitfall below) whenever m.opt.broadphase_filter contains SPHERE (the default does) or another test at least as tight as the 1-D interval test, because then every pair SAP skips would also have been rejected by NXN's filter. With PLANE alone, NXN passes every non-plane pair and the counts differ (Example 15.7 shows 435 vs 137 candidates on 30 packed bodies, with the same number of contacts). Examples 15.7 and 15.8 check that ncollision agrees for all three broadphases with the default filter.

    Pitfall: SAP can miss a far-apart distance-sensor pair

    The sensor override ... or pairid[1] >= 0 in _sap_broadphase only runs on pairs the sweep visits, and the sweep reaches, from sorted position i, only up to the first geom whose lower bound is greater than i's upper bound (one extra neighbour, capped at n - 1). Take a plane and three free spheres at x = -2, 0 and 2 with <distance geom1="ga" geom2="gb" cutoff="10"/> between the outer two. C MuJoCo reports 3.8 and NXN gives ncollision = 1, sensor 3.8. SAP_TILE and SAP_SEGMENTED give ncollision = 0 and the sensor reads 10, the cutoff. With only two spheres SAP happens to get it right, because the far sphere is the extra neighbour. If a model relies on long-range distance sensors, keep m.opt.broadphase = NXN (mujoco-warp 4.0.1, CPU check).

    Going deeper: why the arrays are twice as long

    sap_broadphase allocates projection_lower and sort_index with shape (nworld, ngeom, 2) and passes them everywhere as .reshape((-1, ngeom)), a (2 * nworld, ngeom) view. Rows 0 .. nworld-1 of that view hold the data; the second half is scratch space, because Warp's radix-sort utilities (segmented_sort_pairs) need room for a second copy of keys and values. projection_upper, range_ and cumulative_sum are plain (nworld, ngeom).

    The count is per world (the static list does not depend on nworld). 250,000 listed pairs is about 708 geoms with nothing filtered. Try the rule:

    The rule is a starting point, not a law. m.opt.broadphase and m.opt.broadphase_filter are plain Python attributes on the Model: collision() reads m.opt.broadphase on every call to decide which function to run, and both values are arguments of @cache_kernel builders, so a new value builds (and caches) new kernels the first time it is used.     8Skip rules: sleeping bodies, missing meshes, NaN positions

    Both broadphases run the same three per-pair skips before the filter.

    Sleeping

    With EnableBit.SLEEP on (Ch 21 owns sleeping), forward.py::fwd_position calls collision() twice. Pass 1 is a normal pass that skips pairs that cannot change; then sleep.wake_collision wakes sleeping trees touched by awake ones, the awake state before update_sleep is saved as awake_prev, and pass 2 runs collision(m, d, awake_prev=awake_prev). Pass 2 keeps d.nacon (it appends) and only emits pairs that pass 1 skipped and that now involve a woken body.

    For pass 2, NXN goes one step further: when m.opt.graph_conditional is on, a tiny kernel _any_awake_changed sets a flag if any body's state differs from awake_prev, and the whole broadphase launch is wrapped in wp.capture_if(cond, on_true=_launch), so on steps where nothing woke it does no work at all. SAP cannot do this: as its docstring says, its sort and scan utilities allocate scratch internally, which is not allowed inside a conditional body. It still applies the incremental per-pair filter.

    Per-world missing meshes

    With per-world mesh variants (Ch 10, §12), m.geom_dataid is batched and a mesh geom can have data id -1 in some worlds: it does not exist there. _mesh_missing is compiled in only when geom_dataid.shape[0] > 1: NXN returns early for such a pair, SAP continues, and _sap_project gives the missing geom the inverted interval [MJ_MAXVAL, -MJ_MAXVAL]. Its lower bound sorts it to the end of the axis, and because no real geom's upper bound reaches MJ_MAXVAL, no bounded geom ever sweeps over it ("an inverted interval at the end of the axis").

    NaN positions

    If a world blows up and a geom's centre is NaN, _sap_project writes [MJ_MAXVAL, MJ_MAXVAL], which also parks it at the end. NXN has no such branch: the filter's comparisons with NaN are false, so the sphere test rejects the pair. Either way a NaN geom silently gets no contacts, and there is no NaN check in MJWarp (Ch 8 shows the non-finite reset kernel).

    10Lab: your own filter kernel, and explicit pairs

    A broadphase-like kernel

    You now know enough to write the core of NXN yourself. Example 15.9 launches one thread per (world, filtered pair) over m.nxn_geom_pair_filtered, reads d.geom_xpos, d.geom_xmat, m.geom_rbound, m.geom_margin and m.geom_gap (with the worldid % shape[0] batching rule), applies the plane band and the sphere test, and counts the passes with wp.atomic_add, overall and per world. The counts must equal d.ncollision under BroadphaseFilter.SPHERE and PLANE | SPHERE. Each world is lowered by a different amount, so the plane test gives different per-world counts.

    Two simplifications keep the kernel short and are safe for this scene: it has no explicit <pair>, so the pair margin branch is not needed, and it has no collision sensors, so the nxn_pairid[pairid][1] >= 0 override never fires (it is there to show where it goes). With sphere-only filtering every world has the same 127 candidates: the 60 plane pairs (no plane test) plus 67 sphere overlaps, which do not depend on height because all bodies move together. Turning on the plane band removes plane pairs as the bodies rise: 67, 67, 90, 127 from the highest world to the lowest.

    Explicit pairs

    Example 15.11 gives the ball contype = conaffinity = 0, which alone would mark ball-floor -2, and then declares <pair geom1="ball" geom2="floor" condim="4" friction="..." margin="0.01"/>. The pair's row of nxn_pairid becomes [0, -1] (pair number 0), the filter uses the pair's margin, and the narrowphase uses the pair's condim, friction and margin. The ball sits 5 mm above the floor and still gets a contact (inside the 10 mm pair margin); the box gets the floor's default material.

    Pitfall: a <pair> overrides exclusions

    put_model writes the pair id into column 0 after all five masks, so an explicit pair also re-enables pairs on the same body, parent-child pairs and <exclude>d bodies. That is usually what you want (you asked for it by name), but it means an explicit pair list is not a "subset" of the default one.

    11Check yourself

    Quiz

    What does -2 in column 0 of m.nxn_pairid mean?

    -2 is set in put_model when contype/conaffinity, same weld body, both dof-less, parent-child or exclude rejects the pair. Only a collision sensor (column 1 ≥ 0) brings it back into the work list.

    Geom A has contype=2, conaffinity=1; geom B has contype=1, conaffinity=1. Do they pass the bitmask test?

    (2 & 1) | (1 & 1) = 0 | 1 = 1, non-zero, so the pair is compatible. This is why "contype 2, conaffinity 1" links still touch a default floor.

    You turn off OBB in m.opt.broadphase_filter. Which statement is true?

    Every filter is conservative, so contacts are unchanged (Example 15.6). Fewer tests means more candidates, and the new bitmask is a new @cache_kernel key.

    A model has 1,200 geoms and 400,000 pairs survive the static filter. Which broadphase does put_model pick?

    400,000 ≥ 250,000 rules out NXN; 1,200 ≥ 1,000 rules out SAP_TILE.

    Why does SAP project onto normalize(0.5935, 0.7790, 0.1235) instead of the x-axis?

    A row of boxes along x, or a layer of objects at one height, would collapse onto the same interval on a world axis. The direction is a fixed constant (# TODO(team): direction).

    What is the prefix sum (array_scan) over range for in SAP?

    The floor's range is ngeom-1 and a lonely sphere's is 1. Numbering every work package and striding through them spreads that uneven work over all threads.

    _obb_filter tests 6 axes. An exact 3-D box-box separating-axis test needs 15. What follows?

    An axis can only prove separation. Skipping the 9 edge-edge axes means some separations go unproven: conservative, cheaper, and the narrowphase cleans up.

    With sleeping enabled, which pair does the broadphase skip?

    The kernels return early for ASLEEP + ASLEEP and ASLEEP + STATIC. Nothing in such a pair can move, so its contact would not change.

        r1 = np.sum(np.abs(h1 * (R1.T @ n)))
        r2 = np.sum(np.abs(h2 * (R2.T @ n)))
        if r1 + r2 + margin < abs((c2 - c1) @ n):
            return False
    return True

    def pair_of(n, k):
    """Inverse of upper_tri_index: packed row k -> (i, j) with i < j."""
    i = 0
    while upper_tri_index(n, i, n - 1) < k:     # last entry of row i
        i += 1
    return i, k - upper_tri_index(n, i, i + 1) + i + 1

    13Summary

    Key takeaways

    • Collision is a funnel: all pairs → static work list (put_model) → candidates (broadphase, d.ncollision) → contacts (narrowphase, d.nacon). Candidates and contacts both live in buffers of size naconmax.
    • Bounding volumes trade tightness for cost: sphere (rotation-free), AABB (world axes, rebuilt on rotation), OBB (local box, needs separating axes). All are conservative.
    • m.nxn_pairid column 0 is -2 (never), -1 (default) or a <pair> id; column 1 is a collision-sensor id. The masks are contype/conaffinity, same weld body, both dof-less, parent-child (unless FILTERPARENT is disabled) and exclude, applied once.
    • upper_tri_index(n, i, j) = i(2n-i-3)/2 + j - 1 maps a pair to its row; SAP uses it to look pairs up in the full nxn_pairid.
    • The filter bits (PLANE=1, SPHERE=2, AABB=4, OBB=8, default 11) are baked in with wp.static. Planes (rbound == 0) only see the plane test; margins are margin + gap, from the geoms or the explicit pair.
    • NXN: logical dim (nworld, nnxn_geom_pair_filtered), one filter call each, atomic compaction. SAP: project on a fixed oblique axis, sort per world (tile or segmented), sap_range by binary search, array_scan, then nsweep = 5 * nworld * ngeom threads stride through the work packages.
    • Auto rule: fewer than 250,000 listed pairs → NXN; else ngeom < 1000 → SAP_TILE; else SAP_SEGMENTED. With the default filter all three give the same candidates, except collision-sensor pairs that SAP's sweep does not reach; switch freely and benchmark on your GPU.
    • Sleeping pairs (asleep + asleep, asleep + static), missing per-world meshes and NaN centres are skipped; SAP allocates scratch per call and cannot sit in a CUDA-graph conditional.
